Tommy Robinson: Where’s the Money Gone? review – endless shocking allegations

TheTools · 09.09.2026 22:20 · source · permalink

From his claim of being ‘de-banked’ to a disturbing detail about a rape survivor’s story being used to fundraise, this film puts big questions to the co-founder of the English Defence League. But it is frustratingly hard to get answers

You can’t say it would be a huge surprise if a leading figure of the British far right turned out to be a fraudster: that whole strand of politics is, after all, a con job preying on angry citizens who are daft enough to believe that immigrants, and not the rapaciously greedy super-rich, are somehow the cause of the nation’s problems. If people will fall for that, fleecing them out of their money is not a big leap.

Tommy Robinson, co-founder of the English Defence League and now an influencer, independent documentary-maker and international flag-shaggers’ guru, was convicted of mortgage fraud in 2014, but what about 2026? Is he a scam artist juicing idiots for cash? Robinson, real name Stephen Christopher Yaxley-Lennon, is undeniably a talented harvester of donations from his fans, whether it’s in person at his rallies or via the internet: in a video posted online, he admits to “continually ask[ing] you, the people, to support us”, and in another he boasts of having once convinced his admirers to hand over £350,000 in the space of two weeks. If you try to avoid seeing or hearing about Robinson, the amount of money he generates may shock you.

As a piece of theatre, Matt Shea’s Dispatches film is suitably foreboding, with his findings presented inside a half-lit industrial space furnished with those transparent white boards TV detectives use because they look better on camera. Shea sticks up photos and draws arrows forcefully in marker pen. The dramatic highlight is a phone call with, and then a voice note from, Robinson himself, accusing Shea of “chatting shit”.

In his slightly more cogent right-of-reply statements sent to the programme-makers, Robinson’s contention is that, although he is the public face of the fundraising, the money doesn’t go to him: he works for a Portuguese company that pays him “contract fees”, and which uses donations to pay for “overheads, events, campaigns, documentaries and related work”.

Shea first looks into a claim Robinson has made that he has been “de-banked” in the UK – in other words, that no British bank will let him have an account. This was the justification Robinson gave after an incident in 2024 when, following a rally in Trafalgar Square the day before, where buckets had been passed around the crowd collecting cash, he was stopped by police on his way out of the country and found to have £13,000 in the car. Shea has details of an account in the name of Stephen Lennon with Metrobank in the UK; he tests it by sending it £1; it works. Robinson’s response is that this is merely a “personal basic account … I do not have a normal current account”, and that supporter funds don’t go into it.

Next, Shea turns to appeals made on behalf of needy individuals, be they the families of deceased Robinson supporters or survivors of grooming gangs. Here, the reporting gets bogged down: it seems to be true that there are multiple instances of the advertised recipients ultimately not getting the money, but Robinson’s contention that, in each example, the person either broke contact or actively rejected the donation is one Shea can’t quite defeat. Shea’s interview with a rape survivor who was involved in one of Robinson’s grooming-gang documentaries, and who makes disturbing allegations about how her story was used, is compelling – but it’s inconclusive with regard to possible fraud.

Shea makes more progress when he looks into a practice that always makes fraud-hunters’ antennae twitch: the use of multiple limited companies. Shea identifies five that have at some stage been behind fundraising drives fronted by Robinson, finding that four never filed accounts and three went into liquidation owing money to HMRC. Robinson points out that he was not a director of any of them.

In 2018, Robinson lost a libel case having failed to back up allegations he made about young Syrian refugee Jamal Hijazi. The damages Hijazi was awarded were never paid, with Robinson claiming to have no money or assets – but in order to do that, he made statements under oath about his financial arrangements that Shea thinks raise further questions. Shea cites evidence suggesting that those claims, which Robinson stands by, might not be accurate and posits this as the mistake that could ultimately bring him down.

After a final allegation that a cryptocurrency wallet used to accept donations was also used to transfer funds to a gambling platform – Robinson, who during the Hijazi case told the court he had lost £100,000 gambling, issues another flat denial that supporter funds have been misappropriated – Shea departs. Has he landed an unanswerable haymaker? Not quite. Would any evidence of impropriety cause the flow of money from the faithful to dry up? Probably not. But this programme asks a lot of the right awkward questions.
