Nick Sirianni continues to waste the talents of tight end Dallas Goedert | Marcus Hayes

TheTools · 26.11.2026 23:35 · source · permalink

He's averaging fewer targets this season than his career average despite the absence of weapons in a last-place offense. Big D says he's been happy just winning, but this is coaching malpractice.

    At the height of his abilities during the 2011 season, Eagles tight end Brent Celek told me that, if he was used differently, he could produce offensive numbers to rival Jason Witten’s.

    Instead, Celek wasted his prime helping to protect fading Donovan McNabb, Michael Vick 2.0., uber-backup Nick Foles, butt-fumbling knife magnet Mark Sanchez, and Sammy “Sleeves” Bradford. Celek ran tight routes, had sticky hands, and loved to crush dudes after a catch, but he spent far too much time on the hip of such unremarkable right tackles as Winston Justice, King Dunlap, and Dennis Kelly, bolstering offensive lines in which Danny Watkins and Dallas Reynolds played major roles.

    But … Witten?

    Seriously?

    After all, Witten is an 11-time Pro Bowl participant who ranks second in catches, third in receiving yards, and sixth in receiving touchdowns among tight ends. These numbers one day will land him in the Pro Football Hall of Fame.

    The thing is, Celek was pretty close to being right.

    In his best season, 2009, Celek caught eight touchdown passes, one behind Witten’s career high, with 76 catches, a mark that Witten didn’t reach in five of his 11 Pro Bowl seasons. While Celek might be somewhat hyperbolic in his self estimation, his point was, if he’d been given more chances, maybe he would made a Pro Bowl or two himself.

    Why does this matter now?

    Because, this past Sunday, the next generation of Brent Celek eclipsed the original Brent Celek.

    Celek moved from No. 11 in career receiving yards among Eagles to No. 12. Dallas Goedert passed him with a 38-yard reception on second-and-18 that set up an early field goal. The Eagles would get no longer play that day, and they won by three points.

    Goedert needed 13 more yards to move past A.J. Brown and into the top 10, but he would get no more chances. On a team with one viable starting wide receiver in DeVonta Smith — a team with 6-foot-5, ex-practice squad quarterback Joe Milton starting in his second NFL game — that 38-yarder was the only pass thrown Goedert’s way.

    It was a criminal misuse of an asset. You can understand such an oversight by first-year offensive coordinator Sean Mannion, but sixth-year head coach Nick Sirianni has consistently underutilized Goedert, this season, more egregiously than ever.

    “When he doesn’t get a target, that doesn’t necessarily mean that he wasn’t the first read in a target,” Sirianni explained Wednesday.

    The Eagles’ offense is averaging the eighth-fewest plays per drive this season, as Goedert averages 5.4 targets per game, and it averaged the 10th-fewest in 2025, when he averaged 5.5. But they ranked seventh in 2024, second in 2023, and ninth in 2022, when Goedert averaged 5.7, which, over a 17-game season, means just five more targets per season.

    Granted, Goedert might not be Travis Kelce (7.7) or George Kittle (6.1), but, for a team as talent-challenged as these Eagles, he should get more than one target in any game.

    Maybe Goedert will get the chance to pass Brown on Thanksgiving in Dallas.

    That’s another reason why Goedert resonates in this moment, and not just because of nomenclatural symmetry.

    Witten, Celek’s personal fantasy comp, played the first 16 of his 17 seasons in Dallas.

    Goedert already has had far more success than Celek.

    Goedert ranks No. 4 among Eagles in career receptions with 441. He’s eighth in TD catches with 37. He gets even better when the lights get brighter. He is the Eagles’ all-time leader in playoff receptions with 56. He ranks second in playoff receiving yards with 595, and playoff touchdown catches with five.

    For reference, in the postseason Witten had 45 catches, 486 yards, and one TD.

    No, Goedert isn’t Witten, either.

    But he certainly is the Rodney Dangerfield of the team’s greatest run. He gets nowhere near the respect he deserves.

    “You know,” he told me Tuesday, “there’s been times where I feel like more targets could come my way.”

    Pause.

    “That said, I’ve always said that I love to do whatever I can to help this team win,” Goedert said. “Throughout my time here, we’ve had a ton of success. You know, I wouldn’t trade another 100 targets for the Super Bowls and the playoff experiences.”

    Another 100 targets over nine seasons surely would push him past Hall of Fame tight end Pete Retzlaff, who last played in 1966. Retzlaff was a North Dakota native who, like Goedert, played college ball at South Dakota State. And, like Goedert, Retzlaff possessed humility and selflessness typical of someone raised in the Upper Midwest.

    Goedert tucked his unkempt brown hair under a trucker’s cap, shrugged, and smiled: “I just try to do whatever I can to put wins on tape and prove to the coaches that, when my number gets called, good things happen.”

    He’s in his ninth season as an Eagle, a second-round pick in 2018 out of FCS (I-AA) South Dakota State who played nine-man football at tiny Britton (S.D.)-Hecla High. He is a blue-collar, feel-good story whom the Eagles seem to take for granted.

    Despite seven years of consistent production, a few marred by injury, he had to settle for one-year deals for both the 2025 and 2026 seasons. He’s no charity case: He’s making $7 million this season, and he’s made more than $65 million in his career, but, at 31, and with firstborn Dallas Jr. having arrived in January, it would have been nice to get a little security from the team that one day will induct you into its Hall of Fame.

    Practically speaking, Goedert didn’t get many plays called for him Sunday because he was mainly tasked with helping rookie right tackle Markel Bell smother Steelers edge rusher T.J. Watt, which they did superbly.

    But, practically speaking, Goedert has played his entire career with elite tackles Jason Peters, Jordan Mailata, and Lane Johnson. He has been a victim of teammates’ deficiencies and circumstance.

    The real issues:

    1. In Goedert’s first three seasons, former starter Carson Wentz force-fed Pro Bowl tight end and good buddy Zach Ertz.

    2. When Wentz lost his job to Jalen Hurts in 2020, Goedert was suddenly dealing with a short, 6-foot-1 quarterback who has never liked to throw over the middle or throw into tight windows and who is quick to abandon plays and run.

    3. The departure of Ertz in 2021 was preceded that year by the drafting of receiver DeVonta Smith, who’d won the Heisman Trophy. That was followed in 2022 by the arrival of Brown, the archetype of a selfish receiver; and in 2024 by the arrival of running back Saquon Barkley, who quickly established himself as the team’s best weapon.

    “We had me and Smitty, and then we signed A.J. Brown, so, you know, we had two great receivers on the outside. I can’t complain that they’re getting more targets than me, right?” he said. “Then we got Saquon. He rushed for 2,000 yards. You know, so it’s hard to say that I should get more opportunities when we’re having success.”

    No, it’s not.

    Especially not this year.

    Brown forced a trade. The line has issues. The run game is nonexistent. Smith and Barkley have been hurt. The team is 3-7, ranks last in offensive yards per game and second-to-last in scoring.

    Goedert has been healthy for eight of the 10 games. He should have been their go-to guy all along. Goedert is averaging 5.4 targets, which is fewer than the 5.6 targets he’d averaged in the previous seven seasons, when he had to share opportunities with Ertz, Smith, Brown, and Barkley.

    If anyone wonders why the Eagles’ offense stinks, look no further than the way it looks past its tight end.

    Goedert told me he wants to remain an Eagle, and he told me he wants to play at least four more seasons so maybe Dallas Jr. can have a memory of Dad on the field.

    The way things are trending, if Goedert wants Dallas Jr. to remember seeing Dad make catches, Dallas Sr. better seek employment elsewhere.

    “You know, you think sometimes, if you were somewhere else, maybe it’d be better,” Goedert said. “But, you know, maybe it’d be worse.”

    Maybe not.

    Nick Sirianni’s fate with the Eagles beyond 2026 is far from certain. But Sunday’s 19-16 win over Pittsburgh produced an important sign: the Eagles’ determined performance suggests the head coach still has a grip on his team — at least for now. There are seven games left in what figures to be a lost season. That’s plenty of time for things to go wrong. At least for one afternoon, however, the Eagles did a few things right.
