Charlie Hunnam is assessing different notorious criminals from American history.

The actor, who starred in both Monster seasons about Ed Gein and Lizzie Borden, tells Entertainment Weekly about the differences between the two true-crime figures. Hunnam plays Lizzie Borden's father, Andrew Borden, in Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story, which focuses on the infamous 1892 murders.

"I think they're very different characters," Hunnam begins. "Ed Gein was a deeply disturbed character that engaged in a prolonged period of really devious depravity. These really heinous things that he was doing."

Gein, known as the "Butcher of Plainfield," confessed to two murders in a small Wisconsin town in the 1950s (though he was suspected of more).

Among the other heinous acts he committed were robbing graves and skinning corpses, which is depicted in Monster: The Ed Gein Story — starring Hunnam as the titular criminal.

Charlie Hunnam as 'Ed Gein' in 'Monster: The Ed Gein Story'
Credit: Netflix

"Whereas Lizzie, although if she did indeed commit this crime, and it was never proved, she was exonerated in court— although all of the evidence does suggest that she probably was responsible," continues Hunnam.

"But if she was responsible, it was a much more understandable crime of passion. She was living in this incredibly oppressive household with a father who was completely inaccessible and ruled in this cold, iron-fisted, miserly way, and a stepmother who was miserable and resented her."

Hunnam concludes that there was "jealousy" and "resentment" in the Borden house that "culminated in this crime of passion where this seemingly very innocent, very gentle girl, flipped and brutally murdered both of her parents."

Per the BBC, Lizzie and her sister Emma lived with their father, Andrew Borden, and stepmother, Abby Borden, before the killings. There were rumors of tension in the home over financial issues. Andrew was said to have been frugal with his daughters, but gave money to his new wife's family.

The grisly killings of her stepmother and then father with an axe, as the nursery rhyme goes, happened one hot summer morning at the Borden house in August 1892 in Fall River, Mass.

Authorities initially doubted Lizzie, who alerted the police about the murders, as being capable of such a heinous crime and claimed she wasn't strong enough to wield a weapon. However, Lizzie's account of the day of the murders kept changing, and authorities grew suspicious. She and the Irish maid Bridget Sullivan were the only people home when the murder took place.

Lizzie was arrested that August and eventually charged with the killings. The murder trial took place the following year in June 1893. Lizzie used her inheritance to hire a top lawyer from Boston to defend her before a jury of 12 men (this happened before women were allowed to serve on a jury).

After a deliberation believed to have taken less than 2 hours, a jury acquitted Lizzie of the murders. She died in 1927, decades after she was found innocent.

No one was ever convicted of the murders; the murder weapon was never found, and the perpetrator remains a mystery to this day.
