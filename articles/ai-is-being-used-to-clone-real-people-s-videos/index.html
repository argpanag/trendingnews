AI is being used to clone real people's videos

TheTools · 22.11.2026 10:54 · source · permalink

Watch: Musicians being 'reskinned' by AI copycats

  • Published
    5 hours ago

Is this real or AI? It is a question increasingly asked underneath social media videos, and often difficult to tell.

But there is a new trend when it comes to digital manipulation. Across social media, AI-generated characters are appearing in videos that look remarkably similar to those posted by real people.

From musicians to dancers and comedians, content creators are finding their performances cloned by these characters that are designed to look funny, realistic, or hypersexualised.

Experts call the practice "reskinning" and say it is made possible by cheap, easy-to-use AI tools. People can discover their work has been copied by seeing a video of a character making the same joke or doing the same dance, even with the same background. Sometimes the AI version has millions more views than the original.

Some of the accounts identified by BBC Verify appear to be using these AI-generated videos to get views and likes, and to eventually make money. Others appear to be experimenting with AI to make people laugh or simply for the attention that comes with going viral. But the practice has angered content creators who say their original work is being stolen.

Guitarist Sophie Lloyd (left) and "Mila Vasari"

"A lot [of these accounts] are getting thousands of views, thousands of followers, just for literally doing nothing and stealing the stuff that we've created," says Sophie Lloyd, a UK-based guitarist who tours with US rapper Machine Gun Kelly, who has spent years building a successful online presence.

Lloyd says videos of her performances have been repeatedly reskinned by accounts that replaced her with over-sexualised AI-generated characters. One Facebook account published hundreds of short videos of a guitarist called "Mila Vasari" that had each gained thousands of views. BBC Verify found that at least three copied Lloyd's performances and used the exact same music, movements, and even facial expressions.

BBC Verify did not find any evidence that "Mila Vasari" is a real person. The videos on the Vasari account also contain visual inconsistencies that suggest AI was used to create them, including distorted fingers playing chords that don't match the music, and guitar strings that fail to move when strummed.

The account, which appears to be run from Canada, did not respond to questions about the purpose of these videos. Its profile links to a page devoted to Vasari on Fanvue, a subscription platform that allows creators to charge for content, including adult material. BBC Verify has found two other accounts fronted by AI-generated guitarists that have reskinned Lloyd's performances and use those avatars to sell and promote adult content.

But Lloyd is far from alone in having her content manipulated by AI.

"I've got so many messages from other artists and creators that have had the same thing happening to them," says Kjetil Krogstad, a Norwegian content creator known for videos showing him happily dancing in a suit while carrying a cup of tea.

In March, his followers alerted him to the fact that one of his videos had been reskinned using an AI-generated bunny. The post went on to attract millions of views - outperforming Krogstad's original video - before being deleted. When Krogstad looked into the account behind the bunny video, he found it promoting courses on how to make money from AI-generated content.

Dr Tina Kendall, an expert in online culture at Anglia Ruskin University, says that while these videos are sometimes made for fun, often the people behind the accounts copy videos that have already proven popular in the hope of attracting audiences and making money.

"What these tools allow people to do is just automatically take a viral concept that's already existing and to reuse it through these reskinning tools," she says.

Kendall sees this as part of a wider online "hustle culture", in which accounts seek to turn users' attention and engagement into money - be it through subscriptions to fan pages featuring adult content, risky cryptocurrency products like meme coins, or monetisation features on social media platforms.

And making these copies "conventionally attractive, white, feminine" characters can help, Kendall says. "That's the kind of stuff that always floats to the top and is successful, it really conforms with these conventional cultural ideals of attractiveness, success, aspiration, beauty."

Dozens of people have taken to social media in recent weeks to call out the AI-generated accounts they found using their content without permission.

"I cannot explain how angry I am right now," Grace Roberts, a British car creator, said on Instagram. "Someone not only stole my video, but also changed me into basically the societal norm of what people want to watch, and this is literally my job," she said, showing the original video of herself wearing jeans, smiling and covering her ears at a drag car race next to an AI-generated version with a blonde woman in a short dress.

"I put the videos side by side and I could see it was my exact facial movements, how my body moved, the exact same background and even the people around me were completely copied," she told BBC Verify. "It was just me that had been altered."

"I pay for the cars, I build the cars, I pay for the cameras, the filming equipment, and then someone can just hit download on their computer, change it, and then make money off the back of my video," she added.

Her concerns are echoed by other content creators who fear these AI-generated accounts could cost them valuable exposure, followers, and potential income.

American comedian Julia Jasunas, who called out having her jokes stolen online by AI, said in a recent Instagram video: "The thing that is most concerning is these AI bots are going viral and people think that they're real people.

"And for context, comedians post their stand-up clips online to get you to come out and see live comedy," she said, adding that comedians spent a long time crafting jokes on stage and honing them into punchy lines.

Equity, the UK's largest entertainment union, has called for new laws to protect performers against the unauthorised use of their work by AI, arguing that existing copyright legislation is inadequate.

But legal protections are only part of the challenge. Identifying those responsible for copying the videos, and stopping them from doing it again, can also prove difficult.

Stopping these pirated performances is a game of whack-a-mole, says Alwyn Cruz, who has made a name for himself on social media by exposing AI musicians. "You don't even know who's making these [videos]. And then if you shut it down, they're just going to create another," he says.

"It's not only guitarists that are being cloned: there are drummers, taekwondo players that are being cloned, and I've seen this all over the internet."

Meta told BBC Verify it had a policy of alerting users when their videos were reposted without permission and was exploring ways to limit the reach of the accounts that repeatedly did this.

Earlier this week the head of Instagram, Adam Mosseri, said the platform is also "working quickly on ways to catch copies that are harder to spot, like when someone takes scripts or jokes and uses AI to swap out the visuals".

But for artists like Lloyd, the damage may already be done. "It takes years to master an instrument and seconds to take and rip it off as something else - and then people don't even know it's you."
