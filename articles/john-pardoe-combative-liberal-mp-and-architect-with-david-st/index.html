John Pardoe, combative Liberal MP and architect with David Steel of the Lib-Lab pact

TheTools · 18.11.2026 12:47 · source · permalink

His instinct for the jugular made him an impressive performer in the Commons, but he lost to David Steel in the Liberals’ leadership contest

John Pardoe, who has died aged 92, was a political bruiser who came off second best to David Steel in the hard-fought 1976 contest for the Liberal Party leadership. He was also the architect, with Steel, of the Lib-Lab pact which kept James Callaghan’s government in office during 1977 and 1978, briefly gaining a say in Denis Healey’s economic policies.

As MP for North Cornwall from 1966 to 1979, when he was defeated in the wake of the Jeremy Thorpe affair, the burly, saturnine Pardoe became a national figure. With Labour in power he was, as economics spokesman, a combative foil to Healey on inflation and taxes. Pardoe and the Chancellor had much in common, not least an overbearing manner.

Quick-witted and strong on television, Pardoe did much to establish the Liberals as a credible third force. He was on his party’s radical wing: for redistribution of wealth, for choice in education but against selection, for voucher-based welfare; and against apartheid and American involvement in Vietnam. He urged the Young Liberals to demonstrate against South African sporting teams but criticised their “lunatic fringe”.

Pardoe’s instinct for the jugular made him an impressive performer in the Commons. He came of age in 1973 questioning the full extent of corruption among Labour councils in the North East exposed by the Poulson affair.

In the frenzied atmosphere after the February 1974 election, as Edward Heath tried to hang on despite losing his majority, Pardoe opposed any deal with the Tories even though ministerial posts were offered. In opposition he had argued that the Liberals could only afford a pact or coalition if they had more than 30 seats; having won just 14 despite polling 6 million votes, they stayed out.

Early in 1976 Pardoe bid for the leadership as revelations over the homosexual relationship between Thorpe and the model Norman Scott devastated the party; having been Thorpe’s economic adviser, he felt personally traduced. In the Commons his thuggish streak cost him much of his earlier cross-party popularity, but it did contrast with Thorpe’s foppish style as public opinion polarised.

Steel, four years his junior, emerged as the man to beat. Pardoe’s radical and at times brutal campaign, headed by Lord Tim Beaumont and the gargantuan Cyril Smith, was characterised as “an invitation to the party to live dangerously”.

With the full colour of Thorpe’s intrigues (and Smith’s proclivities) yet to be revealed, Liberals opted for safety, and in a party ballot Steel – denouncing Pardoe as a “kamikaze pilot” – triumphed on July 7 1976 by 12,541 votes to 7,032. Steel, whose acolytes had falsely claimed that Pardoe wore a toupee, had proved himself “the more effective bastard”. He refused to make Pardoe deputy leader, but the media unofficially awarded him the title.

Pardoe talked of a collective leadership, but his career had peaked. Healey’s policies for a time showed signs of succeeding, and with Pardoe tied into them after securing the right to be consulted during the Pact negotiations of March 1977, his impact in the House waned.

His threat to force the Government to drop a Budget increase in petrol tax, followed by a climbdown, did not help. Nor did Callaghan’s refusal to make him a privy counsellor, which barred him from receiving sensitive information.

Pardoe saw the Pact, which he insisted had “restored confidence in Britain”, as essential to averting disaster in an early election, and spurned approaches from anti-pact Liberals to challenge Steel. He pressed Healey to include in his 1978 Budget large increases in indirect taxes offset by a 4p cut in income tax, but the Chancellor feared the impact on inflation and a stand-up row ensued. Later, with Labour MPs threatening rebellion, Pardoe forced Healey to scale back an increase in National Insurance contributions.

Labour hung on for almost a year after the pact’s termination in May 1978, and Pardoe’s vigorous prosecution of the 1979 election campaign led to most Liberals keeping their seats. Yet he himself – in the neighbouring constituency – fell victim to the Thorpe factor and lasting irritation over the Pact, losing to the Conservative Gerry Neale by 3,747 votes. Cornish MPs felt Pardoe lost because he had become famous and the locals did not approve.

He was a passionate advocate for Cornwall and its people. He campaigned for Cornish babies born in Plymouth to be registered as Cornish, sought curbs on the migration of hippies to the county, and even joined Mebyon Kernow, the Cornish nationalist party; the Liberal whips did not rate this disloyal.

John Wentworth Pardoe was born in Bridgwater, Somerset on July 27 1934, the son of Cuthbert Pardoe, a solicitor, and the former Marjorie Taylor. He was educated at Sherborne and Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, where he was an organ scholar.

After National Service in RAF fighter control, he joined Television Audience Measurement, who produced Britain’s pioneering ratings, in 1958. He was intermittently a consultant to television companies and director of a market research company.

Pardoe’s family were staunch Tories, but he flirted with Labour before joining the Liberals, who were then enjoying a mini-revival under Jo Grimond, in 1959; his wife, from a strongly Liberal family, converted him.

In 1961 he joined Liberal News as business manager on its takeover by Beaumont, then owner of Time and Tide. Soon after, he was adopted for Margaret Thatcher’s Finchley constituency, where the Liberals had briefly captured the council. He nursed the seat energetically, and as a lifelong supporter of Israel he capitalised on the outrage at Jews being barred from a local golf club. In 1964 he halved Mrs Thatcher’s majority to 8,802.

By now one of Grimond’s informal “Cabinet”, he found a rare winnable seat for the 1966 election – North Cornwall, where James Scott-Hopkins was defending a Conservative majority of 669. Pardoe campaigned frenetically, and on capturing the seat by 1,508 votes sang, “We have overcome” in his fine tenor voice to a torchlight rally at Launceston.

Not yet 32, he was one of four Liberal arrivals at Westminster, an influx the still-tiny party was unused to. His maiden speech was a call for class barriers in education to be broken down; it was widely acclaimed, but he did not hear it himself, having been temporarily struck deaf by catarrh.

Appointed education and social security spokesman, Pardoe was for five years an unpaid consultant to the National Association of Schoolmasters; he advocated the free education of underprivileged children at fee-paying schools.

When Grimond retired as leader in 1967, Pardoe backed Thorpe, his constituency neighbour, against Emlyn Hooson. The next year, with Steel, Richard Holme and Christopher Layton, he founded the Radical Action Movement, a ginger group canvassing a realignment of the Left that attracted just one Labour MP.

In 1968 Beaumont and Pardoe opposed the “establishment nominees” for party president and treasurer; both were elected and Pardoe found debts totalling £93,000. He took vigorous action, cutting headquarters staff by a quarter – triggering the resignation of its head, Pratap Chitnis – but stood down after a year.

In 1970 Pardoe hung on by 630 votes as Liberal strength in the Commons was halved to six. He responded vigorously, demanding and securing a commitment to fight every seat next time, and urging the Liberal Assembly in vain to scrap its agenda and debate the party’s future. Thorpe appointed him to head a policy review.

In 1972 he became a director of the international traders Gerald Metals, and the next year began a 10-year membership of the London Metal Exchange.

He took a prominent campaigning role during the Heath years as Liberals won by-elections in safe Tory seats, particularly after Thorpe promoted him to spokesman on the economy. In the first 1974 election he secured a thumping 8,729 majority, but after a summer campaigning with Thorpe on his much-derided hovercraft this was pegged back to 3,856.

After his defeat in 1979 he was briefly a television presenter with London Weekend and Westward, then for 10 years chaired Sight and Sound, the secretarial, word processing and training firm. From 1985 to 1989 he was a member of the Youth Training Board.

Pardoe stood down as candidate for North Cornwall in 1980 and, when the SDP was formed, spurned efforts by Liberal dissidents to field him as a candidate to block Shirley Williams. He revealed that Steel had talked Roy Jenkins out of joining the Liberals, a story Steel dismissed but which was latter accepted as true.

Steel wanted Pardoe back in the Commons, but, sceptical about the chances of the Liberal/SDP alliance breaking through, he stuck to his business career. He did join the Alliance’s 1983 campaign committee, being present at the “Ettrick Bridge Summit” when Steel elbowed Jenkins aside for the closing stages of the election.

Four years on, Steel and David Owen turned to Pardoe, still only 52, to run their parties’ joint campaign. Further ground was lost as Labour picked itself off the floor, Pardoe conceding that the Alliance “lacked passion”.

In 1958 John Pardoe married the actress Joyce Peerman, whom he had first met at kindergarten; they had a daughter and two sons.
