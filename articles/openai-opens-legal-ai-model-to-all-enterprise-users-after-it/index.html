OpenAI Opens Legal AI Model to All Enterprise Users After It Tops Law Benchmarks — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 18.11.2026 03:35 · source · permalink

OpenAI is widening access to a purpose-built legal version of its flagship model, saying the configuration now leads several independent tests of legal reasoning and research. The company said on November 17, 2026, that every ChatGPT Enterprise customer can request Astra for Law through its Trusted Access Program, including access through the API.

Astra for Law is not a separate foundation model but a configuration of GPT-6.2 Astra, OpenAI's newest frontier model, wrapped in legal-specific instructions and a dedicated search index. The setup is designed to help lawyers research questions, weigh competing authorities and draft documents.

The centerpiece of the announcement is the Harvey LAB-AA benchmark, an Artificial Analysis-run version of Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark that puts models through 120 assignments spanning 24 areas of law, from drafting deposition outlines to reviewing commercial leases to mapping ownership changes in acquisitions. Full credit requires meeting every requirement of an assignment without introducing a material claim unsupported by the source documents.

Astra for Law finished first, earning full credit on 20.8% of assignments. That compared with 15.0% for plain GPT-6.2 Astra and 13.1% for the top non-OpenAI entrant, Muse Wonder Full. OpenAI said the legal configuration cleared the all-pass bar nearly 40% more often than the runner-up, GPT-6.2 Astra.

The model also did so while producing far less text. Astra for Law averaged roughly 64,000 output tokens per assignment, 54% below Muse Wonder Full's 139,000, a gap OpenAI argues matters for legal teams billed by usage.

Note: LAB-AA figures come from Artificial Analysis's evaluation; Vals AI figures are from its November 16 evaluation.

Hallucinations have become a central concern as law firms adopt generative AI, since a single fabricated citation can damage a lawyer's standing in court. On that measure, Astra for Law averaged 0.03 material hallucinations per assignment, tied with GPT-6 for the lowest rate in the comparison and about half the 0.06 recorded by regular GPT-6.2 Astra. Claude Opus 5.5, the strongest non-OpenAI performer on the metric, averaged 0.59, roughly 20 times the Astra for Law figure. OpenAI said the legal model logged no material hallucinations on tasks that met the all-pass criteria.

On the Vals AI Legal Research Bench, where practicing lawyers pose questions on federal and state law, the share of answers passing every check climbed to 53.8% from about 46% in less than two months. Across all questions, the model captured 90.9% of available points including partial credit.

"Astra for Law 6.2 is a material improvement over prior Astra models across our benchmarks," said Julio Pereyra, head of legal research at Harvey. "It writes more comprehensive and better reasoned briefs that are preferred to those of GPT-6 Astra nearly 90% of the time by our pairwise judges." He added that the gain in depth came without a regression in accuracy, with Astra-class models continuing to post some of the lowest hallucination rates on the LAB and its U.S. Case Law extension.

OpenAI attributed the gains to a research team dedicated to legal intelligence and to a rapidly expanding search index. Since a September 2026 rollout to a small group of firms including Cooley, Ropes & Gray and Sullivan & Cromwell, the index has grown 34%, adding more than 80 million URLs, some of them covering international jurisdictions such as the UK, Australia and Canada. It now spans over 310 million URLs and covers more than 99.9% of published U.S. precedential case law, according to the company.

"We were grateful to preview early versions of Astra for Law, which were built with legal use-cases in mind," said John Savva, a partner at Sullivan & Cromwell. "Across both litigation and transactional matters, the models demonstrated impressive research depth and sensitivity to authority."

OpenAI has framed law as a research priority, arguing that lawyers underpin commerce, civil rights and legislation and therefore deserve tools built specifically for their work rather than general-purpose models adapted after the fact. The company said early users are deploying the system as a sparring partner to stress-test arguments, to separate recommendations from requirements in draft legislation, and to convert stacks of regulatory policy into internal workflows.

The competitive picture is sharpening. Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 remains a reference point in legal evaluations, while Google's Gemini 4 Argon was among the frontier models Harvey's graders compared against in brief-writing tests. Harvey and Grubel co-developed the LAB: USCL extension, in which models write a brief for a real U.S. federal case either by finding the relevant precedents themselves or by working from the record and citations the original lawyer used. Astra for Law placed among the top scorers in both versions.

For law firms and corporate legal departments, the practical effect is a lower barrier to procurement: access now runs through the ChatGPT Enterprise admin console rather than a bespoke pilot, and API access lets teams embed the model in their own research and drafting tools. The efficiency numbers may prove as consequential as the accuracy scores, since token consumption directly drives cost for firms running high-volume document review and brief drafting.

The broader question is whether benchmark leadership translates into courtroom and deal-room trust. Legal AI vendors have faced scrutiny over fabricated citations, and firms typically validate outputs before filing. OpenAI's emphasis on hallucination rates alongside accuracy suggests it sees reliability, not raw capability, as the gate to wider adoption.
