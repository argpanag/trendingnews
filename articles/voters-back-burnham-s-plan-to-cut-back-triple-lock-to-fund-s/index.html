Voters back Burnham's plan to cut back triple lock to fund social care reform by 48% to 28%, poll suggests

Sharon Graham, the Unite general secretary, said earlier this week that getting rid of the pensions triple lock to help fund social care reform would be “electoral suicide”.

This morning YouGov has published polling suggesting that she might be wrong. It suggests the voters support Burnham’s plan by 48% to 28%.

When people are asked in isolation if they support setting up a National Care Service, they were overwhelmingly in favour, by 74% to 11%.

YouGov also found that, when asked in isolation if they would back turning the triple lock into a double lock (by removing the earnings link), respondents were also in favour of that too, by 43% to 30%.

(In fact, Burnham’s proposal is more generous than a simply reversion to a double lock – see 8.45am.)

When YouGov put the two proposals together, and described them as Burnham’s plan, getting rid of the triple lock in its current form became more popular.

What people tell pollsters about a policy after it has just been announced may bear little resemblance to how they feel about it by the time it gets implemented. But these findings suggest Burnham is well placed to win the public argument on the merits of his policy.

Nandy says for too long government has ceded too much power over internet to private sector

Lisa Nandy, the culture secretary, criticised previous governments for giving private companies too much power over the internet revolution in her speech to the conference.

In the face of new technology, and in particular the threat that the internet posed to women, the state had been “far too small and far too timid for too long”, she said.

She went on:

I am on a mission to change it.

Because the World Wide Web – that created by Tim Berners Lee, to share knowledge, not exploitation - has revolutionised the way we live. An extraordinary democratic force, a source of connection, creativity and solace that has unleashed incomprehensible hatred, anger and polarisation - ask yourselves this, while it has undeniably shaped and defined us, have we done anything like enough to shape and define it?

Like the printing press, electricity and television, it has upended society. But what is almost unique to this moment is how absent the state - on behalf of its people - has been for so long. We have outsourced a revolution almost entirely to the private sphere to shape and police.

It is ... an almost violent indifference. To the women whose privacy and dignity is stripped from them. To the children who are preyed upon, their childhoods stolen. To people - how many of you in this room particularly women - hounded from public life. Where anger is monetised and profit put before people’s lives.

So conference, we are calling time on that indifference. Enough is enough.

Brexit 'the biggest lie of the last 30 years', Macron says

Jakub Krupa writes the Guardian’s Europe live blog.

Andy Burnham was critical of Brexit yesterday, but not as critical as the French president Emmanuel Macron who – not for the first time – has blasted those who campaigned for it.

Speaking with students at the Universidad Complutense de Madrid last night, Macron took aim at Brexit calling it “the biggest lie of the last 30 years.”

In a long defence of Europe’s unique integration process – where, according to Macron, even nationalist leaders have come to appreciate the bloc’s role – he described Brexit as a “disastrous exception” to the rule.

He said:

Brexit is the biggest lie of the last 30 years.

Those who said, ‘We’ll be richer,’ eight points of GDP less 10 years on. We’ll solve the immigration problem; it’s more complicated than before. And we’re going to be a huge success in international trade, it’s even worse.

Tories rule out taking part in Burnham's proposed cross-party commission on electoral reform

The Conservatives have ruled out supporting Andy Burnham’s proposed cross-party commission to work on electoral reform.

In an interview with Sky News, Kevin Hollinrake, the Tory chair, replied with a clear “no” when asked if his party would take part.

Hollinrake went on:

We think this is totally the wrong thing, despite the fact under any system of proportional representation, we’d have got more seats than we did in 2024, in that election.

That’s not the point. The point is you want decisive elections.

The public had a referendum on proportional representation back in 2011. It rejected it.

And we’re in an era now potentially moving into an era of sectarian politics, where you have small parties that represent particular interests, be it Gaza, be it Jeremy Corbyn’s Your Party.

Does anybody think it’d be a good idea for a government to be propped up by one of those parties who have single issue policies that really would be potentially very damaging to the UK itself and to our foreign relationships?

In fact, the referendum in 2011 was on the alternative vote – which is not strictly a proportional voting system, even though in UK politics it would deliver a more proportional outcome in some elections.

That was a reference to an exchange Cooper had with George Osborne when she was interviewed by him over the summer for his podcast, Political Currency. As Tory chancellor, Osborne cut the funding for Sure Start, which Cooper helped to set up as a junior minister in the Blair government. Osborne now runs his podcast with Ed Balls, Cooper’s husband.

As the Tories ran down the Sure Start programme, Sure Start centres were replaced by family hubs. When Labour took power in 2024, these were beefed up as Best Start family hubs.

Sure Start is now widely regarded as one of New Labour’s most successful social programmes. A report by the Institute for Fiscal Studies said that it had a significant positive impact on the health and educational outcome of children. “For every £1 of net spending, Sure Start may eventually generate around £11 in benefits for the children who attended,” the IFS said.

Burnham says he has spent 'a lot of time' working out how to fill £4.7bn funding gap in defence investment plan

Andy Burnham has said that he has spent “a lot of time” working out how to fill the £4.7bn gap in the plans to fund the defence investment plan.

When Keir Starmer unveiled the defence investment plan in June, after he had announced he was standing down to make way for Burnham, he said the increased investment would cost £15bn. While explaining where most of the money would come from, he said the remaining funds – worth £4.7bn over four years – would have to come from savings to be announced in the budget.

In an interview on Times Radio, Burnham said that finding this money had been a “major priority”.

He said:

I said as soon as I came in that we would fully fund the defence investment plan. I’ve spent a lot of time on that and it will be fully funded.

There was a shortfall - there won’t be [at the budget].
