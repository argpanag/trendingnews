Dhoni, off the record: Stories from the people who were there

TheTools · 18.11.2026 21:12 · source · permalink

Few cricketers have been watched as closely as MS Dhoni, and fewer have given away as little. But the people who shared dressing rooms, training camps and team buses with him have stories of their own. Cricbuzz asked some of them to pick one. Here they are, in their own words.

Ravichandran Ashwin

Legendary India offspinner; won four titles [two IPLs, two CLT20s] at CSK alongside Dhoni

As told to Vijay Tagore

It was the third season [of the IPL, in 2010]. I was a youngster, still adjusting to the IPL environment. In a match at Eden Gardens, MS was hit on the elbow by Shane Bond and had to sit out the next three games. Suresh Raina took over in his absence.

We were in a very difficult position in the league, having won just two of our first seven games. It took a serious effort from the team to turn things around and make the semi-finals. [In those days, the top four went into semi-finals, not the playoffs.]

And then came the last league game, in Dharamsala. Everyone knows what happened that evening. We needed 16 off the last over, and MS smashed Irfan Pathan for a boundary and two sixes, finishing the game in four balls. That win took us into the last four.

I saw a charged-up Dhoni that evening. After hitting the final six, he thumped his helmet and let his emotions out with a loud roar. After the match, he spoke to the team in the dressing room about what went through his mind before and during the game, and how he approached such situations.

Those were my early days in the IPL, and it was a valuable learning experience. It gave me a glimpse of Dhoni’s leadership. I also feel the famous bond between CSK and Dhoni was forged that evening, and it only grew stronger over the years.

Ramji Srinivasan

Former India strength and conditioning coach; ran CSK’s pre-season camps

As told to Kaushik Rangarajan

When CSK returned to the IPL in 2018, after two seasons out, I ran their pre-season camp. People called that squad the “Dad’s Army”. It’s been nearly eight years now, but some memories are fresh. We’d bought a machine to test players' hand-eye coordination. Nobody had one like that at that time.

It was the Dynavision. It's an interactive light-board system to assess and train visual-motor reaction time, peripheral awareness, hand-eye coordination, and cognitive processing. It measures your reflexes and helps you develop them. I brought it to the camp and we set it up in the dressing room.

MS Dhoni didn't question anything. He just let me conduct the camp. Everyone tried it, from Eric Simons to Suresh Raina, all the guys... And then I remember Dhoni casually coming in and he clocked a 0.3. A reaction time of 0.3 seconds kept up over a full minute was remarkable. And he turned to me and asked, "Is that okay?"

Okay? It was unbelievable. He did it twice [Ramji wanted to be sure machine wasn't malfunctioning] and again 0.3. "Is that okay?," he casually asked and went away. What do I even say to that? I even commented on it at the time. Those are reactions like a Formula 1 driver.

Later, I saw that he had a stumping clocked at 0.25 seconds of reaction time. It was exceptional. Unbelievable really.

KB Arun Karthik

Member of the CSK squad between 2008-10; later served as Assistant Fielding Coach

As told to Sooryanarayanan S

During my time at CSK, [Dhoni] once told me something superb: 'to communicate something to a player, you have to do it inside 15 seconds, and if it’s a hard conversation, it has to be done within 50 seconds. Not even one minute.'

When I was playing, I couldn’t understand it. But now that I’m coaching, I can relate. If you speak to someone for more than 50 seconds, they start thinking too much, and you need to end the conversation before it gets there.

We keep watching matches in the dressing room [during the season]. One thing he’d do, and I wouldn’t call it a habit, was predict what would happen off a particular ball. He’d play it like a game. I think that’s why he’s so strong tactically. If we were predicting six balls, he’d get at least five right. We’d get about three at best.

He watches everything: the Ranji Trophy, the Syed Mushtaq Ali Trophy, MLC, SA20. He loves watching cricket. He knows what each player does, how he bats, how he bowls. That’s why he’s a genius. A good leader needs to know people, their strengths and weaknesses. Only then can you get the best out of them. You aren’t born with leadership skills. It’s these small things he does, and he does them because he loves to, not because he’s forced to.

James Pamment

Mumbai Indians fielding and assistant coach, 2018–2024

As told to Prakash Govindasreenivasan

We played CSK in Pune [in IPL 2018]. I’d been working with Ishan Kishan a little on his wicketkeeping. At the end of games, people get together in their little groups and have a chat, so I asked Ishan, “Are you going to have a conversation with MS after the game? If you’re talking to him about your wicketkeeping, do you mind if I stand alongside and just listen?”

They got together at the end of the game and I stood at the side and listened. They spoke mainly in Hindi, but that was fine. I was a bit of a fly on the wall, just trying to grab a little wisdom. But when MS talks, he demonstrates what he’s saying. He was showing Ishan certain things with his hands about technique, and it was pretty much exactly what we’d been working on.

For somebody to be so good and yet keep things so simple: that was the beauty of MS Dhoni, and of how he went about being one of the world’s best wicketkeeper-batters. Just the simplicity. The work he’d done to get to that point, to be able to repeat it consistently with bat or gloves, that’s the genius really, isn’t it? It’s the basics repeated, and they would have been done at high volume.

It was an intriguing moment for me, and I kept referring to it with Ishan for the next three or four years we worked together.

Jaydev Unadkat

Rising Pune Supergiant teammate, IPL 2017

As told to Gokul Gopal

I was bowling really well that year [2017, when Steve Smith captained RPS]. One thing that worked was MS guiding me from behind the wicket: what and where to bowl to a particular batter. Because he’s a wicketkeeper, he doesn’t come up and talk to you every ball. He’d direct me with a hand gesture most of the time. If he wanted me to bowl wide, he’d just gesture: “Bowl wide to him now.” Take this fielder back, bring this one up. T20 is a quick game and a lot happens fast, so having someone behind you giving that assurance really helps.

There was a game at the Wankhede when I was bowling the last over [Mumbai needed 17], and Hardik and Rohit were batting. We planned that I’d bowl only wide of the crease, slower balls on a wide line. He kept telling me from behind that if the batter shifts, bowl wider. I got both of them out on those deliveries. Even though I bowled a wide or two in that over, he kept telling me to keep bowling there.

Mukesh Choudhary

CSK net bowler in 2021; in the CSK squad since 2022

As told to Sooryanarayanan S

I met Mahi bhai for the first time when I was a net bowler in the 2021 season, in Mumbai. After the nets, he called me by my name, and I thought, “Mahi bhai ko mera naam bhi yaad hai!” [Mahi bhai even knows my name!] I was shocked for a couple of minutes. I thought he was calling someone else, but he said, “Haan, tujhe hi bula raha hoon, aaja idhar” [Yes, I’m calling you, come here]. Then he talked me through the seam position with the red and the white ball. I have a video of that, and I still watch it now and then. That was a very special moment for me.

For the last two years, I’ve had the chair beside him in the dressing room. We’ve had a lot of conversations about cricket, and even when I wasn’t playing, he gave me a lot of good advice. He told me that just because you aren’t playing doesn’t mean you don’t belong at this level, and that sometimes, when things aren’t going your way, you have to wait it out. Whatever he told me gave me a lot of confidence. He speaks little, but what he says is very valuable, and I can never forget it. It feels good when a leader that big, whom I’ve spent five seasons with, tells you that.

Tushar Deshpande

CSK, 2022–2024; CSK’s highest wicket-taker in 2023 and 2024

As told to Aayush Puthran

In the 2023 season, we had a few injuries in the camp, so there was a good chance I’d get an opportunity. In one of the sessions, Mahi bhai came to me, put his arm around me and took me for a walk. He told me I had everything it takes to be successful in the IPL and at the international level: “You just need to back yourself, and we will back you.”

That gave me a lot of confidence. It was the best thing that could have happened to me. I became CSK’s highest wicket-taker that season, with 21 wickets. But the icing on the cake was that we won the IPL. Seeing Mahi bhai lift the cup felt unreal. It was something I had dreamt of.

The next year, I came in off a long domestic season [Mumbai won the Ranji Trophy] and didn’t have a great first game against RCB. He came to me again, put his arm around me and said, “Take your time. You’ve played a whole domestic season. Follow your process, stick to your plans and back yourself.” Again, I ended up as CSK’s highest wicket-taker that season, with 17 wickets, and made my international debut soon after [against Zimbabwe, July 2024].

He’s been a guiding light and a support for me throughout my CSK days.

Vidyut Sivaramakrishnan

CSK opener, IPL 2008

As told to Vijay Tagore

In 2007, after the World Cup, we played the Mushtaq Ali Trophy. Tamil Nadu won it for the first time, and a lot of us got picked by CSK. Dhoni was the captain, and we won the first four games quite comfortably. Then Matthew Hayden and Mike Hussey had to leave [for Australia’s tour of the West Indies], and that’s when I got the chance to open. I played nine games after that.

It never felt as though we were talking to a World Cup-winning captain, and the way he got the best out of people like me, Palani Amarnath and others was amazing. He spoke very little, but whenever he did, his words carried a lot of meaning. He would say a particular bowler should be approached in a certain way, you’d follow his advice, and it would work, not one time out of 10 but nine times out of 10.

I was having trouble playing Sohail Tanvir. He simply told me to try something different, perhaps stand slightly outside the crease. He spoke only when it really mattered.

He knew what was going to happen before it happened. He could read a batter’s mind. In the 2010 IPL final, he had a fielder [Matthew Hayden] right behind the bowler, and Pollard was caught there off Albie Morkel. That was a masterstroke, and it played a crucial role in winning us the title. It was an on-the-spot decision. He was never someone who spent hours planning with the video analyst. He was a man of the ground. He would simply say, “This is what is going to happen. I know what’s going to happen.”

Eric Simons

Former India bowling coach; worked with Dhoni at RPS & CSK

As told to Narbavi R

It was an absolute privilege to watch him win games that shouldn’t have been won, and to see him make decisions that might not have made sense at the time. When you saw them unfold, you realised how clever he was at reading the game. But for me, the great privilege was MS Dhoni the man.

I saw him at his highest moments, winning World Cups and IPL trophies, and I saw him when the team really struggled. He never changed. He was the same person who won the World Cup as the one who came last in an IPL. Results did not define him. To witness his humility, and a hotel room door that was literally always open to anybody, was part of what made him remarkable.

It is almost impossible to explain the demands made on him by everyone he came into contact with: the autograph, the handshake, just some acknowledgement. He never let that adoration change the way he saw life, or himself.

I always felt the secret to MS was his ability to take the complex and understand it simply, and to explain it simply: people, pressure, tactics, the game itself. A remarkable cricketer, but to me, even more so a remarkable man.
