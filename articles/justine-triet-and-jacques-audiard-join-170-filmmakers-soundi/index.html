Justine Triet and Jacques Audiard join 170 filmmakers sounding alarm over French film funding cuts

TheTools · 18.10.2026 10:38 · source · permalink

Source: SYSPEO/SIPA/Shutterstock

Justin Triet at the 2023 Palme d’Or award ceremony

More than 170 filmmakers including Jacques Audiard, Justine Triet, Jean-Pierre and Luc Dardenne, and Claire Denis have sounded the alarm over France’s proposed 2027 budget, warning measures affecting Canal+, France Télévisions and the CNC could put 400 films at risk over the next five years.

The filmmakers issued the warning in an open letter published in French newspaper Le Monde on Wednesday (October 14), as France’s National Assembly prepares to examine a 2027 finance bill.

Other signatories include Costa-Gavras, Mati Diop, Michel Hazanavicius, Audrey Diwan, Arnaud Desplechin, Robert Guédiguian, Agnès Jaoui, Dominik Moll, Pierre Salvadori, Alice Winocour and Rebecca Zlotowski.

“The new finance bill risks causing an unprecedented disappearance of French cinema from our screens,” they argued, estimating the measures could result in 80 fewer films being made each year.

The intervention marks the latest escalation in an industry backlash against a series of measures threatening France’s film financing ecosystem.

A key concern is the proposed doubling of VAT on pay-TV subscriptions from 10% to 20%. Canal+, France’s leading private financier of cinema, has said the move could cost it up to €200m annually and force cuts to jobs and investment in film and television.

In July, the group signed a five-year agreement with French cinema organisations committing €980m to French and European films between 2028 and 2032, representing around 100 co-productions a year. But the deal allows those commitments to be suspended in the event of a VAT increase.

The filmmakers estimate the change would result in €750m less investment in cinema. “Over five years, 400 films would not get made,” they said.

The VAT proposal forms part of France’s 2027 budget, unveiled on October 1 as the government seeks €54bn in savings and additional revenues to reduce the deficit. It still needs to pass through parliament and could be amended. Canal+ has described the proposal as a “direct attack” on its activities in France.

The letter also points to a further €47m reduction in France Télévisions’ resources in 2027 and the prospect of the state taking money directly from the CNC. “The financing of French cinema rests on two pillars, one public and the other private,” the signatories said. “The new finance bill proposes provisions that will affect both.” They noted the sector supports 260,000 direct and indirect jobs, while French films account for around 40% of domestic cinema admissions.

Their intervention follows warnings from industry organisations including filmmakers’ body ARP and producers union USPA, which have also called on the government to protect Canal+, France Télévisions and the CNC.

The filmmakers also point to wider threats facing the sector, from far-right calls to abolish the CNC and public broadcasting to what they describe as the “unprecedented standardisation” of content by predominantly US platforms. “Weakening our cinema means impoverishing our French and European identity,” they said, warning that abandoning an ambitious cultural policy risks “gradually losing what defines us.” 

The filmmakers are calling for a dedicated French “cinema law”, arguing the sector continues to operate under audiovisual legislation dating from 1986, before the emergence of streaming platforms and the digital economy. They said such legislation could safeguard France’s media chronology, regulate artificial intelligence, rethink public broadcasting financing and protect the CNC’s resources, while providing “the expression of a collective economic and cultural vision”.

The signatories urged MPs to back amendments put forward by France’s film organisations to protect public broadcasting and the CNC’s budget, and maintain the 10% VAT rate in exchange for commitments to invest in French production.

“This is not about avoiding a collective effort, but should we really dismantle, in the name of urgency, a model that works and has made France a cultural and economic exception?” they asked.
