Too Fast, Too Long: NTSB Details Amazon 767’s Final Moments In Miami

TheTools · 02.10.2026 19:23 · source · permalink

Five people were killed when an Amazon Prime Air-branded Boeing 767-300F freighter overran a runway at Miami International Airport (MIA) last month, but the newly released preliminary report from the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) provides the clearest reconstruction yet of how the accident unfolded. Flight 7598, operated by 21 Air on behalf of Amazon, arrived fast, touched down thousands of feet into the runway, and was still traveling at more than 110 knots when it reached the pavement's end.

The data contained within the preliminary report paints an extraordinary picture of the final seconds. The crew repeatedly discussed excessive speed during the approach; the 767 crossed the threshold at 177 knots, and a go-around was ultimately initiated with only about 930 feet (283 meters) of runway remaining, before being abandoned seconds later. The NTSB has not determined a probable cause, and its findings remain preliminary.

The Boeing 767 Was Too Fast Before It Even Reached The Runway

Warning signs were already building well before touchdown. About one minute and 42 seconds before the cockpit voice recorder stopped, the crew selected flaps 20, and one pilot cautioned that the aircraft was too fast. Further comments about excessive speed followed, with the NTSB noting that they did not always receive a verbal response. The autopilot was disconnected roughly 23 seconds later, and the approach was then punctuated by automated “sink rate” and repeated “too low terrain” alerts. The crew did not call for Flaps 30 until around 41 seconds before the recording ended.

When Flight 7598 crossed Runway 30’s displaced threshold, the 767 was still about 105 feet (32 m) above ground level and traveling at a ground speed of 177 knots. A conventional landing profile would normally put an aircraft at roughly 50 feet (15 m) over the threshold, while a Boeing 767-300’s Flaps 30 approach speed would typically be around 135–150 knots indicated, depending on weight and wind.

Flight 7598 was therefore arriving both higher and faster than a normal landing profile, and the preliminary report characterized the situation.

"By the time Flight 7598 crossed the threshold, speed concerns and automated warnings had been building for more than a minute, yet the 767 was still carrying substantial energy into a runway where thousands of feet would disappear before the landing was fully established."

The height is particularly notablebecause Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) landing-performance guidance assumes a 50-foot threshold crossing height and explicitly warns that an additional 50 feet of height can add roughly 1,000 feet (305 meters) to the landing distance. That is particularly notable because although Runway 12/30 at MIA is physically 9,360 feet (2,853 meters) long, it has a displaced threshold and a declared landing distance available of only 7,913 feet (2,412 meters). The aircraft simply couldn't afford to lose so much landing space, especially when traveling 25-30 knots faster than a conventional landing profile.

Too Long: The 767 Touched Down With Thousands Of Feet Already Gone

Earlier reporting on the accident highlighted the unusually long touchdown sequence. The flight data recorder confirms this, indicating that the nose and right main landing gear initially registered touchdown at around 158 knots, while the left main gear did not register touchdown until the aircraft had slowed to about 134 knots. Wheel braking began at 146 knots. Crucially, the NTSB found no recorded indication that the speed brakes deployed or that either engine's thrust reverser was used during the landing roll.

Then came perhaps the most striking part of the sequence. A pilot called for a go-around, and at approximately 120 knots, the brakes were released, and both throttles advanced to settings consistent with go-around thrust. Only about 930 feet (283 meters) of runway remained. Around four seconds later, with the aircraft still traveling at 117 knots and with just 415 feet (126 meters) of runway remaining, the throttles were returned to idle, and braking resumed.

The 767 ultimately crossed the end of the pavement at approximately 113 knots. It struck localizer and approach-lighting equipment before continuing beyond the airport perimeter and colliding with ground vehicles. Five people on the ground were killed, and another three were seriously injured; both pilots escaped with minor injuries. The wreckage was subsequently removed, and the affected runway reopened nine days later following inspections and repairs.

The Crew, The Missing Wind Call, And The Questions Still Unanswered

According to CBS News, the captain, who was flying the aircraft, had 7,267 total flight hours but only about 150 hours on the Boeing 767. He joined 21 Air in April and received his 767 type rating the following month. The first officer had around 2,800 total hours and had been with the airline since February 2025. Flight 7598 was reportedly the first day the two pilots had flown together. The NTSB has not yet identified any of those details as a cause of the accident.

Investigators are also examining the weather and air traffic control handling. Around the time of the crash, winds were reported from 190 degrees at 17 knots, gusting to 26 knots, producing a tailwind component for Runway 30. The controller did not provide Flight 7598 with wind information during its landing clearance, despite FAA procedures requiring controllers to give wind direction and velocity when a tailwind component exists, according to Reuters. The preliminary report does not conclude that this omission caused or contributed to the crash.

Much therefore remains unanswered: why the crew continued an approach after repeated comments about excessive speed, why touchdown occurred so far beyond the threshold, why the speed brakes and thrust reversers were not deployed, and why the attempted go-around was abandoned almost immediately. Amazon Air stopped its partnership with 21 Air following the accident, while the NTSB's broader investigation continues, with the aircraft, crew actions, operations, and other factors still under examination. The final report and probable-cause determination are likely to take considerably longer.
