The A20 Pro Chip Is Apple's High-Stakes Hardware Pivot to Own On-Device AI

TheTools · 12.09.2026 13:30 · source · permalink

Another Apple September event is in the books, and this time, most people are excited about Apple's new foldable iPhone Duo, or the kicked-up camera features on the iPhone 18 Pro. But I'm here for the chips and AI, and a lot of cool new stuff is coming to the iPhone on this front, too.

Apple's new CEO, John Ternus, now frames the iPhone as an "intelligent personal hub," putting Apple's most personal device at the center of its most powerful new capabilities. Translation: The iPhone's processor and thermal management are getting major upgrades to enable new Apple Intelligence features.

Let's take a look at those upgrades, starting with the processor, the A20 Pro silicon. It's the world's first 2-nanometer smartphone processor. Rather than a routine annual performance tweak, it represents a total hardware rethink built from the ground up to handle local AI workloads in a bid to keep them fast, private, and useful.

A20 Pro Silicon: Shifting Your Cores Into a Higher Gear

Long gone are the days when an iPhone processor's main job was simply to power iOS. The A20 Pro isn't just there to smooth your scrolling; it's designed to churn through AI workflows.

The CPU has been upgraded to a six-core design, combining two super cores and four efficiency cores. Apple introduced the idea of "super cores" earlier this year at the spring launch of the MacBook Neo and new M5 MacBooks. And while many nuances differentiate a super core from a performance core from an efficiency core, here's the simple breakdown:

  • Super cores: Peak single-thread speed and boosted throughput

  • Performance cores: Best for multithreaded use

  • Efficiency cores: Best for low-energy applications and background tasks

On the Mac side of things, you don't always see super cores and efficiency cores on the same chips—M5 and M6 leverage efficiency cores for longer battery life, but higher-tier M5 Pro and M5 Max chips stick to super cores and performance cores for pure power.

The A20 Pro chip in the new iPhones is taking a different approach. It uses the same 2nm manufacturing process employed on the M6 chip, but it pairs super cores and efficiency cores to shift from low to high gear, with no middle-ground performance cores in between. That's a step up from the A19 Pro, which uses two performance cores and four efficiency cores.

And just to goose things a little more, Apple is adding neural accelerators to every core, offloading some of the math used for running AI models more efficiently.

The A20 Pro also boasts a faster seven-core GPU (according to Cupertino, it's up to 40% faster than the previous generation) and the same dual 16-core Neural Engine seen on upcoming Macs, which should theoretically give the iPhone more power for complex apps, gaming, and on-device AI.

The A20 Pro also boosts memory throughput by 50%, because the CPU cores lie side by side with the memory on the silicon die. Shortening the distance between them, Apple says, alleviates data bottlenecks among the CPU, GPU, and Neural Engine, enabling better sustained multitasking performance and unlocking new capabilities for running local AI models.

Also, Apple claims the new super cores will drive a 20% improvement in CPU performance. And that's where things really start to heat up.

Vapor Chamber Cooling: Tackling the Mobile Thermal Bottleneck

Cramming so much compute into a pocket-sized device necessitates trade-offs, with thinner phones making it more difficult to keep chips cool enough to run fast. (A phone chip running too hot can result in throttling, or a chip decelerating to keep heat levels within a safe or comfortable zone.) It's an even bigger problem for phones than for laptops. The latter have more room for cooling fans or passive ventilation, and they aren't expected to live in someone's sweaty hip pocket.

Apple attempts to solve this thermal problem by borrowing a technology from high-performance laptops (and the most recent iPad Pro), called vapor chamber cooling. At its core is a flat, vacuum-sealed metal chamber that's partially filled with fluid (usually deionized water). The liquid evaporates and recondenses inside the chamber continuously to eliminate hot spots and efficiently draw heat away from the hot-running chips. It's like harnessing the water cycle in a slim sandwich of metal. Boiling, evaporation, condensation, and capillary return are all handled in the chamber, continuously moving heat away from the chip without moving parts.

The iPhone 17 employs vapor-chamber tech, but the iPhone 18's implementation, according to Apple, is better: new materials, triple the total surface area, and a package design that relocates the memory for better cooling and dissipation. The result should be better performance at the chip's limits, for graphics-intensive apps and games, and for extended multitasking. Longer peak performance is the goal, and Apple claims the vapor-chamber design used with the A20 Pro will improve sustained performance by 40% compared with the last-generation iPhone.

Smarter Siri: Personal Agents and Deepfake Defense

That's a lot of computing muscle, but Apple will need every ounce of it to power the long-awaited revamp of Siri (now called Siri AI) and other improvements coming in iOS 27.

Powered by the A20 Pro's increased compute, Siri is getting a lot more powerful. It's now a standalone AI companion that can handle full conversations and that should exhibit improved on-device dictation. Plus, customizable voice personalities will let you set your Siri to your liking, dialing in the level of engagement from nonchalant to bubbly and eager.

But it's also giving Siri new abilities, making Siri an active AI agent that can handle tasks across apps, with on-screen context awareness. It can now do things for you across more than 300,000 apps, leveraging personal context from apps such as Mail, Messages, and Photos.

In addition to a smarter Siri, new AI-enhanced camera features can analyze physical surroundings and answer real-time questions about what the camera sees. And since image editing and generation have gotten scary good, Apple is also using more tech to prevent AI deepfakes. Apple Reference Image uses cryptographic pixel signing that adds data directly at the sensor level during capture. It's sort of like a "digital negative" of metadata, offering a verifiable guarantee of an image's authenticity.

Apple is also adding support for Google's metadata standard, SynthID, for broader AI image identification, so you can be warned when a picture was manipulated or generated with AI. The result is a multifaceted defense against synthetic media, whether it's in your browsing, your messages, or your own photos.

Apple's Betting That Hardware Is the New AI Moat

While the headlines and customer hype will surely center on folding screens and revamped cameras, Apple has done a lot to position the iPhone at the center of its growing AI ecosystem. New agentic capabilities in Siri and the ability to run local models all come down to dramatic improvements in the hardware.

Apple's big bet is that the mobile AI race won't be won in the cloud, but in your pocket. And with this new, tightly integrated combination of 2nm chips, enhanced processing and cooling, and a more robust Apple Intelligence, Apple means to own every piece of it.
