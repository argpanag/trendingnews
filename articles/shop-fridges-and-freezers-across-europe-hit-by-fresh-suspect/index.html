Shop fridges and freezers across Europe hit by fresh suspected cyber-attack

TheTools · 02.11.2026 16:39 · source · permalink

Chains in seven countries say cooling unit temperatures rose overnight in incident similar to hack of French shops

Supermarket chains in at least seven European countries have said the temperatures of refrigerators and freezers in some of their shops soared overnight to as high as 50C (122F), echoing a cyber-attack last month that caused millions of euros of damage in France.

Retailers in Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Finland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden reported problems with their refrigeration systems on Monday, and several companies confirmed they had been hacked.

The EU food and agriculture commissioner, Christophe Hansen, said the repeated cyber-attacks were deeply concerning, calling them “a deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain and undermine consumers’ confidence in it”.

The incidents follow an attack in France last month in which hackers seized control of cooling units in as many as 700 supermarkets, raising their temperatures as high ⁠as 50C (122F) and causing fires and food losses estimated in the tens of millions of euros.

A growing range of devices, including refrigeration systems, can now be monitored and controlled remotely, leading authorities to particularly fear government-sponsored hybrid attacks ​in after Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

Sweden’s Coop chain told the daily Aftonbladet that six of ​its shops ‌had been ​hit, while ICA said it was “aware that a limited ​number of stores were affected by an incident overnight which impacted their refrigeration and freezer systems”.

An ICA shop at Sjöbo in southern Sweden posted that its fridges and freezers had been hacked and their temperature set at 50C. Another in Stockholm said its freezers were broken “due to a 2am cyberattack” and that “everything was destroyed”.

The head of Sweden’s national cybersecurity centre, John Billow, said it ‌was working on incidents around the country. It had established the country had “quite a lot of exposed operational ‌technology devices” that needed updating or patching, he told Reuters.

The Dutch retailer Ahold Delhaize, which last ​month ​reported a ​similar incident ​to police in the Netherlands, said fridges and ⁠freezers in dozens of its ⁠supermarkets in ​Belgium ‌and ‌Luxembourg had been hit.

It did not specify the cause, but reporters visiting a shop north-east of Brussels saw refrigerators and freezers empty and a sign reading: “Due to cyberattacks, there will be no products on the refrigerated or freezer shelves. We offer our sincere apologies.”

A company spokesperson, Sarah ‌De Meester, said the range of fresh and frozen products was “temporarily somewhat limited” in affected shops, but that distribution centres were operating normally and there was no disruption to deliveries.

Belgium’s Le Soir newspaper reported that dozens of shops and supermarkets had been affected, including five Spar outlets, Shop & Go convenience shops attached to Q8 service stations and some smaller Proxy Delhaize mini-markets.

“We started mobilising technicians at 1am,” Stephan Moelans, the chief executive of Sabcobel, which operates Delhaize’s refrigeration systems, told the paper. “We spent all night and morning disconnecting the affected installations.”

He told the Belga news agency the ‌incident had been a cyber-attack and that the units’ evaporators were switched to defrost mode.

The Colruyt and Intermarché supermarket chains also said they had been hit by technical problems affecting fridges in five and 15 of their shops respectively, and Carrefour Belgium reported disruption in about 15 of its 700 shops.

“It’s a cyber-attack, there is no other reason,” said Mich Vergauwen, a Carrefour spokesperson. An Ikea Belgium spokesperson likewise told Agence France-Presse that a cyber-attack had caused the temperature of freezers in its Mons shop to rise suddenly.

Ikea, famous for its in-store Swedish meatballs, said it had had to ‌remove some food products from refrigeration units ​as a precaution and expected some food waste as ⁠a result.

In Finland, the Helsingin Sanomat newspaper said shops operated by the Kesko supermarket chain were investigating similar problems, while the Dutch public radio NOS reported that the systems of at least 17 mainly small local shops had been affected.

At one Estonian supermarket chain, attackers accessed a computer controlling the refrigeration system and turned up the temperature to 50C, the refrigeration supply company Kitman Coldtech told the ERR broadcaster.

“This is very fresh, it happened this morning,” its chief executive, Jann Mutso, said, adding that the attack was responded to within minutes and there had been no significant damage. The Selver supermarket chain said one of its outlets had been affected.

Ireland’s third-largest supermarket ⁠chain, ⁠SuperValu, said ​refrigeration systems limited ⁠to ​a ‌specific ‌model ‌in 13 of its 223 shops ‌had been affected by ​a cyber-incident, ⁠and that chilled and ​frozen products ​had been removed from ​sale.

Many cyber-attacks involve extortion or are politically motivated and are potentially carried out by state-level actors, said Tom Hegel of the cybersecurity firm SentinelOne. But the supermarket attacks, with no financial demands, point to sabotage, he said.

“Retail is an easy target because retail chains run enormous estates of refrigeration controllers, building management systems, door access panels and point-of-sale gear,” Hegel told Reuters.

Patrick Lamplmair of the cybersecurity firm Tributech said cyber-attackers would keep going “until it stops working” because the method was “cheap and the damage is immediate”. Many refrigeration systems had known and as yet unfixed flaws, he said.

“Thousands of these controllers were still reachable from the internet after the French attack,” he added. “A few commands to a temperature controller can destroy stock overnight and close stores.”

In the French attack, the hackers pirated controllers mostly supplied by the Danish company Danfoss, which said subsequently that it had identified some security flaws last year and had “long recommended” customers install updates.

The hackers remotely overrode the settings on thousands of freezers, fridges and chiller cabinets at chains including Intermarché, Coopérative U, Carrefour, Leclerc and Monoprix, pushing them from as low as -27C to well over 50C.

The shops had to discard vast quantities of fresh meat, dairy and frozen products. Individual supermarkets reportedly suffered losses ranging from tens of thousands of euros to more than €130,000 (£109,000).

The Paris prosecutor’s office has opened a formal investigation into the French attack and the prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, instructed France’s national cybersecurity agency, Ansii, to investigate.

The agency subsequently reported that supermarkets using controllers from two other companies had also been targeted in the attack, but authorities have not indicated who could have been behind the incidents. There have been no claims of responsibility.
