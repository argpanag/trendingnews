How to light up your neighborhood for the holidays — not in flames though - Spartan Newsroom

TheTools · 25.11.2026 15:08 · source · permalink

Highly decorated house in Coopersville. Credit: Danny Becerra

By OLIVIA O’NEILL

Capital News Service

LANSING — While Christmas lights and decorations can fill communities with holiday cheer and bring that feeling of inner-warmth on cold nights, they can also heat up electrical sockets and buildings when a fire breaks out.

Most Michigan communities don’t regulate residential holiday lights through the fire code, said Kurt Corradi, the first vice president of the Michigan Association of Fire Chiefs and current fire chief in the Bay City Department of Public Safety. 

Fire codes cover commercial and public buildings in regards to live trees, flame-resistant decorations and keeping fire exits and equipment clear. These public places include assembly spaces, schools, healthcare facilities and businesses, but not homes. 

Typically it’s not cities that set limits on house decorations. 

Restrictions on holiday lights and decorations generally come from local homeowners association regulations through nuisance, zoning or noise rules, which include rules on brightness, timing or traffic from people coming to look at large displays, said Corradi. 

HOA and condominium rules are private agreements. 

He said fire departments aren’t typically involved with such regulations unless there’s a hazard. Examples include extension cords run through doors or windows, decorations blocking exits in multi-unit buildings and open flames on balconies.

However, HOA regulations focus more on zoning, neighborhood aesthetics, light pollution and other local considerations, not fire-safety. 

According to the National Fire Protection Association, U.S. fire departments respond to about 835 home fires a year started by decorations and an estimated 143 annually started by Christmas trees. Electrical distribution or lighting equipment is involved in 40% of these tree fires. 

“The risk is real,” Corradi said. “Tree fires are less common, but when they happen, they’re more likely to be deadly. That’s because a dry tree burns very fast, and more than a third of fires happen in January, when trees dry out.”

According to the Michigan Agriculture Council’s Michigan Grown initiative, the state ranks third in the nation in the number of Christmas trees harvested, supplying around 2 million fresh trees annually. 

Fresh trees are a big flag of caution for holiday season fires, said Emily Fitzgerald, a Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs communications representative. 

“For those using natural Christmas trees, we recommend keeping trees properly watered, maintaining at least 3 feet of clearance from heat sources and removing trees when they become dry,” Fitzgerald said.

“Ultimately, we encourage Michigan residents to enjoy their holiday traditions while taking a few simple precautions to protect their homes, neighbors and families,” she said.

While trees contribute greatly to fires in the holiday season, Corradi said the most common problems seen by the State Fire Marshal’s Office are overloaded outlets. 

Overloading problems include daisy-chained power strips or extension cords, indoor-rated lights and cords used outside, damaged or old light strings, cords pinched under doors or rugs and decorations too close to heat sources like candles, space heaters and fireplaces.

“Cords and outlets can overheat inside walls or behind furniture where you can’t see them,” Corradi said. “Fewer lights on a properly sized circuit are safer than more lights on an overloaded one.”

Recommendations from the State Fire Marshal and Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs fall into seven categories: using properly tested electrical decorations; inspecting lights and cords annually; following manufacturers instructions; plugging outdoor displays in protected outlets and using LED lights; setting timers or turning off lights when leaving home; avoiding overloading; and using only outdoor lights outdoors and indoor lights indoors.

“From a fire safety perspective, holiday lighting has become safer with the widespread adoption of LED technology,” Fitzgerald said. “Regardless of the type of lighting or decorations used, we recommend that all electrical products be tested and certified by a nationally recognized testing laboratory, such as UL or ETL.”

Even with certified electrical decorations and lights, wires can get frayed or pinched. The general recommendation is to inspect them each year and throw out damaged ones, Corradi said.

He said it’s important to know the limit to avoid overloading electrical outlets, extension cords and circuits. 

Overloading outlets isn’t always about shoving plugs on top of plugs like in the movie “A Christmas Story.” It can be about following the manufacturer’s limit on how many strands can be connected. 

Use outdoor-rated lighting and extension cords for exterior decorations like they are intended. Plugging outdoor displays into GFCI-protected outlets and switching to LED lights are good precautions to take with decorations, Corradi said. 

“They run cooler and draw much less power,” he said. “So you can have the same display with less load and a lower electric bill.” 

In addition to safety precautions, checking with local municipalities or HOA restrictions on lights and decorations is important, Fitzgerald said.

“Some communities and HOAs regulate how long holiday decorations may be displayed, prohibit flashing or strobing lights or limit the hours lighting may be operated,” said Fitzgerald.

Corradi said, “Holiday displays boost neighborhood pride, draw visitors to downtowns and bring neighbors together.”

“We want people to celebrate,” Corradi said. “We just want them to do it safely.”
