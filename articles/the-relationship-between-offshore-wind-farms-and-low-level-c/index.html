The relationship between offshore wind farms and low-level clouds

TheTools · 18.11.2026 09:49 · source · permalink
  • Open Access News
  • Energy

Researchers at the University of Twente demonstrate that offshore wind farms and low-level clouds mutually influence one another, revealing that cloud radiative properties alter power generation while turbine wakes dynamically reshape atmospheric boundary layers

Published on November 17, 2026, in the Journal of Renewable and Sustainable Energy (AIP Publishing), the research challenges standard wind farm engineering models that rely on cloudless, idealised atmospheres, proving that cloud cover directly alters turbine power output while large-scale turbine arrays physically deform surrounding cloud layers.

Bidirectional atmospheric feedback mechanics

Offshore wind farms across the North Sea and northern Europe frequently operate beneath persistent, low-altitude cloud decks. By scaling micro-domain climate simulations up to full-scale offshore wind farm models, researchers Davide Selvatici, Thijs van den Ham, and Richard J.A.M. Stevens mapped how these two systems interact:

  • Cloud radiative impact on power output:
    • Low-level clouds and tropospheric humidity directly affect wind farm energy generation through their radiative properties—specifically by altering longwave thermal radiation, which shifts atmospheric stability and wind speeds at turbine hub height.
  • Boundary layer deformation by turbines:
    • Conversely, wind farms physically alter local cloud behaviour. As air approaches the turbine array, the wind farm pushes the atmospheric boundary layer upward in front of the turbines and pulls it downward within the turbulent wake behind them.
  • Altitude and climate sensitivity:
    • The strength and nature of this two-way interaction depend heavily on prevailing meteorological conditions, particularly cloud base height, tropospheric moisture, and vertical temperature gradients.

Implications for wind farm modelling and forecasting

Historically, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and wake simulations for offshore wind farms omitted cloud physics to reduce computational overhead. The University of Twente team’s findings establish new priorities for renewable energy meteorology:

  1. Closing forecasting blind spots:
    • Incorporating cloud radiative transfer and humidity dynamics into site assessments and grid forecasting models is essential for accurately predicting power yield in cloudy maritime regions like the North Sea.
  2. Next-generation computational scaling:
    • As high-performance computing capacity expands, integrating real-time meteorological conditions across larger multi-farm clusters will allow operators to optimise array layouts and minimise wake-induced atmospheric losses.
