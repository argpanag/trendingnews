Secret drone mothership threatens Russia’s foothold in Africa

TheTools · 23.11.2026 20:59 · source · permalink

Attack on Putin’s vessel in Libyan port shows Ukraine is expanding operations to another continent

When Vladimir Putin praised the growing strength of the Russian navy three years ago, he singled out a brand new corvette as a symbol of pride.

The Russian president said during his Navy Day speech that the 342ft vessel, Merkury, and its St Andrew’s cross signified “the inviolability of our naval traditions”.

The ship, commissioned in 2023 and capable of carrying KH-35 cruise missiles and a Ka-27 helicopter, is today largely submerged, with only its bows visible above water at Benghazi’s port in eastern Libya.

Over the weekend, the Merkury was the target of an audacious Ukrainian drone attack, which has dealt a sharp strategic blow to Putin’s ambitions in Africa.

A flotilla of Kyiv’s exploding autonomous craft has shown that Putin’s forces are no longer safe in the Mediterranean, or in the Libyan naval logistics hubs that are vital for supplying his mercenaries across Africa.

After months of Kyiv hitting refineries and oil plants deep inside Russia’s territory, Ukraine has now extended its deep-strike drone war on to the edge of Africa.

Volodymyr Zelensky said the attack, more than 1,500 miles from the front lines of eastern Ukraine, had been “an exemplary Ukrainian operation in response to Russia’s war”.

To reinforce the message of Kyiv’s growing reach, a Russian cargo ship, the MV Angara, was simultaneously hit by a sea drone while, a day earlier, another cargo vessel called the Sparta II had also been hit in the Baltic.

Ukraine said the MV Angara had been transporting Russian weapons. Mr Zelensky said: “We continue our operations in designated waters.”

Sea Baby drones hard to intercept

There have been several reports this year of Ukrainian drone specialists operating in western Libya, in the cities of Misrata, Tripoli and Zawiya.

In September, Libyan authorities denied a report by CNN that they were allowing Ukraine to use a military base to strike Russia’s “shadow fleet” in the Mediterranean.

Ukraine’s security service (SBU) said seven naval drones loaded with explosives were launched from a “mother ship” during the attack on the Merkury.

In the absence of a traditional navy, Ukraine has relied on the development of cutting-edge sea drones to neutralise Russia’s advantage, becoming a global pioneer of the technology.

Multiple Ukrainian media outlets reported that Kyiv had used Sea Baby drones to carry out Sunday’s attack. The uncrewed naval drones carry a payload of up to 2,000kg and can travel a little more than 55mph, with a reduced radar signature that makes them hard to intercept, especially at greater distances.

Some models of the inexpensive drones, which cost under £230,000 per unit, can also carry thermobaric launchers, machine-gun turrets and launch swarms of first-person view (FPV) drones, while others are equipped with AI-assisted targeting.

Such drones, originally developed by the SBU, have previously been used in attacks on Russian warships and maritime infrastructure in the Black Sea and the Mediterranean.

Ukraine has increasingly deployed operational drone motherships since early 2025, to extend the effective range of its unmanned weapons systems and circumvent electronic jamming.

Putin’s power play undermined

The location of the Merkury’s sinking is critical for Putin’s ambitions to project power in the Mediterranean, Middle East and in Africa.

Until the fall of Bashar al-Assad’s regime in late 2024, the Syrian port of Tartus had served as the Kremlin’s main naval logistics hub for operations in the Med, Middle East and Africa.

Yet with Assad gone, Syria’s new leaders insisted Tartus fell under stricter civilian control, and Moscow found itself needing a new base.

Its gaze quickly fell on Tobruk and Benghazi in eastern Libya under the control of Gen Khalifa Haftar.

Assad was barely gone when flight- and shipping-tracking websites started to pick up Russian military transports shuttling between Syria and Libya, as Moscow began transferring air defences, troops, armour and heavy equipment.

At the same time there were plans to upgrade the Libyan ports and nearby military airfields were refurbished and expanded.

“As international attention remains focused elsewhere, Russia is quietly expanding its presence in Libya,” the Washington Institute for Near East Policy warned last month.

The foreign policy think tank said Libya gave Russia “a highly strategic location that offers access to oil resources, military bases, and a deep-water naval port in the East Mediterranean”.

“If Moscow succeeds in consolidating its position there, it would gain a critical hub from which to project power in the Middle East and across Nato’s southern flank, potentially interfering with the alliance’s naval operations, overflights, and military readiness.”

Libya a critical staging post

The ports’ significance extended far beyond the Mediterranean coast.

The proximity of the ports and air bases made the country a jumping-off point for supplying mercenary missions on the continent staffed by the Wagner Group and later Russia’s Africa Corps, particularly in the Sahel and East Africa.

The Kremlin has wooed African leaders to gain access to raw materials, such as gold, to lessen its own diplomatic isolation and to edge out Western influence.

Offering mercenaries to help against insurgents, or to keep a regime in power, has been Moscow’s way to curry favour and open up deals on energy and resources.

“Libyan bases have also helped the Kremlin route military flights, weapons, and personnel to other locations in Africa, particularly Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger,” said the institute.

Russia’s operations in Africa have been a target for Kyiv before, and Ukrainian forces have at times seemed to be fighting a shadowy front on the continent.

Small groups of drone specialists are reported to have been in Mali and Sudan, helping Russia’s enemies wherever they find them.

But none of their operations have been as significant as the attack in Benghazi.

Osama Hamad, prime minister of Libya’s eastern-based government, said ‌he had asked the UN to investigate “suspicions that the boats had been launched from western Libya” and to verify the presence of Ukrainian military experts there.

No comment was immediately available from Tripoli’s rival government.

Italy fears war on its shores

Antonio Tajani, Italy’s foreign minister, expressed concern about the risk of the Ukraine war spreading to the Mediterranean.

“This is concerning, if it is a continuation of the war between Russia and Ukraine just a few hundred kilometres from Italy, especially since we are working for stability in Libya,” he said.

“War in the western Mediterranean is something that must be avoided at all costs. It’s too close for comfort, but we hope these are just isolated incidents.”

The Kremlin itself was taciturn.

Dmitry Peskov, the Kremlin’s spokesman, declined to comment on the attack, saying that Russia’s ministry of defence would provide more information “if deemed to be appropriate”.

However, Russian media blamed the British government, citing the presence of “intelligence centres belonging to the UK, Italy and the US [located] at an air force academy base in the Libyan city of Misrata”.

Benghazi and Tobruk are not the only ports in Africa where Russia has sought a maritime footprint for its operations on the continent.

Moscow particularly covets a naval base on the Red Sea and has been in talks for years with the Sudanese government about setting up a site, though its civil war has put that on hold.

Yet as Moscow’s naval commanders look at the wreckage of the Merkury and assess the growing reach of Ukraine’s drones, they will be asking whether their warships will be any safer elsewhere.
