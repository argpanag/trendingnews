Arkansas football players Caleb Wooden, Sean Williams win preliminary injunction in case vs. NCAA | Whole Hog Sports

TheTools · 14.09.2026 16:22 · source · permalink

Today at 11:22 a.m.

FAYETTEVILLE — Washington County Circuit Judge Doug Martin on Monday granted a preliminary injunction against the NCAA that was sought by Arkansas football players Caleb Wooden and Sean Williams. 

Martin enjoined the NCAA from enforcing its Bylaw 12.6 that was passed this summer and granted a fifth year of eligibility to athletes in the high school classes of 2023 and later. Williams and Wooden graduated high school in 2022 and would be limited to four seasons of eligibility without the injunction. 

As part of Martin's ruling, the NCAA is also prohibited from enforcing its so-called rule of restitution, which allows the NCAA to penalize a team that plays an athlete through a court order that is overturned by an appellate court.  

Neither Wooden nor Williams have played in a game this season. They traveled to Salt Lake City for the Razorbacks' 43-10 loss to Utah on Saturday but did not dress out for the game. 

Martin heard nearly three hours of testimony and arguments last Thursday from attorneys representing Williams, Wooden and the NCAA in the joint lawsuit filed by the players. He said at the conclusion of the hearing that he would “digest” the information and would rule as quickly as possible. 

In his opinion, Martin wrote the plaintiffs were likely to succeed on the merits of their lawsuit and could suffer irreparable harm if their eligibility was revoked. 

“The court finds that Plaintiffs' irreparable harm is not merely the loss of NIL compensation, revenue-sharing payments, scholarship benefits, stipends, or other money,” Martin wrote. “Even if every financial component of Plaintiffs' losses could later be calculated and recovered, monetary damages cannot recreate a collegiate football season after it has passed. The court cannot predict what Plaintiffs will accomplish during the 2026-27 season, and it need not do so. 

“What is certain is that, absent relief, they will lose the opportunity to participate and the benefits associated with that participation. No damages award entered after the season can restore a game not played, a practice missed, game film never created, athletic development and exposure never received, relationships and team experiences lost, or educational and professional choices Plaintiffs made in reliance upon this opportunity. Plaintiffs therefore lack an adequate remedy at law and face irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary injunctive relief.” 

Wooden and Williams testified they were offered a chance to play at Arkansas after a July 31 decision in the U.S. District Court of Colorado, where the judge in Wisne v. NCAA granted a preliminary injunction that granted eligibility this year to all athletes in the high school class of 2022. That federal decision was stayed by the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals on Aug. 21, which led many athletes in multiple sports to seek temporary restraining orders in state courts. 

Martin granted Wooden and Williams a temporary restraining order Aug. 26 that made them eligible to play until a ruling was made on the preliminary injunction. 

“The court cannot treat the period from July 31 through August 21 as though it did not occur,” Martin wrote in his ruling Monday. “The evidence establishes that real opportunities were presented to these Plaintiffs during that period and that Plaintiffs acted upon them. The world in which these Plaintiffs were operating treated them as college student-athletes. By the time the Wisne injunction was stayed on August 21, Plaintiffs' interests were no longer merely prospective; they had materialized and were concrete.” 

Martin’s ruling came five days after Arkansas soccer player Makenzie Malham was denied a preliminary injunction in her case by Washington County Circuit Judge Matt Durrett. Malham, a high school class of 2022 graduate, played in five games this season but is ineligible without the injunction she sought. 

It was not clear last week whether she would appeal to the Arkansas Court of Appeals. 

In the Malham case, Malham's attorney argued she was an intended third-party beneficiary of an agreement that made the University of Arkansas a member of the NCAA, thus resulting in breach of contract. 

From the bench, Durrett said it was a stretch there was ever a contract between Malham and the NCAA, and if there was a contract, it expired when she exhausted her eligibility at the end of the 2025 season. 

Martin, however, wrote Williams and Wooden “have demonstrated a reasonable probability of success on their third-party beneficiary breach of contract claim, the court need not determine at this preliminary stage whether Plaintiffs will ultimately prevail on their alternative theory.

“Nevertheless, the evidence establishes that actual opportunities to compete and associated benefits were presented to Plaintiffs, and they materially changed their positions in reliance upon those opportunities. ... Their scholarships, roster positions, enrollment, housing arrangements, team participation, and associated financial and educational interests had become concrete. Those circumstances support Plaintiffs' contention that injustice can be avoided only by enforcement and further support the equitable relief requested.”
