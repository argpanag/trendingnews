El Niño will cause ‘pandemic-scale mortality’

TheTools · 12.11.2026 09:30 · source · permalink

Nearly 500,000 deaths expected in 2027 from extreme heat alone as oceans get warmer

This year’s El Niño will cause “pandemic-scale mortality” worldwide, epidemiologists have claimed.

Scientists on Tuesday said that El Niño – predicted to be the most intense on record – should be classified as a global health emergency which could kill hundreds of thousands.

“The 2026-27 El Niño is going to cause pandemic-scale mortality,” said Dr Colin Carlson, an assistant professor of epidemiology at Yale University, warning it would be deadlier than the 2009 swine flu pandemic, which killed roughly 284,000 people.

He urged the World Health Organisation to classify the “discrete and deadly event” as a public health emergency of international concern, on par with the Ebola epidemic in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the circulation of polio.

“An early analysis suggests that nearly half a million deaths will result from extreme heat alone, to say nothing of epidemics of dengue fever, cholera, and other infectious diseases that could also occur in tandem,” Dr Carlson said, in an article in Think Global Health with Prof Christopher Callahan, a climate scientist at Indiana University.

A naturally occurring weather phenomenon intensified by climate change, El Niño, which occurs every few years, usually raises temperatures across the world by warming the surface of the Pacific Ocean.

The oceanic heatwave can trigger erratic storms, severe droughts and intense wildfires – which can in turn wreak havoc on crop harvests, food supplies and global security.

Modellers have offered a snapshot of how deadly this could prove. Taking heat-related fatalities alone, El Niño could cause as many as 451,000 excess deaths between June 2026 and February 2027, according to research by the University of Chicago’s Climate Impact Lab.

The study, which has not yet been peer-reviewed, suggested the number of extremely hot days would rise by 44 per cent compared with normal years.

Nigeria is expected to suffer the most heat-related deaths, an estimated 31,400 between September and February, followed by Indonesia and Sudan.

“We have long been able to predict the impacts of temperatures on crops, and now we can predict their impact on the worst outcome – death,” Michael Greenstone, a report author and co-founder of the Climate Impact Lab, told CNN.

“This is such a large number that it can be numbing, but it is made up of parents and children, grandparents and neighbours, people going to work, caring for families, and living their lives,” he added.

Concerns about the fallout from El Niño rose as world leaders, climate scientists and policymakers met at the COP31 climate conference in Turkey this week, where the World Meteorological Organisation (WMO) unveiled its latest State of the Climate update.

It found the past four years had been the warmest on record, and warned El Niño would unleash “even more heat and extreme weather well into 2027”.

Celeste Saulo, the WMO secretary-general, said: “El Niño and indeed the state of the global climate in 2026 gives new meaning to the phrase ‘off the charts’.

“El Niño doesn’t add new heat to the planet. It releases heat from the ocean, which is [currently] the warmest ever measured. There is simply more heat available to release. We are supercharging our weather and El Niño will be a risk multiplier.”

In a speech on Monday, Simon Stiell, the United Nations climate chief, warned that the climate crisis, turbocharged by a super El Niño, was “a global health crisis of pandemic proportions”.
