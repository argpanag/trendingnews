Judge orders Albertsons to pay $900 million in Washington opioid case

SEATTLE — A King County Superior Court judge ordered Albertsons to pay $900 million, plus fees and costs, after ruling the company failed to prevent opioid misuse and diversion in Washington, the state attorney general’s office said Thursday.

Judge Janet Helson found Albertsons violated the law by failing to adequately investigate and document warning signs that opioid prescriptions could be misused or diverted, the office said.

From 2006 to 2022, Albertsons pharmacies filled opioid prescriptions more than 6.5 million times despite red flags, without documenting efforts to ensure the prescriptions were for legitimate medical purposes, according to the attorney general’s office.

Albertsons owns more than 200 Washington pharmacies under the Albertsons, Safeway, and Haggen names.

The state’s case argued that the company’s policies and practices contributed to the opioid crisis. The office said Albertsons dispensed more than 641 million opioid pills in Washington during that period.

During the trial, the AG’s office exposed Albertsons’ culpability:

  • At trial, the AG’s office showed that between 2006 and 2022 these pharmacies unlawfully dispensed opioid orders without adequately investigating and documenting the resolution of red flags. In total, Albertsons pharmacies unlawfully filled red-flagged prescriptions for opioids more than 6.5 million times without documenting efforts to ensure that they were written for legitimate medical purposes despite the presence of such red flags. As a result, significant volumes of unlawful opioid prescriptions were filled and likely diverted into illicit use in Washington State.
  • During the trial, the AG’s office presented evidence that Albertsons’ corporate policies prioritized profits over patient and customer safety. Albertsons discouraged its pharmacists from identifying problematic over-prescribers and prescriptions, and did little to review its own data or industry data to identify them. In some cases, Albertsons did not allow pharmacists to turn away prescriptions prescribed by known “pill mill” prescribers, even though competing pharmacy chains had blocked them. Pharmacies received complaints about prescribers who overprescribed opioids, and Albertsons had access to data to identify risky stores, orders, prescribers, and prescriptions. Instead, Albertsons kept its stores and pharmacists siloed so they couldn't share information.
  • Albertsons also failed to track its controlled substances inventory for theft closely. In one case, a Safeway employee stole more than 70,000 opioid pills from a pharmacy in North Bend over the course of two years before finally being caught; other thefts included 12,000 pills from a Leavenworth pharmacy and 19,000 pills from an Olympia location.
  • Albertsons chronically understaffed stores, leading to overworked and overwhelmed pharmacy staff. During the trial, the state presented emails from Albertsons pharmacists asserting that, given how overworked they were, it was “impossible” to review the legitimacy of prescriptions before filling them. Albertsons even refused to let its pharmacy staff at some Washington stores take lunch breaks until recent years.
  • Albertsons’ undermanned pharmacists and pharmacy managers described to their superiors the physical and mental toll the work was taking on them, including complaints of exhaustion, vomiting “due to exhaustion, dehydration, and hunger,” of “breaking down in tears in the back,” and of feeling like they were “all stretched like rubber bands that are about to snap.” Under these conditions, as one Albertsons’ pharmacist warned the company, “mistakes can easily be made to put someone’s life at risk.”
  • Albertsons also continued to accept discount cards as payments for controlled substances well after other chains had abandoned the practice because the cards could be used by patients or prescribers to avoid scrutiny from insurers. Albertsons internal documents acknowledged that users were coming to their pharmacies as “refugees” from other chains that would not allow them to use discount cards.
  • During the trial, the state called dozens of witnesses who testified that Albertsons’ policies and practices contributed to the opioid crisis and to discuss the scope and impacts of the opioid epidemic in Washington. These included state employees and community members who have dedicated years of their lives working on the front lines of the crisis.

“This verdict can’t undo the devastating harms of the opioid crisis, but it will provide desperately needed resources to support treatment and prevent future crises,” Attorney General Nick Brown said.

The attorney general’s office said more than 26,000 Washingtonians have died from opioid overdoses since 1999.

The state first sued several major pharmacy chains in 2022; Albertsons was the last remaining defendant after others settled or declared bankruptcy.

Albertsons said it disagrees with the ruling and is reviewing the decision and provided he following statement:

“Albertsons Companies is disappointed by the court’s ruling and respectfully disagrees with its conclusions. We are reviewing the decision closely and will evaluate all available options, including appeal. We are proud of our pharmacists and their dedication to the patients and communities they serve, and we will continue to support our customers and communities across Washington through responsible pharmacy operations.”
