The women behind the rebellion: Whitney Peak on her pivotal 'Hunger Games' role

TheTools · 20.11.2026 18:58 · source · permalink

The actress and co-star Joseph Zada spoke with theGrio about the power of believing in change, the women who inspire revolution and why it's cool to care.

Whitney Peak almost missed her chance to become part of “The Hunger Games” universe.

The actress, who stars as Lenore Dove Baird in “The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping,” admits she was initially intimidated by the audition requirements, particularly one that called for singing.

“I think I was just scared,” Peak told theGrio. “The audition said, ‘Must be able to sing,’ and I have no training whatsoever.”

With Rachel Zegler’s performance as Lucy Gray Baird in “The Hunger Games: The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes” fresh in her mind, Peak wasn’t confident she was up for the challenge.

“I was picturing Lucy Gray. I was picturing Rachel Zegler,” she recalled. “It’s big shoes to fill.”

Fortunately, her team wasn’t willing to let her pass up the opportunity.

“They were like, ‘What are you doing? Get in front of a camera and tape it,'” Peak said.

It’s fitting that Peak ultimately landed the role of Lenore Dove, a young woman whose unwavering belief in the possibility of a better world helps inspire one of the most consequential rebellions in Panem’s history.

Set 24 years before the events of the original “Hunger Games” films, “Sunrise on the Reaping” follows a teenage Haymitch Abernathy, played by Joseph Zada, as he’s selected to compete in the 50th Hunger Games. Audiences already know Haymitch as the cynical, battle-scarred mentor portrayed by Woody Harrelson, but this installment explores the experiences that transformed him.

Among the most influential people in his life is Lenore Dove, his girlfriend, who refuses to accept that the Capitol’s brutal annual competition must continue forever.

While Haymitch considers the Games as an unavoidable reality, Lenore imagines a future without them. Her convictions help plant the seeds of resistance that will eventually grow into a revolution.

For Peak, the character represents something larger than a romantic relationship.

“I think it’s really special, and it’s so beautiful,” she told theGrio, reflecting on the female characters who help drive the franchise’s revolutionary narrative.

“Just the female characters that are leading this revolution,” she continued, emphasizing the importance of hope and believing in the possibility of change.

“You have to have hope and things are possible, and you do deserve to be in certain places,” Peak said. “It can just be you that believes that. But just believing in yourself is sometimes more than enough.”

That philosophy is central to Lenore Dove’s influence on Haymitch.

Zada believes the character does more than provide Haymitch with something to fight for. She helps him recognize that resistance is possible in the first place.

“Opening his eyes to it in the first place,” Zada explained when asked about Lenore’s importance to Haymitch’s journey. “Her rebellion sparks that in him, and is in the end, what sparks Katniss to do what she did, and motivates District 13 and gets to what people in all of the districts are feeling.”

It’s worth noting that while Katniss Everdeen ultimately becomes the face of the revolution decades later, the movement is rooted in the courage and convictions of people who came before her.

Lenore Dove’s connection to that history runs particularly deep. A descendant of the Covey, the traveling musical community introduced in “The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes,” she carries forward a legacy of independence and resistance to the Capitol’s authority.

While The Hunger Games franchise is known for its elaborate arenas, deadly competitions and political strife, its appeal also lies in the ordinary people who decide they no longer have to accept the world they’ve inherited. That idea is at the heart of “Sunrise on the Reaping” and its call to “break the machine.”

For Peak, breaking the machine means refusing to conform and embracing what makes you different.

“Go against the grain,” she said. “Just be unapologetically who you are.”

But she also believes resistance requires something that can sometimes seem unfashionable: caring deeply.

“I think it’s so cool and it’s hot to care,” Peak said. “Whatever you’re passionate about, just stand on it.”

It’s a message that resonates beyond the fictional world of Panem, where the most powerful acts of rebellion often begin with someone daring to imagine that things could be different.

“The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping” opens in theaters November 20.
