The Blame Episode 1 Recap & Ending Explained: Who Killed Sophie Madsen?

TheTools · 20.09.2026 09:55 · source · permalink

Michelle Keegan and Douglas Booth star in ITV’s twisty new crime thriller

Back in 2017, an American study revealed that 56 per cent of people spent more time with their “work family” than their own, actual family at home. There’s a separate article to be had here about any company that refers to its employees as part of a “family”.

Almost 10 years later, and with Covid and working from home presumably reducing that statistic somewhat, there are still some professions where that figure has increased. Which brings us on to ITV’s latest crime thriller, The Blame, starring Michelle Keegan and Douglas Booth as a pair of detective inspectors tasked with solving crime in the fictional town of Wakestead.

At first glance, The Blame looks more like workplace drama, such as Billions, Severance or The Office (without the humour), than a police procedural. We’re introduced to the characters of flirty co-workers DI Emma Crane (Keegan) and DI Tom Radley (Booth), who Crane seems to be pining over, on a night out at the pub with their colleagues. The men are drunk, leery and lecherous towards the barmaid, which doesn’t bode well for the general public when these are the serving members of the police force. But even Radley gets in on the act, sauntering up to the bar and getting her number. Sure, he might not be sitting with them in their pub booth, but he still plays his part in their sexist banter, while Crane looks on unimpressed.

Back in the office the next day, the crap chat continues, as her co-workers fire around images and jokes on their phones that, for some reason, never make it to Crane’s mobile. But there’s work to do, and the team mobilises when they are told that a 16-year-old girl, Sophie Madsen, has gone missing.

While the team launches a manhunt, the viewer is shown old social-media footage of Sophie, a figure skater. Is this the most incongruous use of Robyn’s “Dancing On My Own”? Yes, I’m afraid it is. I do get what they’re doing, interspersing social-media footage of Sophie to show who she was when she was alive, but then turning the song into a haunted echo of itself as they discover her body in a rainy woodland forest? Incredibly jarring. And then they find her body.

We’ll soon find out if this is a point in the plot, but Sophie’s parents seem remarkably composed and only just a little bit teary when Crane and Radley inform them that their daughter has just been murdered. A crime this horrific, inflicted on a child you had birthed, loved and brought up, would leave most parents destroyed, falling to the ground and howling with grief for literally years afterwards. Here, a tissue for some tears and a polite smile seem to be as far as the characters can get. Come on, Crane, surely that’s some red flags right there?

Anyway, it turns out that Sophie had got into an argument with her skating coach and was kicked off the team, so we’ve got our first suspect right there. Things get darker when Crane reveals that one of the force’s officers bumped into Sophie, upset, in a car with an older man, the skating coach, but failed to do anything further. Oh, apart from, as Crane puts it: “It sounds like you could have been there for her at a crucial moment, but instead you went online, found a picture of her, printed it off, and drew a dick on it.” No employee of the month award for this guy.

“The whole department doesn’t give a shit about women,” Crane later complains to Radley, who’s told there’s nothing they can do about it. “Keep our heads low, clear our cases and get promoted” is his five-year plan, but Crane looks like she has other ideas. There’s a fire in her belly now.

A brief interview with Coach Frasier (Joe Armstrong) reveals nothing, other than Radley having to play his questioning line like a slimeball. But then Sophie’s friend Chloe calls Crane to tell her that not only did Sophie make a complaint about the coach to the police, but they recorded it, and did nothing. “We don’t record victim statements,” gasps Crane, and off she heads down a new institutional rabbit hole.

Sure enough, she finds the video in which Sophie, after saying the coach had “touched” her in his car without her consent, is told that “we get a lot of girls coming to report things… that they were making up”, victim blamed, while someone holding the camera chuckles. She’s then forced to act out what he did to her.

Crane wants to whistleblow, but Radley tells her not to: “This shit is systemic in every single station I’ve worked in again and again.” A clumsy pass from Crane, which Radley declines, and it’s just about time to call it a night on a truly terrible day.

However, the next day the coach has changed his tune. He’s now admitting to “touching her a bit”, claiming that “she wanted it… at first.” Things get worse when Crane manages to sneak a look at her boss’s phone and finds what sounds like the world’s worst WhatsApp group. It’s full of photos of women who have come into the station to make statements, alongside the men laughing at them and making rape jokes. She’s rightly appalled, especially when it’s so normalised alongside other conversations about football or coffee orders. It’s a crushing moment for Crane to realise that these men she works with, her force “family”, are dangerously misogynistic, and she wonders just how many desperate women looking for help they have failed over the years. The blinkers for her workplace have fallen off, revealing it as totally toxic.

Equally toxic is Radley, who admits to being a “coward” in the office when these sorts of chats happen, but also goes out of his way to convince Crane not to pursue some kind of exposé. “You don’t know me at all!” he says. Red alert. And again: “I don’t care about doing the right thing, not more than losing my job.” This is “urgent exit required” territory now, Crane, but then he weaponises her crush on him, and they start humping each other in an alleyway.

This guy is a wrong-un, and the morning after, following Frasier’s alibi collapsing and his arrest for the murder, Crane receives the dash-cam footage she was after from the lorry on the day of Sophie’s murder. And there, plain as day, is Sophie, getting into Radley’s car. All told, yet another reason why workplace crushes should never be acted on.

The Blame continues on ITV and ITVX.
