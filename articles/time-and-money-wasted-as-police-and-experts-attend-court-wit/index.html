'Time and money wasted' as police and experts attend court without testifying

TheTools · 20.11.2026 02:07 · source · permalink

Police officers were "routinely" being taken away from other work to attend court hearings, the CJI found

  • Published
    19 November 2026

Large numbers of witnesses required to attend court are not called to give evidence - including police officers who have been pulled from front-line duty, a report has found.

Criminal Justice Inspection Northern Ireland (CJI) said the reasons included last-minute guilty pleas by defendants.

It said evidence "pointed to substantial levels of wasted time and costs to the public purse".

In 2024-25, the hours attributed to police attendance at court equated to almost 18,500 average working days, or £4m.

But CJI discovered that in many cases officers were not required to testify.

Its acting chief inspector James Corrigan said it was "concerning" that they were "routinely" taken away from other work in order to attend.

"The criminal justice system in Northern Ireland - it's a very slow system. In fact, it's one of the slowest systems in the neighbouring jurisdictions," he told the BBC's Good Morning Ulster programme.

"It's not just the responsibility of any one organisation. The delays within the system start at the investigation," he added.

"They continue when the Public Prosecution Service need to make a decision; and then those delays are repeated in the courts, particularly in terms of adjournments."

CJI reviewed 30 prosecution cases and discovered only 34% of hearings went ahead as scheduled, with plea changes and the availability of lawyers among the contributory factors.

It also surveyed witnesses.

Sixty percent of police officers and forensic scientists said they testified "rarely", and 55% of members of the public said they did not have to take the witness stand.

'Loss of trust in justice system'

Corrigan said: "When cases do not go ahead as scheduled, witnesses may feel frustrated and anxious.

"They may have taken time off work and arranged childcare and travel to attend court, only to be told at short notice they are no longer required.

"This can cause a loss of trust in the criminal justice system."

He said the true scale of the "inefficiency" was difficult to estimate as data collection was limited.

The report recommended that the Police Service of Northern Ireland (PSNI) gets a "strategic grip" on officer attendance and establishes the impact on resources.

The estimate of £4m to cover the annual cost of 18,500 PSNI court attendances was not a reflection of the full costs to the public purse, according to Corrigan.

He explained the CJI's estimate did not take account of PSNI shifts having to be "back-filled" in order to cover the officers who have to attend court.

"It doesn't account for all the Public Prosecution Service staff that's in a court for a case that doesn't go ahead.

"It doesn't take account of the defence witnesses; the expert witnesses," he added.

James Corrigan has called for a greater use of technology for witness appearances in court

CJI also said the PSNI and the Public Prosecution Service (PPS) should implement a system to notify officers when they are no longer required in court.

"This should be done inside the next six months," said Corrigan.

He added: "Criminal justice organisations should consider electronic and digital methods of serving summonsing witnesses, as used elsewhere in the United Kingdom.

"This move to increased use of technology is long overdue."

Corrigan also told the programme that "remote evidence centres" could be used more often to allow some witnesses to testify from locations outside court.

"It's particularly important in terms of vulnerable witnesses who may need special support and may be intimidated by the court atmosphere," he explained.

"I think there's also opportunity for more police to be able to give evidence remotely from police stations... and then when they're freed up they can go back to their normal duties".

The PSNI it was considering the report's findings.

In a statement, Assistant Chief Constable Anthony McNally said: "Police officers have an essential role in bringing offenders to justice and giving evidence is a core part of that responsibility.

"However, unnecessary or late-cancelled court attendances can affect our ability to deploy officers to front-line and other operational duties.

"We are committed to ensuring that officers are available to give evidence when required, while reducing avoidable attendance and disruption for all witnesses."

'A really anxious time'

Speaking to BBC News NI, Janice Bunting, chief executive of Victim Support NI, said it supports witnesses in crown and magistrates' courts.

"At the outset I would say that no witness going to court should feel there isn't support there available for them - there is, it's through Victim Support," Bunting said.

She said being called to court can be a "really anxious time for witnesses".

"It is not something people do on an everyday basis, so the court still holds a wee bit of mystery for them. So they are very anxious whenever cases are coming up," Bunting said.

She added that the anxiety can be "exacerbated if there is short notice cancellation to their case".

"If you can image someone who has prepared for their case coming up in the Crown Court in a very difficult situation - they have gathered themselves, they have taken time off work, prepared their family and then that case is cancelled or adjourned at the very last moment," Bunting said.

"They just feel so anxious, so disempowered, so disappointed and then they know they have to do that all over again."
