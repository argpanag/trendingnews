Donald Trump’s Repudiation, America’s Revenge in the Midterms

TheTools · 04.11.2026 18:06 · source · permalink

Yes, we get it. He’s not going to go quietly. He’s not going to accept blame. Trump in defeat is still Trump. “I shouldn’t feel guilty,” he told Bret Baier, of Fox News. “It’s just a point in time.”

But the scale of Tuesday night’s reckoning defies even his ability to lie, dissemble, and generally squirm his way out of it. It is really hard to steal an election when voters all across America reject you. These were the President’s own voters, in states where he won by huge margins just a couple of years ago. By Wednesday morning, J. D. Vance admitted it wasn’t that Republicans failed to show up but that they did. “The main problem is that many people who voted for Trump/Vance in 2024 voted for Democrats in 2026,” he wrote on social media. Reality, if only temporarily, burst the White House bubble. Even Trump and his cronies know that Iowa and Kansas and Michigan and North Carolina and South Carolina and Texas did not all conspire together to rig the outcome. This righteous blue wave was made in red America.

Whatever he may say later—and I have no doubt he will spend years pretending that what just happened somehow did not just happen—Trump knows it, too. Even as the scale of the disaster for him was still becoming clear, he told the Times that he was “absolutely willing” to accept the results that he’d spent the past two years preparing to contest.

For now and for forever, the midterm election of 2026 will be known as one of the most decisive rejections of an American President since long before Richard Nixon got on that helicopter in 1974. Republicans losing both the House by thirty-five or more seats and the Senate by as many as eight seats—which now seems like a very real possibility—has happened only once since the Second World War: in 1958. And that campaign wasn’t anything close to the 2026 referendum on the dangerous words, disruptive actions, and bizarre personal preferences of the man in the White House.

Trump’s insistence on making the election so singularly about himself was not a surprise, but it was an error right out of a Greek tragedy—an epic self-own by the Narcissus of the National Mall that could be read only as a direct disavowal of the President. Republicans in Washington these past two years failed spectacularly when it came to defending the nation from the depredations of their party’s leader, but Republican voters around the country—enough of them, at least—admitted their mistake and did something big to rectify it. As the MAGA pundits on Fox News struggled for words to explain the calamity that they had not wanted to admit was coming, there was much clarity to be found in the viral clips of regular Republican voters interviewed at the polls, clarity and the clearest possible sense of moral revulsion: they did not want Trump, his policies, or his party anymore.

Was it because of the Iran war, as many here in Washington are already starting to argue? (“The reason we lost is the war,” Vance wrote on X. “The president himself said so last night.”) Maybe, but I think not only that. Results like this don’t happen just because diesel is pushing seven dollars a gallon at the pump. The scale of this is so large because the scale of Trump’s offenses has been so vast. Tariffs and trade wars, Canada-bashing, Europe-bashing, the betrayal of Ukraine and embrace of Vladimir Putin, the grotesque self-enrichment of himself and his circle, the unleashing of an armed militia of ICE goons on our streets. People voted for Alex Pretti and Lorenzo Salgado Araujo. They voted against data centers and Elon Musk buying elections. They voted against the cost of everything being too damn high. They voted because so much of what is happening in Trump’s America seems so very un-American.

Trump’s own role in this cannot be discounted. I know that structural factors are often considered the most important ones by political scientists. They will perform their regression analyses and examine the economic data and conclude that the results were probably baked in months ago. But, still, I hope they study the President’s last month of campaigning, too, an orgy of foolishness and mean-spirited lies that included twenty-one rallies in twenty-one days, where he talked about Jon Husted’s ass, the glories of his golden ballroom, and the algae in the Reflecting Pool while dismissing and demeaning voters’ concerns about practically everything. He said that affordability was a Democratic hoax and that the war he started and has failed to win wasn’t really a war at all and that the other guys were so awful that they would allow men to play in women’s sports. It speaks to the utter debasement of those around the President that they allowed him to keep saying these things—and to the terrifying emptiness of Trump’s soul that he thought these were winning sentiments at all.

As the magnitude of the election began to hit on Tuesday, I started to keep track of all the different words being used to describe it: blue wave, blue tsunami, blue-nami, bloodbath, extinction-level event, flood, landslide, obliteration, once in a generation, once in a lifetime, rebuke, rejection, repudiation. As early as 9:58 P.M., John King announced on CNN, clearly, unequivocally, “This is the collapse of the President’s party, on live television.” He was right. This was more clear than Trump’s 2018 midterm defeat, more clear even than his ouster in 2020, amid COVID and Trump-created chaos—which is why it doesn’t matter what you call it.

By Wednesday morning, the White House was reduced to sending out pictures of their guy behind the Resolute desk, under the caption, “PSA: TRUMP IS STILL YOUR PRESIDENT.” He was glaring. If it seemed like a threat, I’m sure that was the intent. But so what? That’s a tomorrow problem. As Senator Jon Ossoff, of Georgia, said in a victory speech that might have been the opening lines of the 2028 Presidential campaign, “The first light of dawn is on the horizon.”

Whatever happens next, the American people have finally and definitively told Donald Trump that enough is enough. ♦
