By HIKARI MARUYAMA/ Staff Writer

September 12, 2026 at 07:00 JST

Tadao Kikumoto, a former president of Roland Corp., shows off the drum machine TR-808 on July 14 in Hamamatsu, as the product, whose development was led by Kikumoto, is still fully taken advantage of worldwide even 46 years following its release. (Hikari Maruyama)

HAMAMATSU—The production of the famed Roland TR-808 Rhythm Composer ended just two years after its launch in 1980 primarily due to poor sales.

However, despite its unfortunate start, the electronic instrument from Japan continues to captivate musicians the world over these days.

The drum machine, commonly known as the 808 and manufactured by Roland Corp., is specifically designed to allow users to produce 16 types of sounds, including drums, handclaps and cowbells.

The developer of the TR-808 tried to replicate sounds of acoustic instruments. Unique sounds created that way won over many fans and contributed to the advent of new musical genres, particularly hip-hop and techno.

The TR-808 has similarly been utilized for hit pop songs.

Tadao Kikumoto, 84, a former president of Roland who led the development of the epoch-making machine, referred to the TR-808 as “so lambasted that I myself once forgot about its existence for a long time.”

In a recent interview, Kikumoto spoke about “a happy accident where people took advantage of the TR-808 in an attempt to create new genres of music,” among other episodes surrounding the product’s birth.

Kikumoto worked at Roland between 1977 and 2009, holding a number of positions, inclusive of president and senior managing director responsible for the technological department.

He is likewise famed for having spearheaded the project to make the TR-909 legendary model, released in 1983.

Kikumoto shared exhaustive details about not only how he had felt when learning that musicians overseas were using the 808 in creative ways despite the termination of its production in 1982 but also his thoughts on “sound creation,” “artistry” and “deep bass.”

Excerpts of the interview follow:

MERELY ‘HAPPY ACCIDENT’

Question: Could you explain how the 808 came into being, given that X, formerly Twitter, is flooded with posts every year on Aug. 8 that show users’ memories with the electric instrument with the hashtag “#808day”?

Kikumoto: Ikutaro Kakehashi, the founder and then president of Roland, went on a business trip to North America in the latter half of the 1970s and became aware that there was a huge market opportunity if we could pitch a rhythm machine capable of generating realistic sounds for $1,000 (158,000 yen) or less.

He stressed that such a product would make it possible to finish recording tunes without hiring drummers at music production studios and thereby save on labor costs, leading to good sales for sure.

At the time, a rumor was circulating that the rhythm machine Linn LM-1 Drum Computer would shortly be marketed in the United States that sampled the actual sounds of percussion instruments. It was expected to carry a price tag of around $5,000.

Kakehashi asked us to make something similar at a far cheaper price, but an estimate quickly revealed that making a similar product on our own would cost $10,000 per unit. Such a thing could not be released under Roland.

PRIDE OF SYNTHESIZER MANUFACTURER

We therefore gave a second thought to the project. Taking into account that the LM-1 could only produce sounds based on the recordings of actual instruments, we determined that Roland, as a synthesizer maker, needed to develop a rhythm machine with which users could create their own sounds freely.

This idea afterward won official approval within the company anyhow.

However, doing so turned out to be much more difficult than initially predicted. Though we could not reproduce realistic, lifelike tones in the end, the 808 arrived on the market in 1980.

Q: How did you create sounds for the model?

A: An analog method was employed. First, we analyzed the waveforms of instrument sounds and human handclaps with an oscilloscope and created the sounds by combining mainly sine waves, square waves and noise with the assistance of the large modular synthesizer System-700.

Analog electronic circuits were built into the TR-808 in a bid to reproduce the sounds created this way.

HANDCLAPS HIGHLY REGARDED

Q: What sounds do you think were particularly well designed?

A: Whereas handclap sounds were not realistic, I found them somewhat intriguing and actually saw them receive good feedback from people around us shortly following the launch of the 808.

The reproduced handclaps were crisper and drier than real ones. The 808 was specifically designed to generate a pattering sound four times to resemble the sound of many people clapping simultaneously, while a sound similar to white noise (hissing noise) was likewise adopted to create a bang-like reverberation.

Q: What is your take on the fact that the handclapping sounds were effectively utilized by Marvin Gaye (1939-1984), one of the greatest soul musicians, for his worldwide hit tune “Sexual Healing” in 1982?

A: They were used for the 16 measures in the intro of a version of YMO (Yellow Magic Orchestra)’s “Thousand Knives” (included on the album BGM from 1981) as well.

BASS DRUM SOUNDS FIRST POORLY RECEIVED, LATER ACCEPTED

Q: What about the other sounds?

A: The bass drum sounds were poorly received, in particular. No matter how hard we worked, we could not render them realistic and ran out of time. However, they have eventually been accepted as “ideal” sounds that many people can love.

Q: How do you view the globally well-known fact that the bass drum sounds from the 808 were employed by the U.S. musician Afrika Bambaataa (1957-2026), one of the pioneers of hip-hop culture who incorporated electronic music into the genre, for his major hit “Planet Rock” in 1982?

A: When played through a large speaker, the 808 produces an extraordinarily deep, banging bass sound.

Drums typically used on concert stages at that time had a more explosive attack (the onset of sound). Whereas sounds from the 808 were completely different from those of actual instruments, they likely proved rather ideal for dance music.

DEEP BASS TO RESONATE THROUGH BODY

I realized the true power of this deep bass five years ago for the first time. I heard it played through a large speaker and felt it resonate all the way into my abdomen. I felt that this may be the kind of sound humans inherently seek.

In addition, I have heard a story about a devout Christian who described this sort of sound as “a sound of the devil.” The reasoning is that listening to the deep bass on a dance floor was sacrilegious, because it resonates like a pipe organ in a church. This anecdote made me aware that deep bass can also be a religious mode of sound.

Q: Why did you add the “decay” knob to the tone-changing functions of the 808, so that the bass drum sound could be prolonged and rendered far more different from its authentic counterpart?

A: My hope then was to allow users to be able to alter the sounds by themselves, since the 808 was developed as a synthesizer above all.

While some insiders argued that “you should stop generating unrealistic, embarrassing sounds” and that “it is not good for a machine created by an instrument maker to produce such strange sounds,” I decided to incorporate the function in the end on the grounds that those who did not like it did not have to use it.

Surprisingly, musicians began actively utilizing the feature.

They did not merely prolong the sounds; they recorded and processed the 808’s sounds, giving them pitches to serve as basslines. We had never imagined that the feature would be taken advantage of in that way. This development was nothing more than a happy accident.

It should be noted, however, that Kakehashi and I believed that the true mission of an electronic musical instrument manufacturer is to offer customers the possibility of creating sounds freely. Anyway, a synthesizer is a machine for pursuing “novel sounds.”

I also know a musician who continuously turned the knobs while performing toward creating a groove typical of human-adjusted tunes.

Q: What is your opinion about the unique cowbell sounds more recently used in Pikotaro’s “Pen-Pineapple-Apple-Pen” to attract worldwide attention in 2016?

A: Both the cowbell and rimshot (drum-edge-striking) sounds could produce nothing but boring effects. They used simple circuits. But now that they have repeatedly been used, they have come to serve as something of “standards” for cowbell and rimshot sounds.

Q: Do you think the 808 is beloved so much only thanks to its specialized auditory appeal?

A: I think that it was appreciated first and foremost because it enabled people to easily create their own favorite rhythm patterns.

Even by randomly pressing the 16 buttons arranged in a row from left to right, unexpected rhythm patterns that human drummers could never play are produced. I believe it all started when Arthur Baker (a U.S. music producer) and Afrika Bambaataa found our product interesting and gave it a try.

Those moving to give rise to new genres of music were likely delighted to rely on the 808 additionally, as it generated sounds they had never heard before.

But the product did not sell well in any case, with its production promptly discontinued.

COMPLETELY FORGOT ABOUT THE 808

Q: Will you talk of when its production was terminated after just two years, with a total output of 12,000 units?

A: The era of digital synthesizers was arriving. Yamaha Corp. released the digital synthesizer DX7 in 1983, and we changed course to explore the same technology.

I completely forgot about the 808. Although I heard at the beginning of the 1990s that it was being used by hip-hop and other musicians outside Japan, the news was unbelievable to me. I could not believe that an instrument that had been criticized so harshly was being utilized in the underground music scenes of Europe and the United States.

UNBELIEVABLE TURN OF EVENTS

Q: Did you check how the 808 was used at the time?

A: Nope. I did not check it out soon. I did not want to. I simply thought why on earth were they resorting to something like that.

Q: Why, first of all, did you take interest in synthesizers designed to electronically combine sounds?

A: I cannot play a musical instrument but have been very curious about sounds since I was a child. I was born and raised in the city of Osaka and started building amateur radio and audio equipment myself after entering a technical school.

After graduation, I was engaged in the development and production of transistors at a company called Kobe Kogyo. At the beginning of the 1970s, I worked on creating dedicated systems based on Intel’s latest microprocessors that could control research devices at universities as well as converters at steel mills.

INSPIRED BY ISAO TOMITA’S ‘CLAIR DE LUNE’

One day in those days, I encountered on the FM radio the (1974) performance of Isao Tomita in which he played “Clair de Lune” by Debussy on a synthesizer. I had no interest in electronic music whatsoever before, because the genre was then home to solely avant-garde tunes using unusual sounds, like beeps and hisses.

But Tomita performed a classical music tune familiar to a lot of people on a synthesizer. Though I knew nothing at all about synthesizers at the time, I was really impressed that such amazing sounds could be created.

Q: What happened to you next following the dramatic encounter with Tomita, who is famed for not only his pioneering works in electronic music but his activities in both Japan and abroad, including his Grammy Award nomination?

A: Searching for information, I discovered that a synthesizer seemed possible to make from transistors and ICs (integrated circuits). With this in mind, I developed a synthesizer that should be played with a guitar and founded a start-up firm on my own.

But its specialized structure turned out to be impossible to play in reality. I eventually ran out of funds and went to present my brainchild to Roland, whose headquarters were situated in the city of Osaka at the time. This ultimately led to me joining Roland as an expert in transistors and computers.

Q: What did you develop first with the new employer?

A: I developed the DR-55, a lower-priced edition of the CR-78 rhythm machine marketed by Roland, in 1978. Whereas conventional rhythm machines could only play patterns recorded in the bodies, it (CR-78) was “programmable” in that users could play patterns of their own choice.

CREATING SOUNDS AS PAINFUL EXPERIENCE

Q: Could you retrace your commitment to sound creation not only in the process of the experience in your earlier days ending up resulting in the completion of the 808 but in the development of its successor, the TR-909, and the highly admired TB-303 model, which is considered indispensable for the bass sound of house music?

A: To me, creating sounds is always painful. Reaching the goal in the process is not easy. Continuing pursuing a particular sound, I occasionally find good sounds along the way unexpectedly. Isn’t this true of every form of art? No artists, in my opinion, can release their works after becoming completely content with them.

Musical instruments are often described as an “art form of compromise.” When pressing the keys for high notes on a piano, you can hear a clicking noise. But this sort of noise is likely not what their designers intended to create.

Meanwhile, this sound is nowadays embraced as the standard. If the noise were replaced by a more accurate one, I think it would probably be impossible to play Chopin’s music today.

TURN TO REVIVAL OF BASS

Q: What does the 808 mean personally to you at present?

A: Watching firsthand the true impact of deep bass on the 808 made me aware of how significant it is to music. We are now living in the age of AI (artificial intelligence). Products and services that are more entertaining than music will emerge en masse from here on out.

But we can still have a far richer musical experience, if we can enjoy baroque music or dance music on commuter trains as though we were in churches or on dance floors.

Acting on this thought, I have recently made a device myself that allows users with earphones to listen to music through their entire bodies, with a dedicated component worn around the abdomen to convey vibrations in synchronization with the rhythm.

I would like music equipment manufacturers to consider products like this in the future.

I will be more strongly pushing for the necessity of a “bass renaissance” to reviving the attraction of low-pitched tones. The 808 got me to turn my attention to this concept.
