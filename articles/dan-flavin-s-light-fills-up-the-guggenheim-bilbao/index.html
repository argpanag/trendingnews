Dan Flavin’s light fills up the Guggenheim Bilbao

TheTools · 20.11.2026 14:48 · source · permalink

20

20 Nov 2026

Until April 4th, 2027, Dan Flavin’s fluorescent tubes encounter Frank Gehry’s architecture, distorting perspectives to the point of hallucination at the Guggenheim Bilbao. Gathering over twenty works by the master, from his early white compositions to his monumental installations, the exhibition showcases the full extent of his exceptional work — to turn light into an experience that can transform architecture and the way we see it.

Text and photos by Thibaut Wychowanok.

Dan Flavin, the master of light

At the Guggenheim Bilbao, Dan Flavin’s (1933–1996) fluorescent tubes trigger a strange hallucination in the viewer’s eyes. The retina, saturated with green first, then with yellow and finally red, suddenly projects new colours onto the surrounding space. The artwork has disappeared from our field of vision, yet it continues to exert its influence on our retina, on our mind, on the space itself.

The exhibition Algo De Luz brings together over twenty works dating from the 1960s to the 1980s, drawn from the New York Guggenheim’s Panza Collection and the Dia Art Foundation, which has loaned seven of Flavin’s “monuments” to Tatlin. Some of his best-known masterpieces.

Following the exhibition devoted to Dan Flavin at the Kunstmuseum Basel last year, what more could the great master of light have to tell us? The answer lies in the confrontation with the spaces of Frank Gehry’s Guggenheim – vast galleries that are far from being plunged into darkness, where natural light filters through.

Dan Flavin’s experimental spirit

In a few surprising, lesser-known works, Dan Flavin showcases his experimental spirit. In Bilbao, even when overcast, the sky plays a role in the relationships between different shades of white, modulating the intensity of the halos and bringing the changing conditions outside into the heart of the museum, stripped off of any theatricality.

Each work sheds light on its instability and dependence on a particular environment. The museum no longer merely welcomes the pieces, but actively participates in shaping our sensory experience.

The radical nature of the artist’s approach took shape in New York. In 1961, Flavin was working as a guard at the Museum of Natural History. A former seminarian who had served in the Air Force, he drew, wrote poetry and explored the possibilities of electric light.

Space as an integral part of the composition

Two years later, he mounted a fluorescent tube diagonally on the wall of his studio. The material was industrial, its dimensions and colours taken straight from a catalogue. Yet, the artist discovered a “gaseous image, floating and implacable”. That sentence captures the full ambiguity of that apparition — an almost immaterial presence, yet one capable of taking over an entire space.

In the early white works, that power was achieved with very little. The nominal three (to William of Ockham) from 1963 arranged the tubes in groups of one, then two, then three. The intervals gave rhythm to the wall, the light spilled beyond it, and the space itself became an integral part of the composition.

Elsewhere, the meeting of warm white and cool white blurs the sharpness of a corner. The architecture loses its apparent solidity. Flavin establishes his own visual grammar using prefabricated elements, without any display of virtuosity or rare materials. What matters now unfolds between the object, the space and the visitor’s body. The artist speaks of a “situation”: the work extends beyond what could simply be taken down from the wall. It can also block the way.

Modern life enters the museum

Greens crossing greens (to Piet Mondrian who lacked green) from 1966 was his first barrier. Uprights and crossbars formed a frontal structure, with the tubes covered in plastic to soften their glare. In Bilbao, there is no way to reach the other side. Colour spreads freely throughout the room. Meanwhile, visitors are kept at a distance.

The desire to move closer comes up against a physical barrier, while the saturation of the retina produces a disconcerting afterimage. The experience engages the body to the very limits of its perception. Beauty is far from harmless. The dedication to Mondrian also introduces a touch of irreverence. The master of abstraction had excluded green from his most famous compositions. Flavin gives him an entire room of it.

The supposed purity of minimalism makes room here for less elevated references. The combinations of pink and yellow also owe something to Brooklyn’s Chinese restaurants. In the works dedicated to his gallerist Margo Leavin, those same colours evoke the garishness of Los Angeles. Abstraction remains permeated by commercial imagery, popular décor and their sometimes kitsch appeal. The bad taste of modern life enters the museum.

The intimate geography of Dan Flavin’s works

Behind the rigour of the arrangements, an intimate geography comes to the surface. The titles evoke friends, art dealers and admired artists. In 1992, Flavin married the artist Tracy Harris in the rotunda of New York’s Guggenheim, beside the light installation he had just created there. Thirty-five years earlier, he had worked in the museum’s mailroom. The place where his career began became the setting of his wedding. Does the coldness of industrial materials really conceal intimacy?

Finally, the seven “monuments” to Tatlin evoke the 400-metre tower project designed for the Third International, which never went past the model stage. Their white silhouettes also recall the skyscrapers in New York.

Flavin preserves the spirit of modernist utopianism, while exposing its fragility. The lamps wear out, and the survival of the work depends on their replacement. The quotation marks around the word “monument” take on their full meaning. The promise of eternity is countered by an electrical assembly whose light will eventually fade out.

“Dan Flavin: A Little Light”, exhibition until April 4th, 2027, at the Guggenheim Bilbao, 48009 Bilbao, Bizkaia, Spain.
