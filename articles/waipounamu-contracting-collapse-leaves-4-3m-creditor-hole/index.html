Waipounamu Contracting collapse leaves $4.3m creditor hole

TheTools · 24.10.2026 00:29 · source · permalink

Waipounamu Contracting ran for more than two decades out of Riversdale in northern Southland. It did not fail because work dried up or a big project went bad. It failed because the one person legally running it was gone, and nothing was in place to carry on without him.

The agricultural contractor was placed into liquidation on 29 September by special resolution of shareholders, after co-owner and sole director Mark Sanson died in September aged 60. It owes creditors $9.927 million, with an estimated deficit of $4.259 million. Creditors, the ODT reports, have little hope of seeing their money.

This is a personal tragedy for a well-known Southland family. It is also a governance lesson that a lot of New Zealand business owners should take personally.

One signature, no fallback

The decision to liquidate followed discussions between the company’s accountant, solicitor and Sanson’s wife, who is executor and trustee of the family trust. Nothing in the public record points to a co-director, a documented succession plan or a funded buy-out arrangement that could have kept the machinery moving.

That is not unusual. Thousands of New Zealand SMEs are structured exactly this way: one director, a family trust holding the shares, and an operating model that lives in one person’s head and phone. It works right up until it doesn’t. A contracting business with seasonal commitments, plant finance and a payroll cannot pause for months while an estate is sorted out.

Note the figures carefully. Some coverage headlined the debt as $4.2 million, but that is the shortfall. The gross amount owed is closer to $10 million, and the gap between the two is what creditors stand to lose.

Unsecured suppliers stand at the back

Liquidator Iain Nellies of Insolvency Management Dunedin says he will review the records to establish whether any loans or current accounts are owed to the company by its director or shareholders, and will recover these “where economic”. That is standard practice and occasionally fruitful. It rarely closes a multimillion-dollar hole.

The creditor waterfall is unforgiving. Staff entitlements and certain tax debts rank first as preferential claims, secured lenders next, and unsecured trade creditors last. Fuel suppliers, parts dealers, mechanics and subcontractors in a rural district are almost always in that last group.

Recent regional failures show how this plays out. When Southland builder Prospec Structures collapsed with a shortfall of just over $4 million, liquidators reported in 2024 that 26 unsecured creditors were owed a combined $2.4 million, behind $1.58 million in secured debt and preferential claims including staff wages. Tiny-home maker Podular went down in late 2022 owing almost $10 million with a predicted shortfall of $9.7 million, and in 2024 the Court of Appeal found even buyers of part-built units did not jump the queue. Earlier this year, Teak Construction’s first liquidators’ report showed $7.9 million in creditor claims against $6.4 million in assets.

The pattern is old. Back in 2017, Challenge Steel’s collapse left 42 unsecured creditors owed $6.1 million while its first secured creditor was owed $4.8 million.

The backdrop was already ugly

Waipounamu’s failure has a specific trigger, but it lands in a market that had been grinding down for a while. BWA Insolvency recorded 933 insolvencies in the fourth quarter of 2025, up 31.5% on a year earlier, with liquidations up 33.8%. In that February 2026 report, Bryan Williams of BWA Insolvency warned that “a rising tide will benefit many businesses but it will not reverse the trajectory for those that are already insolvent”, and noted fewer firms were attempting formal rescue through voluntary administration. The Insolvency and Trustee Service publishes official monthly liquidation data for anyone wanting to track their own sector.

For suppliers, that means more customers carrying thin margins and old debt, and less time to spot trouble before it becomes a write-off.

What owners should fix this year

There is nothing here that demands new regulation. It demands owners doing the unglamorous work themselves.

If you run a sole-director company, appoint an alternate or second director who can legally act. Document who holds the keys to bank accounts, contracts and plant finance. Price key-person insurance and a buy-sell agreement funded by it, so a partner or family can take over or exit cleanly rather than liquidate. Make sure your will and trust deed actually speak to the business.

If you supply others, assume your customer could be one bad week from this outcome. Register security interests on goods you supply on credit, tighten terms on accounts that are stretching, and know where you would rank if the call came.

Waipounamu’s creditors will learn over the coming months how much, if anything, the liquidator can claw back. The rest of us can learn now, for free. A business that cannot survive its owner’s absence was never as solid as its trading history suggested, and it is everyone downstream who pays for finding out.
