Trump hosts Xi for US-China summit in Washington | CNN Politics

TheTools · 24.09.2026 12:01 · source · permalink

Where things stand

• US President Donald Trump, Chinese leader Xi Jinping and their spouses will gather for a tea session on Friday. They will also visit the National Archives in Washington, DC on the final day of Xi’s US state visit.

• On Thursday, Trump hosted a state dinner at the White House. Top US tech executives, including Nvidia’s Jensen Huang and Tesla CEO Elon Musk, were seen at the event.

• This is Xi and Trump’s second summit this year and the Chinese leader’s first visit to Washington in more than a decade as they try to manage the world’s most consequential bilateral relationship.

• The two leaders underlined the importance of discussions on AI. Other issues including trade, Taiwan and the war in Iran also loom large. However, deep mistrust on both sides will likely hamper any significant breakthroughs, experts said.

We have paused our live coverage of Xi’s state visit and will be back to cover Friday’s developments.

A bald eagle sculpture and US tech execs: Key moments from the Trump-Xi state dinner

US President Donald Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping lavished each other with praise at a White House state dinner after their landmark meeting on Thursday.

It was Xi’s first trip to Washington in over a decade, and the Chinese leader will wrap up the trip on Friday with tea at the White House and a visit to the National Archives.

Here are some of the key moments from the dinner:

  • US-China relationship: In their speeches, the two leaders spoke favorably about their relationship. Xi invoked George Washington’s interest in collecting Chinese porcelain, and recalled former US President Richard Nixon’s historic visit to China in 1972. “Together let us nurture the beautiful flowers of Sino-US friendship. Together let us write a new chapter in our friendly relations,” he said.
  • Celebrating differences: During his remarks, Trump celebrated the “foundation of commerce and mutual respect” between the US and China, adding that “President Xi and I both understand that we represent different systems, but the ties between our people endure.” Xi, meanwhile, said Trump’s “Make America Great Again” agenda could stand alongside his aim for China’s “great national rejuvenation.”
  • Technology a key focus: Four top American tech executives could be seen sitting with Trump and Xi at the head table during the state dinner, including Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, AMD CEO Lisa Su, Tesla’s Elon Musk and former Apple CEO Tim Cook. Other heads of top artificial intelligence and tech companies were also on the guest list, as both leaders underscored the importance of managing AI responsibly during their meeting.
  • A very American gift: Trump presented Xi with a bald eagle statue during the state dinner. “The bald eagle is a national symbol of the United States and an embodiment of the free and soaring spirit of America,” Trump said.
  • Reporters denied access: The White House barred two CNN journalists from covering Xi’s arrival for the state dinner, after trying to tell the network which type of journalists could cover the event. MS NOW journalists were also denied access to cover the arrival.

In May, Trump took a bevy of US CEOs to Beijing. Xi didn’t do the same in Washington

When US President Donald Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping held their landmark Beijing meeting in May, Trump brought with him a delegation of top American tech executives, including Tesla’s Elon Musk and then-Apple CEO Tim Cook.

But when Xi visited the United States this week, no Chinese CEOs showed up with him.

Earlier this month, Reuters reported that Xi was planning to have a large business delegation accompany him. The South China Morning Post also reported that executives from Chinese electric vehicle giant BYD, smartphone maker Xiaomi and battery powerhouse CATL were among those expected to accompany him.

But Chinese CEOs were conspicuously absent at Thursday night’s White House banquet.

Cui Hongjian, an international relations scholar with Beijing Foreign Studies University, told CNN that Beijing may have felt it wasn’t the right time for a prominent display of Chinese CEOs.

“Right now, both sides are still in a phase where they want to stabilize the political situation a bit more,” said Cui.

“Once both sides actually achieve some substantive breakthroughs in their economic and trade negotiations, I think it would be more appropriate to see prominent displays of cooperation at the CEO level or in the economic and trade sphere at that time.”

Wu Xinbo, a Chinese Foreign Ministry adviser, told CNN that logistical challenges also hampered plans to send a delegation. He also pointed to concerns in Beijing that some of Trump’s team were less positive about US-China relations than the US president.

“That is a major reason that the Chinese side didn’t send that economic and trade delegation over,” he said.

CNN’s John Liu contributed reporting.

“Thucydides trap” and “win-win”: Xi’s rhetoric mirrors the May summit

During his state visit to the US, Chinese leader Xi Jinping echoed some of the buzzwords and phrases he used at the previous summit with President Donald Trump in May, signaling continuity in his messaging and Beijing’s ongoing concerns.

Under Chinese diplomatic and political norms, public speeches by officials are crafted with predictability and they rarely surprise. Here are some key phrases Xi repeated during his time in Washington:

  • An era of “constructive strategic stability”: Announced during the May summit, Xi sees this concept as the new guiding principle for the bilateral relationship, one marked by more cooperation, less volatility and managed competition.
  • Transcend the “Thucydides trap”: Xi again refers to the political science concept based on Greek history, which argues that when a rising power challenges an incumbent one, it often ends in military conflict. He sought to convince Trump that China and the US need not be caught in a war.
  • “Win-win” and “cooperation benefits both sides; conflict hurts both sides”: Xi has long argued that China and the US could both benefit through cooperation, while Beijing has repeatedly accused Washington of harboring a “Cold War mentality” of a “zero-sum game,” which prevents such kinds of mutually beneficial arrangements.
  • “Lengthen the list of cooperation, shorten the list of issues”: On US-China economic and trade relations, Xi reiterated this phrase to show that cooperation could achieve stability in important areas of concern.
  • Handle the Taiwan issue with caution: The sovereignty of democratic, self-governed Taiwan remains a major sticking point between the two superpowers, and it has long been a red line for Beijing.

Invoking Washington and Nixon, Xi recalls times of warmer US-China ties

During his speech at the White House state dinner, Chinese leader Xi Jinping invoked moments of US-China exchange and cooperation from the 250-year span of American history.

After beginning by noting George Washington’s interest in collecting Chinese porcelain, he turned to cooperation between Chinese and American forces against Japan during World War II, observing a “friendship forged in blood and fire.”

Xi then recalled former US President Richard Nixon’s historic visit to China in 1972. The handshake between Nixon and China’s first leader Mao Zedong marked the start of the normalization of US-China relations.

“Like a river that never stops flowing, history never stands still,” said Xi, before calling for cooperation in the future.

At the end of the speech, Xi said US President Donald Trump’s “Make America Great Again” agenda could stand alongside Xi’s aim for China’s “great national rejuvenation.”

He said “China and the United States must act as responsible major countries, meet the expectations of our peoples, keep pace with the trend of our times, and explore a new approach for major countries to get along with each other.”

Trump invites state dinner guests to view White House ballroom construction

President Donald Trump invited guests at tonight’s state dinner with Chinese leader Xi Jinping to view the construction of the new ballroom at the White House, adding that if it was ready, more people would have been able to attend.

“As a special treat in honor of President Xi and Madam Peng, I’d like to invite everyone in the room to visit through that beautiful curtain in the back, the door, it’s all lit up and looks beautiful,” Trump said in the East Room. “I can tell you that tonight, if we had it, we would have had way more than 1,000 people with standing room only, and this room has about 145.”

“They’ve been looking for this for over 150 years, all presidents, and they will shortly have it,” Trump said, adding that the ballroom is “one year away from completion.”

Trump suggested that guests view the construction after the entertainment concludes.

“Anybody wants to walk into a construction site that’s nice and clean and sparkling, it’s our honor to have you do so,” Trump said.

CNN has previously reported that a highly sophisticated secure bunker is being installed beneath the ballroom, and it’s unclear if that would be visible in the current construction.

Yesterday, Trump told reporters that he wished the ballroom was completed in time for Xi’s state dinner.

During his remarks at the dinner, Trump also celebrated the “foundation of commerce and mutual respect” between the US and China, adding that “President Xi and I both understand that we represent different systems, but the ties between our people endure.”

“Americans and Chinese have always exchanged goods, knowledge, customs, and ideas. This has been the foundation of commerce and mutual respect between our countries, and together we can continue to build a relationship that promotes prosperity and security for future generations,” Trump said.

Why Taiwan has long been a sticking point in China's relations with the US

When it comes to US-China relations, there are few issues more thorny than the ultimate future of Taiwan.

During his meeting with US President Donald Trump at the White House this morning, Chinese leader Xi Jinping said he hopes the US will oppose Taiwan’s independence, according to Chinese state media.

Here are some key things to remember.

  • Taiwan is a democratic, self-governing island of 23 million residents.
  • Taiwan has its own military, currency, constitution and democratically elected government. It controls its own borders and issues its own passports. But as of 2026, it is only recognized diplomatically by an ever-diminishing number of small nations and is not a member of the UN.
  • China’s ruling Chinese Communist Party (CCP) views Taiwan as part of its territory, despite having never controlled it — and has long vowed to “reunify” the island with the Chinese mainland, by force if necessary.
  • The US formally switched diplomatic recognition from Taipei to Beijing in 1979 — but has long trod a delicate middle path. Under the “One China” policy, the US acknowledges China’s position that Taiwan is part of China but has never officially recognized the Communist Party’s claim to the island.
  • Washington maintains robust unofficial relations with Taiwan and is bound by law (under the Taiwan Relations Act) to provide Taiwan with defensive weapons. But the US has remained intentionally ambiguous on whether it would intervene militarily in the event of a Chinese attack — a policy known as “strategic ambiguity.”
  • Under Xi, China has become increasingly assertive in foreign policy and grown more authoritarian at home. Its crackdown in Hong Kong has further alienated many people in Taiwan, who fear they could face the same fate were they to be under Beijing’s rule.
  • Tensions between Washington and Beijing have pulled Taipei closer into the orbit of the US, especially under several Taiwan presidents from an independence-leaning party. This has infuriated Beijing, spurring it to apply more pressure on Taiwan and sending cross-strait relations on a downward spiral.

Xi told Trump he hopes US will oppose Taiwan independence, Chinese state media reports

Chinese leader Xi Jinping said during a bilateral meeting with US President Donald Trump that he hopes the US will oppose Taiwan independence, according to Chinese state media.

“China hopes that the United States will uphold the correct position of opposing ‘Taiwan independence,’” state news agency Xinhua reported Xi as saying, adding that China hopes the US handles the “Taiwan question with prudence.”

The US has long held the position that it does not support Taiwan independence. Under what is known as the “one China policy,” the US recognizes the People’s Republic as the sole legitimate government of China and it also acknowledges Beijing’s position that Taiwan is part of China. However, the US has never accepted the Chinese Communist Party’s claim of sovereignty over the island.

Trump and Xi also discussed the US-Iran war during the bilateral meeting, according to Xinhua.

Xi said that China would support the US and Iran returning to the 14-point memorandum of understanding reached in June and that he hoped the two countries would resume negotiations as soon as possible. He also said he hoped they would ultimately come to an agreement on the nuclear issue, Xinhua reported.

The US is Taiwan’s most important arms supplier and security guarantor. China’s government has consistently opposed arms sales to the island democracy, which Beijing claims as part of its territory, despite having never controlled it.

Xi still holds an ace in his deck: rare earths

China’s dominance over the processing of rare earth materials remains a powerful card for leader Xi Jinping during ongoing talks at the White House — and one that Beijing has quietly reminded Washington it holds in recent months.

China accounts for roughly 90% of the global processing of rare earths and their magnets, which are essential for everything from F-35 fighter jets and cars to iPhones. And Beijing did not shy away from leveraging that dominance during last year’s trade war between the world’s two largest economies.

At the height of the US-China trade war in April last year, Beijing imposed a licensing regime on seven types of rare earths and their magnets, restricting their exports. It drastically stepped up controls in October 2025 but postponed the measures for a year as part of its trade truce with the US sealed in South Korea. That truce was due to expire, but was extended until January on Wednesday.

But despite Beijing’s commitment to address Washington’s concerns about rare earth exports following the Trump-Xi summit in May, shipments are now falling at the fastest pace in volume terms since the pandemic, according to a research note by Oxford Economics.

“Beijing appears willing to test the limits of the truce. It has deliberately kept license approvals slow and selective,” Nguyen Hoan Nam, the research firm’s China economist, said in the report earlier this month.

The critical minerals are expected to feature prominently again in the Washington talks between Xi and US President Donald Trump.

“Washington could point to the drop in rare earth exports as evidence of China not holding up its end of the deal,” Nguyen said.
