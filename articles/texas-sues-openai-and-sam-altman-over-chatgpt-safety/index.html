Texas sues OpenAI and Sam Altman over ChatGPT safety

TheTools · 29.10.2026 01:37 · source · permalink

Texas has sued OpenAI and Sam Altman under consumer and child protection laws, seeking to bar minors from ChatGPT and require court-approved model reviews.

On 23 October 2026, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton filed a lawsuit against OpenAI and its Chief Executive Sam Altman, accusing the company of deceiving the public over the safety of ChatGPT and failing to protect underage users. Filed under the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices, Consumer Protection Act and the state Securing Children Online through Parental Empowerment Act, the legal action requests a state court to bar minors from accessing ChatGPT, mandate independent safety approvals before any new artificial intelligence models are deployed in Texas, and impose civil penalties of up to $10,000 per violation.

The complaint alleges that OpenAI abandoned its founding non-for-profit mission to pursue rapid commercial growth, deploying conversational software without adequate age verification or parental consent controls. Texas state prosecutors argue that the free version of ChatGPT operates without age-assurance mechanisms, while the paid version collects unverified self-reported ages and offers parental alerts that function only in limited scenarios. The petition links unsupervised chatbot reliance to acute real-world harms, citing instances where conversational systems reinforced self-harm ideation or facilitated violent planning, alongside technical incidents where unreleased autonomous agents engaged in unexpected external interactions.

The state seeks injunctions compelling verified age checks, verifiable parental authorisation for underage users, and mandatory submission of all future foundation models to court-approved independent evaluators prior to commercial release. As reported by Politico, OpenAI spokesperson Jason Deutrom stated that governments hold an important role in establishing robust safety standards and affirmed that the company is committed to working with Texas and other states on pragmatic rules that apply to the entire artificial intelligence industry rather than individual providers.

Why does it matter?

Between June and October 2026, US states and a Canadian province turned to the courts to demand stronger safety protections from AI chatbot companies. Florida was first, suing OpenAI and Sam Altman on 1 June 2026 over alleged harm to children. In September, the Province of British Columbia sued OpenAI in a US federal court, claiming the company failed to warn police about the Tumbler Ridge shooter after its own reviewers had flagged the account. On 14 October 2026, eight states filed separate lawsuits alleging that ChatGPT harms young users. On the same day, attorneys general from 39 states and the District of Columbia set child safety and privacy conditions for any sale of Character.AI in its bankruptcy case. Texas acted alone on 23 October 2026.

This litigation is taking place as federal legislation stalls. When President Trump dismissed AI risks as a hoax in September, Democrats and some Republicans in Congress called for guardrails but could not agree on a bill. According to a survey by the University of Maryland, public opinion favours regulation, with 85% of Democrats and 79% of Republicans supporting a new federal agency to legislate AI. In the absence of a federal law, the safety rules AI companies must follow for younger users in the United States are being set state by state.
