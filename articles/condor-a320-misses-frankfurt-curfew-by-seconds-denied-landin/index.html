Condor A320 Misses Frankfurt Curfew By Seconds, Denied Landing At 875 Feet

TheTools · 15.09.2026 06:14 · source · permalink

On September 11, Condor flight DE1809 missed the strict midnight curfew at Frankfurt Airport (FRA) by mere seconds, adding hours of travel time for its passengers. The plane was forced to abandon its landing at the very last moment and divert to Cologne after it missed the extended time limit beyond the routine 11:00 PM curfew.

One Mile At A Time noted that the jet took off on what was essentially an impossible mission from the get-go. The plane was delayed by more than two hours before departure and didn't even take off until 10:39 PM, 25 minutes after its scheduled pushback time of 10:15 PM.

Condor Rolls The Dice And Passengers Pay The Price

When it departed from Olbia Airport (OLB) in Italy, DE1809 had to arrive three minutes faster than its quickest transit time it has ever logged on public record to beat the cut-off. One Mile At A Time recounted that DE1809's best flight time is 1 hour and 24 minutes from FRA to OLB, as seen in public data from sources like FlightAware. When the jet departed on Friday night, there was only 1 hour and 21 minutes remaining before the extended FRA curfew expired.

Against all odds, the Airbus A320-214 was on final approach and descended all the way down to 875 feet when the clock struck midnight, according to Flightradar24. Despite being moments from landing, air traffic control could not clear the plane to land and it was forced to go-around. Ironically, pushing the throttles back up to full power and ascending to divert would cause a much greater sound disturbance than simply allowing the twinjet to touch down.

In this case, there is a clear conflict between the letter of the law and common sense, which portrays the curfew policy as excessively rigid. However, Condor basically dispatched this jet on a flight that was impossible by all measurable standards. It's more surprising that DE1809 got as close as it did before being turned away.

The Flyers Aboard DE1809 Bear The Burden

If Condor had canceled the flight in Olbia or postponed it until the next morning, the financial penalties would have been guaranteed under EU261. Condor would have been legally forced to pay for hotel rooms, ground transportation, and meals for up to 180+ passengers and the flight crew in Olbia. Additionally, leaving the plane in Italy would mean it would not be in Frankfurt the next morning. This would trigger a domino effect, causing cancellations or severe delays for several other flights.

Still, from an airline cost and operational analysis perspective, dispatching Condor flight DE1809 despite the delay seems like a massive blunder. When Condor’s dispatch team chose to let the plane take off from Olbia, they were gambling on a very slim chance of success. Considering these factors, it certainly paints an unflattering portrait of the carrier's priorities.

Condor flight DE1809 illustrates how weakened EU passenger laws incentivize risky corporate gambles. Faced with a delayed flight, Condor chose to fly right against Frankfurt’s midnight curfew. If successful, the airline avoided payouts; if they failed, the penalty was no worse than canceling. Thus, 180 flyers were stuck at Cologne Airport (CGN) until the early hours of the morning sorting out ground transport to get home from the wrong airfield.

Closing The Distance: The Silver Lining Of A Near Miss

It is entirely possible to portray the decision-making made by the Condor dispatch team in a cynical manner, and there's enough evidence in this case to craft a bleak characterization of corporate callousness. However, the counterpoint is that the travelers were at least delivered to an airfield much closer to home, which also benefits customers financially, because the compensation schedule frequently does not match real-world out-of-pocket costs.

If the airline had canceled the flight and left everyone in Italy, the passengers would have been stuck paying for overpriced hotels and expensive food in a tourist town. Because the new laws make it hard to get that money back from the airline, most families would have lost hundreds of euros out of their own pockets.

By pushing through to Germany, even with the miserable middle-of-the-night bus ride, the passengers got back to their normal lives. They didn't have to waste their savings on extra vacation costs. Yes, passengers can legally recoup out-of-pocket costs that exceed their compensation, but the process could easily be a frustrating headache. All in all, DE1809 neatly encapsulated the ongoing struggle between passenger protection and competitive airline policies in just one sortie.
