    15:52 BST

    Jamie Whitehead
    Live reporter

    Navi Pillay was awarded the prestigious Nobel Peace Prize this morning, unbeknownst to her.

    The Norwegian Nobel Committee had been unable to reach Pillay who was eventually called away from her desk to be told she'd won the prestigious award. Pillay said she thought it "must be a joke".

    After being assured it must be true as "it's on the BBC", by someone stood off camera, Pillay went on to make an impromptu speech in front of her friends and colleagues.

    In a more formal news conference later, Pillay dedicated her win to "all the survivors of international crime".

    The Nobel committee said she had been awarded the prize "for her efforts to promote peace and international law".

    In announcing the award in Oslo earlier, the committee's chair, Jørgen Watne Frydnes, said Pillay had a firm moral compass and that she had led the way to a more comprehensive legal order.

    Pillay, 85, was the first non-white woman to be appointed a High Court Judge in South Africa and went on told hold positions in the United Nations and International Criminal Court.

    She is currently currently a judge on the International Court of Justice in the case where Myanmar stands accused of genocide.

    The panel's report, led by Pillay, concluded that members of Hamas "deliberately killed, injured, mistreated, took hostages..." during the 7 October attacks in 2023.

    "These actions constitute war crimes and violations and abuses of IHL [International Humanitarian Law] and IHRL [International Humanitarian Law]," it said.

    Pillay's team said this was because the Israeli military's use of "heavy weapons with large destructive capacity in densely populated areas constitutes an intentional and direct attack on the civilian population".

    A woman of Indian Tamil heritage, Navi Pillay was born into apartheid South Africa and dedicated the first part of her career to fighting that system.

    She defended anti-apartheid activists and helped secure rights for political prisoners, including Nelson Mandela.

    Pillay also confronted apartheid personally. As a non-white woman, she said no law firm would employ her, so in 1967 she opened her own practice, becoming the first woman to do so in Natal Province.

    She later served on the International Criminal Court and as the UN’s human rights chief. Recently, she chaired a UN commission that concluded Israel had committed genocide in Gaza, a finding Israel strongly rejected.

    The Nobel Committee praised her lifelong efforts to advance peace and international law.

  12. Nobel committee mounts explicit defence of international lawpublished at 13:01 BST
    13:01 BST

    James Landale
    Diplomatic correspondent

    By honouring Navi Pillay, the Nobel committee is mounting an explicit defence of the post-WWII global order. International law, it says, is "under tremendous pressure", its institutions "under attack".

    There is a "shift towards power politics at the expense of legal frameworks".

    But it insists nonetheless that "more and more conflicts are being addressed through diplomacy, treaties and legal mechanisms" and that "international law is a foundation for a more peaceful world".

    But there are many people in Ukraine, Iran, Gaza, Sudan, Myanmar and elsewhere who might disagree.

    They might argue they have been failed by international law and treaties, and that their interests and security are better served instead by force of arms and the support of powerful allies.

    As the late Sir Alex Younger, former UK MI6 chief, told the BBC last year: "We are in a new era where international relations are not going to be determined by rules and multi-lateral organisations. They are going to be determined by strong men and deals."

    It is against that bleak conclusion the Nobel committee is railing by awarding its prize to someone who is a living embodiment of the other side of the argument.

  19. The fifth South African winner of the Nobel Peace Prizepublished at 11:38 BST
    11:38 BST

    Ed Habershon
    BBC News Africa

    Navi Pillay today became the fifth South African winner of the Nobel Peace Prize.

    Past winners of the award from South Africa include the former President Nelson Mandela, who became South Africa's first democratically elected leader in 1994 after spending 27 years in prison for his battles against apartheid.

    Mandela's predecessor FW De Klerk was also awarded the Peace Prize for his efforts to negotiate the end of apartheid, the releasing of political prisoners including Mandela, and in overseeing the transition to democracy.

    The pair shared the award in 1993.

    South Africa's other winners include the late Archbishop Desmond Tutu, who was awarded the prize in 1984 for his role in campaigning against apartheid and the promotion of human rights.

    The country's first Peace Prize winner was Albert Luthuli, an early leader of the anti-apartheid African National Congress.

    He was awarded the prize in 1961, becoming Africa's first Nobel Peace Prize laureate.

The fifth South African winner of the Nobel Peace Prizepublished at 11:38 BST
11:38 BST

Ed Habershon
BBC News Africa

Navi Pillay today became the fifth South African winner of the Nobel Peace Prize.

Past winners of the award from South Africa include the former President Nelson Mandela, who became South Africa's first democratically elected leader in 1994 after spending 27 years in prison for his battles against apartheid.

Mandela's predecessor FW De Klerk was also awarded the Peace Prize for his efforts to negotiate the end of apartheid, the releasing of political prisoners including Mandela, and in overseeing the transition to democracy.

The pair shared the award in 1993.

South Africa's other winners include the late Archbishop Desmond Tutu, who was awarded the prize in 1984 for his role in campaigning against apartheid and the promotion of human rights.

The country's first Peace Prize winner was Albert Luthuli, an early leader of the anti-apartheid African National Congress.

He was awarded the prize in 1961, becoming Africa's first Nobel Peace Prize laureate.
