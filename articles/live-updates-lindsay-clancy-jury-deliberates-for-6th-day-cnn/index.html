Live updates: Lindsay Clancy jury deliberates for 6th day | CNN

TheTools · 03.09.2026 12:02 · source · permalink

Where things stand

• No verdict yet: Jury deliberations have resumed for a sixth day in the Lindsay Clancy trial, after jurors told the judge on Tuesday and Wednesday that they could not come to a unanimous verdict.

• Possibility of mistrial: Yesterday, the judge read the Tuey-Rodriguez charge, a special, final instruction that asks them to consider the reasonableness of all opinions. If they are deadlocked again, it is likely the judge will declare a mistrial.

• About the case: The Massachusetts mother is accused of murder after strangling her three young children in 2023. Prosecutors have argued Clancy acted “intentionally, rationally, and swiftly” to kill her children, while the defense said she should not be held criminally responsible because she was suffering from postpartum psychosis.

Jury deliberations near 34 hours as court pauses for lunch

The jury in Lindsay Clancy’s murder trial has deliberated for about 33½ hours as deliberations entered a sixth day.

Court staff are on a lunch break from 1 to 2 p.m. ET, though the judge previously told jurors they could continue deliberating during the recess.

Jurors told the judge on Tuesday and Wednesday that they could not reach a unanimous verdict. If they remain deadlocked, the judge is likely to declare a mistrial.

Intense emotion in Clancy trial makes deadlock predictable, former prosecutor says

The deadlock in the triple-murder trial of Lindsay Clancy is predictable, given how emotional the details of the case are, one former prosecutor told CNN.

“You’ve got those three beautiful children who are no longer with us, and I think somebody – maybe more than one – they’re digging in on finding her guilty, coming back with a guilty verdict, because it’s just so emotional,” Margaret McLean, former prosecutor and host of “The Margaret McLean Show” on YouTube told CNN.

There are parents on the jury, McLean noted, adding that as a former prosecutor, she can’t imagine bringing a case like this one.

“I can imagine some are really dug in that she’s guilty, while others will be dug into Reddington’s concept that what loving mother could do this to these three beautiful young children? She must have been in severe psychosis,” McLean said, pointing to the argument made by Clancy’s defense attorney, Kevin Reddington.

The scene outside the courtroom as we wait

Journalists and spectators are anxiously waiting for any news in the hallway outside the courtroom in Plymouth, Massachusetts. People are squeezing together to fit as many people as possible on the few metal benches, but more than two dozen others have to sit on the tiled floor.

Many spectators are chatting about the case as they sit in circles on the ground, using their bunched-up rain jackets as makeshift cushions.

Others are more solitary. One woman wearing a leopard-print dress is reading a Tom Petty biography (she’s almost finished). Another is sitting on a collapsible stool she brought from home, scrolling on her phone.

When Clancy’s family or court staff leave the courtroom, they have to weave their way through the clusters of people on the ground.

One woman heaved a big sigh as she lowered herself into a newly available seat.

“I couldn’t sit on the floor anymore – I’m old,” she said jokingly.

Attorney says Clancy is receiving letters of support from around the world

Attorney Kevin Reddington brought a box of letters to court for Lindsay Clancy that he said were sent to his office by supporters from “all over the world.”

“She reads them all,” the attorney added.

Reddington handed the letters to Clancy’s parents in court.

A number of Clancy supporters attending her trial over the past few weeks told CNN they, too, were writing letters to Clancy. Some even discussed the possibility of visiting Clancy at Tewksbury Hospital, where she is being held.

Jurors appeared stoic before continuing deliberations

Jurors in Lindsay Clancy’s triple-murder trial filed into the courtroom this morning, their eyes trained on the ground as they walked.

One man stroked his beard, while another woman rocked on her heels as she stood. The jurors appeared stoic but were responsive to the judge, with many telling him “good morning” and responding audibly to his questions about whether they had been exposed to anything that might impact their ability to be impartial.

Before court came into session, about 20 people, primarily women, lined up for the public seating in the courtroom, some donning bright pink T-shirts and pink nails.

Lindsay Clancy’s defense attorney Kevin Reddington stopped to hug and greet a few of her supporters before entering the courtroom and sitting down next to his client. Clancy nodded as he spoke to her.

"Bring it," Clancy's lawyer says about possibility of second trial

Lindsay Clancy’s defense attorney Kevin Reddington arrived at court and was immediately surrounded by members of the press asking about the possibility of his client’s case being retried should a mistrial be declared.

“Bring it,” Reddington said when asked if he thought a second trial would favor the prosecution.

Reddington said he is prepared to go back to trial any time. “I got witnesses lined up. We’re all set. Dr. (Phillip) Resnick is ready. I’m ready. We’ve got all our evidence witnesses revealed,” he told reporters.

Judge likely to declare mistrial to avoid coercion if deadlock remains, lawyer says

If the jury in the Lindsay Clancy case returns with yet another note saying they remain deadlocked, Judge William Sullivan will most likely declare a mistrial to avoid becoming coercive toward the jurors, a criminal defense lawyer tells CNN.

“It makes people think ‘We’re never going to get out of here.’ And then people are not really voting their conscience, they’re voting just to get out of the jury room, and we never want that,” she said.

What could happen next if there's a mistrial

If the jury remains deadlocked and the judge declares a mistrial, what will happen to Lindsay Clancy?

If the judge declares a mistrial, the prosecution will most likely state their intent to retry the case and Clancy would be returned to Tewksbury Hospital where she would continue being held without bail on her murder charges, according to CNN’s Jean Casarez.

If the prosecution decides to retry the case — which Clancy’s attorney Kevin Reddington has indicated would be likely — Clancy would remain in custody until the next trial date, criminal defense attorney Elyse Hershon explained.

If the prosecution chooses not to retry the case, Clancy would be returned to the psychiatric facility until the Plymouth County District Attorney decides to drop the charges, at which point she would be released, Hershon said.

Clancy supporters consider possibility of a mistrial

Several supporters of Lindsay Clancy waiting in line to enter court are discussing the jury’s impasse and expressing concern about a possible mistrial.

“I feel like it’d be really hard for 12 people to all see it one way, I really do,” said Sandy Lucca, a Clancy supporter from nearby Bourne who has attended several days of the proceedings. “I’m sad that it’s deadlocked because what does that mean, we have to do this all over again?”

Lucca expressed concern over the emotional toll another trial would take on everyone, especially Clancy, saying it would “emotionally destroy her.”

It's not unheard of for juries to go from deadlock to verdict

The jury in Lindsay Clancy’s trial reported twice now they cannot come to a unanimous decision. If they tell the court they are deadlocked again, the judge could likely declare a mistrial.

But juries in other high-profile criminal cases have initially said they were deadlocked only to later come to a unanimous verdict.

In Sean “Diddy” Combs’ trial last year, for example, the jury initially said it was deadlocked on the top charge of racketeering. After deliberating further, the jury unanimously reached a not-guilty verdict on that charge, while convicting him of other charges.

In Daniel Penny’s 2024 trial for the killing of a subway rider, the jury twice told the court they could not come to a verdict on second-degree manslaughter. The judge then dismissed the charge at the request of prosecutors, and the jury subsequently reached a not-guilty verdict on a less serious charge of criminally negligent homicide.

In Harvey Weinstein’s 2020 sexual assault trial, the jury sent a note indicating they had reached a verdict on some charges but were deadlocked on others. But after further deliberations, the jury came to a unanimous verdict on each charge. (The guilty verdicts were later overturned on appeal.)

How deliberations in Lindsay Clancy's murder trial have unfolded so far

All eyes are on a Massachusetts courtroom today, as jurors work for sixth day to break an impasse and come to a unanimous verdict in the trial of Lindsay Clancy.

At the center of it all is Clancy, the former labor and delivery nurse charged with murder in the strangulation of her three children. Now paralyzed from a suicide attempt, the 36-year-old has pleaded not guilty to three counts of murder.

Her future hangs in the balance: Will the jury convict her of murder or manslaughter for the killings? Will they consider her mental health issues and find her not guilty by lack of criminal responsibility?

Or will they make no unanimous decision at all and a mistrial is declared?

Here’s a look back at how deliberations have unfolded so far:

Thursday, August 27: The 12-person jury begins deliberations at about midday and deliberates for about 3.5 hours.

Friday, August 28: The jury asks the court to see the prescription pill bottles and the knife, key physical evidence in the case. The jury deliberates for just under 7 hours before leaving for the weekend.

Monday, August 31: The jury deliberates for about 7 more hours.

Tuesday, September 1: The jury sends a note to the court saying they “are unable to come to a unanimous decision.” The judge instructs the jury to go back and deliberate further.

Wednesday, September 2: The jury sends another note that they are unable to reach a verdict. The judge read the Tuey-Rodriguez instructions to the jury and continued to deliberate until they went home for the day.

The judge read the Tuey-Rodriguez charge. Here's what that is

When a jury says it is hopelessly deadlocked, a judge may read aloud special instructions known as a “dynamite charge.” The idea is to blow up any impasse to avoid a hung jury and mistrial.

In Massachusetts, this is referred to as the Tuey-Rodriguez instructions and is known nationally as an Allen Charge. In Clancy’s trial, Judge William F. Sullivan read the Tuey-Rodriguez charge to the jury on Wednesday.

The instruction asks jurors to consider the reasonableness of their own opinions and others’ and to continue deliberating toward a verdict.

Another case: In 2024, the judge overseeing Karen Read’s first murder trial read aloud the Tuey-Rodriguez instructions to a deadlocked jury. After deliberating further, that jury still could not come to a verdict, and the judge declared a mistrial.

The jury has been deliberating for about 30 hours. Catch up on what happened yesterday

The 12 deliberating jurors have not been able to reach a unanimous verdict after nearly 30 hours of deliberations. Twice now, the jury has told the court they are deadlocked on Lindsay Clancy’s guilt. And twice the jurors were directed by the judge to continue deliberating and try to break the impasse.

Now they are back for their sixth day of deliberations. If the jury says it can’t reach a verdict again, the judge will likely declare a mistrial.

Here’s a recap of what happened yesterday to get you up to speed:

  • In the afternoon, the jury sent a note to the court: “After much deliberation we are still unable to come to a unanimous decision.” Jurors also sent a note saying they couldn’t come to a verdict Tuesday morning.
  • Most of the jurors were looking down as they walked into the courtroom. Several female jurors had their hands clasped in front of them.
  • Judge William F. Sullivan read the Tuey-Rodriguez instructions to the jury. This special instruction is known as a “dynamite charge” and is nationally called an Allen Charge. The idea is to blow up any impasse to avoid a hung jury and mistrial.
  • The instruction asks jurors to consider the reasonableness of their own opinions and others’ and states that it is “desirable that the case be decided.”
  • Sullivan then instructed jurors to keep deliberating, which they did until they went home at about 4 p.m. ET.
  • If the judge declares a mistrial, the prosecution will most likely state their intent to retry the case and Clancy would be returned to Tewksbury Hospital where she would continue being held without bail on her murder charges, CNN’s Jean Casarez explained.
