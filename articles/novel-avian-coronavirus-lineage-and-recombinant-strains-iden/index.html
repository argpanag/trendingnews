Novel Avian Coronavirus Lineage and Recombinant Strains Identified in

TheTools · 26.11.2026 02:59 · source · permalink

A comprehensive molecular surveillance study of commercial poultry flocks across Ecuador has identified the circulation of a previously unrecognized avian coronavirus lineage, formally proposed as GI-32, alongside multiple recombinant strains involving both novel field viruses and established vaccine lineages. Published in Frontiers in Veterinary Science by a multidisciplinary team from the Universidad de Las Américas, the University of São Paulo, and partnering research institutions, the investigation analyzed 600 individual clinical specimens collected from diseased broilers, layers, and breeders. Utilizing real-time reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction assays and complete nucleotide sequencing of the viral spike glycoprotein S1 subunit, the researchers detected widespread viral activity in both respiratory and gastrointestinal anatomical compartments. The phylogenetic resolution of 46 representative isolates demonstrated that Ecuadorian poultry production systems host a dynamic viral ecosystem, wherein the newly recognized GI-32 lineage co-circulates with classical GI-1 and GI-13 strains, generating inter-lineage genetic hybrids that exhibit distinct structural, evolutionary, and antigenic profiles.

Infectious bronchitis virus, an enveloped positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus possessing a genome of approximately 27 kilobases within the genus Gammacoronavirus and family Coronaviridae, remains one of the most critical infectious threats confronting global poultry production. The viral genome encodes replication-associated polyproteins ORF1a and ORF1b, accessory proteins, and structural envelope components, among which the S1 subunit of the spike glycoprotein serves as the primary antigenic determinant. Genetic variation within S1 governs host receptor engagement, tissue tropism, and cross-protective immunity between lineages. Standardized taxonomic frameworks have expanded from an initial six genotypes containing 27 lineages to nine recognized genotypes encompassing 31 established GI lineages worldwide. Across South America, historical surveillance has documented the ongoing circulation of lineages GI-1, GI-11, GI-13, GI-16, and GI-23. However, molecular information from Ecuador has historically remained scarce, relying primarily on partial S1 gene fragments that restricted the identification of divergent variants or complex genetic mosaicism emerging within immunized production flocks.

To investigate the epidemiological landscape of the pathogen, field veterinarians collected diagnostic specimens from flocks experiencing clinical disease, obtaining at least ten individual samples per commercial poultry house or farm. The final cohort comprised 400 tracheal swab samples gathered from birds displaying acute respiratory symptoms, such as coughing, sneezing, ocular tearing, conjunctivitis, tracheal rales, nasal discharge, and stretched necks during respiration. In parallel, 200 enteric tissue samples, consisting of individual one-square-centimeter sections of the jejunum, were harvested from chickens that suffered fatal gastrointestinal disease accompanied by severe lethargy, ruffled feathers, cloacal impaction, somnolence, and diarrhea. All samples were preserved individually at four degrees Celsius without pooling to maintain diagnostic granularity and transported under refrigerated conditions for molecular analysis. Field records indicated that commercial flocks adhered to conventional immunization strategies predominantly utilizing Massachusetts-type live and inactivated preparations, although complete flock-level vaccination histories could not be verified across every participating site.

Molecular screening targeting the conserved 5-prime untranslated region revealed substantial statistical divergence in detection frequency and viral copy concentration between anatomical collection sites. Tracheal swabs yielded an overall positivity rate of 90.5 percent, accounting for 362 positive detections among the 400 respiratory specimens, whereas jejunal tissues showed a 30.5 percent positivity rate, representing 61 positive detections among 200 enteric samples. Statistical analysis demonstrated that respiratory samples possessed significantly greater odds of testing positive relative to enteric specimens, with an odds ratio of 21.7 and a relative risk of 2.97. Absolute quantification against standard reference curves revealed mean viral concentrations of 4.9 times 10 to the fifth genomic copies per microliter of cDNA in respiratory samples, compared to 2.8 times 10 to the third genomic copies per microliter in enteric tissues, reaching maximum concentrations of 2.1 times 10 to the eighth and 7.6 times 10 to the sixth copies per microliter, respectively. Furthermore, targeted screening confirmed the presence of avian metapneumoviruses A and B in respiratory samples, while chicken astrovirus and avian nephritis virus were identified in enteric tissues, indicating that viral shedding occurred within a complex multifactorial disease environment.

To overcome the diagnostic resolution limits of partial amplifications, the researchers subjected successfully amplified S1 amplicons to dual sequencing platforms combining bidirectional Sanger dideoxy sequencing with Oxford Nanopore Technologies. Amplicons covering the complete 1,600-base-pair S1 gene were generated using Platinum Taq DNA polymerase and primer walking on an automated capillary genetic analyzer. Parallel high-throughput long-read sequencing was conducted on an Oxford Nanopore MinION Mk1C device using Flongle flow cells and the Rapid Barcoding Kit, followed by high-accuracy basecalling in Dorado version 7.6.7 and quality filtering at a minimum Phred score threshold of nine. Reads were aligned to the Beaudette reference genome within an Ubuntu computing environment to assemble consensus sequences. Following the exclusion of redundant amplicons showing 100 percent nucleotide identity with existing GenBank records or identical internal sequences, a curated dataset of 46 representative complete S1 sequences—comprising 31 enteric-derived and 15 respiratory-derived isolates—was established for downstream phylogenetic and evolutionary evaluations.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction based on the curated complete S1 dataset placed the 46 Ecuadorian sequences into three distinct lineages belonging to genotype GI. Six sequences, representing 13.04 percent of the sequenced cohort, clustered firmly within lineage GI-1, exhibiting close evolutionary proximity to historical reference strains such as IB438 and the classical H120 vaccine strain. A second group of 21 sequences, constituting 45.65 percent of the dataset, clustered within the internationally distributed GI-13 lineage, with 14 of these isolates aligning near the 4/91 commercial vaccine and European reference strains. Most notably, the remaining 19 sequences, representing 41.30 percent of the characterized dataset, formed a separate, robustly supported monophyletic clade. This clade exhibited an approximate likelihood ratio test node support value of 99.9 percent and comprised isolates collected from multiple independent sampling events across different calendar years, thereby fulfilling all formal phylogenetic criteria required to designate a novel taxonomic lineage, designated as GI-32.

Detailed comparative sequence identity analyses revealed that the newly recognized GI-32 lineage maintains an independent evolutionary architecture characterized by pronounced structural divergence from all previously recognized lineages. Within GI-32, pairwise nucleotide identity ranged between 95.24 and 100 percent, while deduced amino acid identity varied between 91.47 and 100 percent, demonstrating consistent intra-lineage genetic cohesion. In contrast, comparisons against external reference lineages revealed that GI-32 nucleotide identity dropped to between 55.31 and 83.76 percent, reflecting a nucleotide divergence of 16.24 to 44.69 percent. Deduced amino acid identity ranged from 46.44 to 82.84 percent, representing a translational divergence of 17.16 to 53.56 percent. These observed genetic distances exceed the conventional taxonomic rules of thumb established for avian coronavirus classification, which generally recognize lineage separation at approximately 13 percent nucleotide and 14 percent amino acid divergence. Comparative profiling across 200 reference sequences identified 16 highly divergent residue positions across the S1 protein, located in the signal peptide, the receptor-binding domain, and the C-terminal domain, separating the Ecuadorian GI-32 strains into six distinct amino acid polymorphism profiles designated P1 through P6.

Structural and selection pressure analyses demonstrated that the divergence of GI-32 is shaped by functional modifications within key viral protein domains. Predictive mapping identified 18 canonical N-linked glycosylation sites within the S1 protein of GI-32, with 17 sites strictly conserved across all 19 analyzed strains and an additional site at position N271 present in 73.68 percent of the isolates. Fourteen of these post-translational modification sites mapped directly to the receptor-binding domain and the proximal region of the C-terminal domain, presenting unique glycosylation combinations distinct from classical H120 and 4/91 vaccine backbones, including the specific acquisition of site N51 and the absence of glycosylation at position 461. Selection pressure models applied to non-recombinant GI-32 sequences revealed five amino acid positions experiencing episodic positive diversifying selection under mixed-effects models at residues 116, 120, 197, 230, and 232. Additionally, fixed-effects likelihood estimation confirmed pervasive positive selection operating at position 230. Crucially, all identified positively selected sites clustered exclusively within the receptor-binding domain, indicating targeted evolutionary adaptation within regions directly responsible for host cellular engagement.

Algorithmic recombination evaluations using RDP5 and confirmation across BootScan analyses in SimPlot++ demonstrated that genetic exchange actively shapes the Ecuadorian viral population, identifying three independent recombinant events that generated hybrid strains classified within lineage GI-13. The first event produced strain 638_UDLA through genetic exchange involving a GI-32 major parental backbone and a GI-13 minor parental donor, with a statistically confirmed breakpoint located at position 738. The second event generated a cluster of five recombinant field isolates—designated 607_UDLA, 78_UDLA, 76.1_UDLA, 76.2_UDLA, and 559_UDLA—originating from a GI-13 major parent and a GI-1 minor parental sequence, with breakpoint boundaries mapped between positions 424 and 680. The third event produced recombinant strain 747_UDLA, which integrated genetic segments from GI-13 and GI-11 parental lineages between breakpoints at positions 414 and 898. Exploratory selection analyses of the GI-13 lineage including these recombinant variants detected 14 apparent episodic selection signals, nine of which resided in the receptor-binding domain, illustrating how genetic recombination can rapidly remodel antigenic domains and mimic traditional evolutionary selection patterns.

Bayesian time-scaled phylodynamic reconstructions performed in BEAST under a relaxed molecular clock inferred an evolutionary substitution rate of 1.2 times 10 to the negative third substitutions per site per year, placing the time to the most recent common ancestor for lineage GI-32 at approximately 1985, within a 95 percent highest posterior density interval spanning 1952 to 2005. Phylogenetically, GI-32 shared a distant common ancestral node dated to approximately 1946 with lineage GI-30.1, previously identified in Trinidad and Tobago. The authors emphasized that several methodological limitations must be considered when interpreting these findings: the sampled population was restricted to clinically diseased birds rather than a random epidemiological cross-section, and the preferential sequencing of specimens with elevated viral titers introduces potential selection bias. Furthermore, the observational investigation lacked in vitro neutralization assays, experimental bird challenges, and full-genome sequencing. Consequently, while the detection of GI-32 and novel recombinants highlights decades of unmonitored viral divergence in South America, these genomic data establish lineage diversification rather than definitive proof of vaccine failure or altered clinical virulence.
