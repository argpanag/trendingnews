Integration Crisis Moves From Shock to Urgent Search for a New Roadmap - Sport for Business

TheTools · 10.09.2026 08:47 · source · permalink

Sep 10, 2026 | Camogie, GAA, Governance, Government, Ladies Football, News Freeview, Women in Sport

A turbulent 24 hours for Gaelic games moved on Wednesday from the shock of Dr Mary McAleese’s resignation to a debate about money, governance, equality and the timetable for bringing the GAA, LGFA and Camogie Association together.

By the end of the day, all three governing bodies had reaffirmed their commitment to integration, the Government had offered to help with the financial challenge, and attention had shifted decisively to the next Steering Group meeting and the need for a credible new timeline.

 

Tom Ryan on Morning Ireland

The day began with GAA Director General Tom Ryan making a rare national broadcast appearance on RTÉ Radio 1’s Morning Ireland.

Ryan acknowledged that McAleese’s departure was a setback and accepted that the Association had missed a deadline of its own making. His central message, however, was that the GAA remains committed to integration but was “just not ready” to deliver it on the previously planned timetable.

He also rejected McAleese’s suggestion in the strongest terms that resistance to having women involved in decision-making at the highest level was playing a role.

He explained that the work required to create a common membership across Gaelic games — covering membership systems, reciprocal membership and fees — is largely ready. The unresolved element is the structure of the new Association, including board composition, delegated powers and the authority of Congress.

Feedback from roadshows earlier this year had convinced the GAA that those proposals needed another revision.

Ryan could not provide a new completion date.

Instead, he talked about phased implementation rather than a single moment of integration and insisted that the GAA’s commitment remained “firm and absolute”.

He said he could not envisage a future in which the GAA existed simply as an organisation promoting male sport.

That was an important statement of intent, but it did not answer the question which had fuelled much of the reaction to McAleese’s departure: if integration remained the objective, how had the process gone from being publicly presented as on course only a few months ago to having no definitive delivery date?

 

Burns Brings Finance to the Centre of the Debate

By lunchtime, the argument had developed further.

GAA President Jarlath Burns appeared on RTÉ’s News at One and provided more detail on another major concern within Croke Park — the financial consequences of integration at elite inter-county level.

Burns said he remained “absolutely committed” to integration but acknowledged that concern about funding men’s and women’s county teams equally was at the heart of some of the hesitation.

The GAA currently spends around €45 million annually preparing its inter-county teams. Burns argued that moving immediately to equal spending across the men’s and women’s games could theoretically take that figure towards €90 million, which he said the Association simply could not sustain.

That explanation shifted the debate.

What had appeared initially to be principally a dispute over governance structures and timetables was now also explicitly about resources.

Burns spoke of equity rather than immediate equality, arguing that the challenge was particularly acute at high-performance county level rather than at club level.

He also responded directly to McAleese.

Her criticism of the GAA Management Committee was, he said, “very regrettable”, while he described the suggestion that women were unwelcome in senior decision-making as offensive to those serving on the committee. Nine of its 19 members are women.

At the same time, Burns was generous about McAleese personally, praising the job she had done and saying he would miss the wisdom she had brought to the process.

He also argued that rigid timelines had begun to work against progress and stressed the scale of what was being contemplated: ultimately winding up the LGFA and Camogie Association as independent governing organisations and creating something genuinely new.

That was a more nuanced position than simply rejecting the 2027 date, but it also reinforced the scale of the gap which had opened between the GAA leadership and the former independent chair.

 

Reaction Hardens During the Day

Away from Croke Park, the reaction increasingly focused on accountability.

The Gaelic Players Association had already insisted that the democratic mandate for integration could not simply be set aside. Its original motion at GAA Congress in 2022 received almost 90 per cent backing, while previous consultation has demonstrated similarly strong support among players and members.

Dublin camogie captain and GPA executive member Aisling Maher questioned how strongly the GAA really wanted integration and expressed frustration that players had received little indication that such a fundamental delay was coming.

She said players had believed the original timeframe was reasonable and suggested the decision appeared to have been taken at management level, without the wider involvement that might have been expected for such a fundamental change of direction.

Cork camogie star Ashling Thompson was even more forthright, saying players’ patience had run out and demanding that, if 2027 was no longer achievable, they should be told clearly what had changed and why.

Former Mayo great Cora Staunton also questioned whether the GAA had ever shown sufficient determination to make integration happen, while the political response extended well beyond the Department responsible for sport.

Minister for Children, Disability and Equality Norma Foley said women’s contribution to the GAA could no longer be regarded as peripheral, arguing that the Association could not function without the leadership, coaching, administration and voluntary work undertaken by women throughout the country.

Fine Gael Senator and former RTÉ sports broadcaster Evanne Ní Chuilinn was another strong critic, while Oireachtas Sports Committee Chair Alan Kelly has already indicated that the three organisations will be called before the Committee to explain what happened.

The controversy had by then moved well beyond an internal Gaelic games disagreement.

Former camogie player and journalist Sinéad O’Carroll characterised the dispute as being fundamentally about “money and power”, also highlighting the relevance of significant State funding and the expectations around gender equality that accompany it.

 

Government Steps In

The focus shifted again during the afternoon when Minister for Culture, Communications and Sport Patrick O’Donovan and Minister of State for Sport Charlie McConalogue held a series of meetings at the Department.

The meetings involved representatives of the GAA, LGFA and Camogie Association, as well as Dr McAleese separately, rather than a single meeting of all four.

O’Donovan emerged describing what had happened as a “massive setback”, but with one important piece of reassurance.

He had asked each of the three governing bodies directly whether it remained committed to integration.

“All three assured me they are in when it comes to integration,” he said.

That is perhaps the most important positive to emerge from Wednesday.

The Minister also recognised the financial issue highlighted by Burns and stressed that Government, as a major funder of Gaelic games, wanted to help find a solution to the future cost of integration.

O’Donovan wants a new schedule that is realistic and achievable.

“It is far better to get this right than to rush it,” was his assessment.

Later in the day, Ryan also wrote directly to GAA clubs seeking to reassure members that the work was continuing. Interestingly, after the language about “pause and reflect” which triggered McAleese’s resignation, Ryan told members that this was “definitely not a pause, a deferral or a suspension” and said work would continue with the LGFA and Camogie Association on a revised phased plan.

That language illustrates how rapidly the position evolved over the course of Wednesday.

 

Where Does It Go From Here?

The immediate danger when McAleese resigned was that four years of work might simply lose momentum.

Wednesday at least established that none of the three Associations is publicly walking away.

The Camogie Association remains committed, with President Brian Molloy also making the important point that integration itself will not magically create additional pitches, dressing rooms or facilities. Those investment problems exist regardless of the governance structure.

The LGFA has similarly maintained its commitment to completing the process.

The critical date now looks to be the next meeting of the Steering Group, which Minister O’Donovan said is due on 8 October, with the intention that a new independent chair will also be in place around that point. Minister McConalogue said on Prime Time last night that Government expects clear outlines from the three organisations on how integration will proceed.

 

Sport for Business Perspective

What happened over the past two days may ultimately prove to be either the moment integration lost its way or, more likely, the moment at which an overly opaque process was forced into the open.

Governance matters. Finance matters. Facilities matter. The treatment of inter-county teams matters. So too does ensuring that the identities and strengths developed by Ladies Gaelic Football and Camogie are not diminished in creating a single organisation.

All of those issues are important; none of them is new.

The mantra that “integration remains our ambition” is no longer acceptable as a substitute for a plan and a timetable.

That does not mean that it needs to be rushed, but it does need to be accelerated.  

That is why the next Steering Group meeting matters so much. 

The new chair should not be expected simply to restore the old April 2027 deadline if the organisations genuinely believe it cannot be delivered. Equally, the process cannot emerge with a vague commitment to spend another four or five years considering the problem.

The Once Club model, which operates in one third of clubs across the country, is tried and tested.  It relies on discussion and negotiation at each club, but with every club having a firm, defined role in the decision process on pitch allocation, funding, and the rest.  If we can do it at club level with wholly volunteer-led management, then surely this is not an insurmountable challenge.

What is required is a strong sequential timetable: what can be delivered in 2027, what requires additional time, who is responsible for each stage and by what date each decision will be made.

A new chair, a clear mandate and an agreed timeline would begin rebuilding the confidence lost this week.

The overwhelming support for one Gaelic games family has not disappeared.

Golf took five years and two months, from November 2015, when its joint discussion group between the old GUI and ILGU and an independent facilitator was appointed, to January 2021, when Golf Ireland formally came into existence.

The gravitas and heft that Dr Mary McAsleese brought to the Steering Group, and having presented to it on the One Club Model and attended this year’s Roadshow as a GAA member, was clear.  But perhaps her greatest contribution will be her departure lighting the fire that moves this along at a faster pace.
