Last Night of the Proms pays tribute to Dolly Parton as classical music showpiece draws to a close

Summary

  • Sheridan Smith, Beverley Knight and Ward Thomas pay tribute to Dolly Parton following her death last month on the Last Night of the Proms - "The hair is big, the suit is glittery and the atmosphere is jolly - the Queen of Country would have loved it"

  • After two months, we have reached the finale of the world's biggest classical music festival at the Royal Albert Hall in London

  • As the last act gets under way, "there's thumping on the floor, clapping and flag waving as soon as the orchestra begins Fantasia on British Sea Songs, and it's only going to get louder from here", writes our reporter at the concert

  • Scottish tenor Nicky Spence gets the crowd "roaring with laughter" as he pays many tributes to Scotland - and his costume changes are equally impressive

  • Superstar pianist Yuja Wang performs Barber's epic 26-minute Piano Concerto on her return to this venue - it's the first time the technical masterpiece has been performed at the Proms

Sheridan Smith, Beverley Knight and Ward Thomas pay tribute to Dolly Parton following her death last month on the Last Night of the Proms - "The hair is big, the suit is glittery and the atmosphere is jolly - the Queen of Country would have loved it"

After two months, we have reached the finale of the world's biggest classical music festival at the Royal Albert Hall in London

As the last act gets under way, "there's thumping on the floor, clapping and flag waving as soon as the orchestra begins Fantasia on British Sea Songs, and it's only going to get louder from here", writes our reporter at the concert

Scottish tenor Nicky Spence gets the crowd "roaring with laughter" as he pays many tributes to Scotland - and his costume changes are equally impressive

Superstar pianist Yuja Wang performs Barber's epic 26-minute Piano Concerto on her return to this venue - it's the first time the technical masterpiece has been performed at the Proms

Edited by Jamie Whitehead, with Rorey Bosotti reporting from the Royal Albert Hall and music correspondent Mark Savage

  1. It’s curtains down on the Last Night of the Promspublished at 23:24 BST 12 September
    23:24 BST 12 September

    Tara Mewawalla
    Live reporter

    Before tonight, I hadn’t ever watched the Proms and I didn’t know what to expect - safe to say I’ve had my expectations surpassed.

    Undoubtedly, one of the highlights was hearing star tenor Nicky Spence’s rich voice for the first time and marvelling as he moved fluidly between Dvořák’s Rusalka, Baccara’s Yes Sir I, Can Boogie and The Proclaimers I’m Gonna Be (500 Miles).

    Even better witnessing his half a dozen outfit changes, much to our Royal Albert Hall reporter Rorey Bosotti’s delight.

    You'd be forgiven for thinking Samuel Barber’s 26-minute“finger-twisting” masterpiece Piano Concerto might pose a minor threat to the attention span.

    But Yuja Wang’s performance and glittering stage presence kept me hooked and delighted concertgoers in the venue.

    For many, though, it will be the Dolly Parton tribute that sticks in the mind.

    The dolled-up, energetic Sheridan Smith and synergetic country-duo Ward Thomas brought 9 to 5 and Jolene to life. While Beverley Knight’s I Will Always Love You proved magnificent and heart-wrenching.

    We’re wrapping up our coverage now as the dapper ladies and gentlemen leave the Royal Albert Hall for their night ahead.

    To conclude, conductor Sakari Oramo left the audience thinking about Parton's legacy and the future of music, telling us: "It's the time to be authentically human."

    Until next year.

  11. Six performances away from the closing of another Proms seasonpublished at 22:11 BST 12 September
    22:11 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    And now we reach the home stretch, with the traditional closing sequence of the Last Night of the Proms.

    It’s now 71 years since these pieces (minus Auld Lang Syne) were first played together for the first time.

    The ever-popular Malcolm Sargent (or “Flash” as he was affectionately known) helped put together the programme that evening, as well as conducting the orchestra.

    The innovation came around the same time that the Proms were first broadcast on television, and Sargent also encouraged the waving of bright flags and banners to give the concert some visual splendour.

  13. A prototypical Broadway showtune with notable coverspublished at 22:04 BST 12 September
    22:04 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    Taken from the musical Guys & Dolls, Sit Down, You’re Rockin’ the Boat is sung by gambler Nicely-Nicely Johnson, as he recounts a dream of being saved from hell.

    In the musical, he’s singing it to a prayer circle – describing how he brough dice aboard the boat across the River Styx.

    The passengers objected to his gambling and drinking, telling him to "sit down, you're rockin' the boat." He was then washed overboard but awoke thanking the Lord.

    A prototypical Broadway showtune, it’s been covered by many famous artists over the years, including Louis Armstrong (1953), Sammy Davis Jr. (1955), Don Henley (1992), and James Taylor (2020).

  15. A commissioned piece for a composer with a long history in British musicpublished at 21:59 BST 12 September
    21:59 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    Another specially commissioned piece, Iris of Life comes from British composer Anne Dudley, who has a long association with the BBC’s orchestras.

    Born in Kent, she first found fame as a member of the experimental synth-pop band Art of Noise; and went on to become an arranger and composer for artists like Frankie Goes To Hollywood, Robbie Williams and Pulp.

  20. A jazz suite that survived the Stalinist erapublished at 21:23 BST 12 September
    21:23 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    Ukrainian composer Alexander Tsfasman was one of the pioneers of Soviet jazz, founding Russia’s first jazz orchestra in Moscow in 1926.

    Suite for Piano and Orchestra, written in 1945, is his best-known work. An effervescent and agile piano suite, it’s a personal favourite of tonight’s soloist Yuja Wang.

    Shortly after it premiered, however, Tsfasman’s career nose-dived, as jazz became seen as a symbol of Western decadence in Cold War-era Russia.

    “Today he plays jazz, tomorrow he’ll betray his country” was a widespread propaganda slogan of the Stalinist era.

    Happily, though, Tsfasman survived that era – living until 1971 – and his music has enjoyed a renaissance in recent years.

    It’s Yuja Wang’s second performance of the evening.
