  1. Opening tonight's performance: Leonard Bernstein's Candide – overturepublished at 19:17 BST 12 September
    19:17 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    We open with one of the biggest, most rambunctious, joy-filled pieces of classical music from the 20th Century.

    It’s taken from Leonard Bernstein’s third Broadway musical, Candide – a quintessential flop that closed after just 73 performances.

    It later became a cult favourite, with Bernstein obsessively revising and rewriting the music several times. But the opening number was a classic from the start.

    Memorably described as a “champagne overture”, it immediately grabs your attention with a huge timpani hit and a brass fanfare, then dashes around the orchestra with an effervescent energy for the next four minutes.

    What a way to start the show.

  5. Historic Proms moments down the yearspublished at 18:53 BST 12 September
    18:53 BST 12 September

    While we wait for the Last Night to kick off, let’s look back at some memorable moments:

    • 1939: After performing Beethoven's Second Piano Concerto and Sixth Symphony, musicians put down their instruments as conductor Henry Wood announces the rest of Proms are cancelled due to the outbreak of World War Two
    • 1947: BBC cameras arrive at the Royal Albert Hall to televise parts of the Last Night for the first time - orchestra members apparently struggled under the heat of the TV lights
    • 1953: Fantasia on British Sea Songs is removed from the Last Night performance due to what conductor Stanford Robinson describes as "hooliganism" in recent seasons. The move doesn't go down well with the public
    • 1969: The first performance of the 40-minute motet for orchestra Worldes Blis has some members of the audience heading for the bar long before the end
    • 1985: Mezzo-soprano Sarah Walker unbuttons the sleeve of her seemingly innocuous white dress to reveal a Union flag - setting a long-lasting sartorial precedent
    • 1999: Prommers get out of their seats for a conga around the Royal Albert Hall during the premiere of Bernstein's Wonderful Town
    • 2001: Just four days after the 9/11 attacks in the United States, the Last Night organisers decide against playing the traditional patriotic pieces Land of Hope and Glory and Rule, Britannia!
    • 2013: Marin Alsop becomes the first woman to conduct the Last Night
    • 2020: The Last Night is performed to an empty hall due to the Coronavirus pandemic
    • 2022: The concert is cancelled 48 hours beforehand following the death of Queen Elizabeth II. It's the first Last Night cancellation since 1944

  6. One of the UK's favourite tenors will take the stage tonightpublished at 18:45 BST 12 September
    18:45 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    Born and raised in Dumfries, Scotland, Nicky Spence has emerged as one of the UK’s favourite tenors.

    Trained at the Guildhall School of Music and Drama, Nicky won a recording contract in his final year and released his debut album My First Love in 2007.

    After touring with Katherine Jenkins and Dame Shirley Bassey, he turned his back on a commercial recording career and returned to Guildhall for a specialist opera course.

    Upon graduating, he joined the English National Opera, where he created the key character Brian in Nico Muhly’s Two Boys – a prescient 2011 opera about the effect online relationships and chartrooms were having on young men.

    Ever since, his international career has flourished. In 2022, he was named BBC Musician Magazine’s personality of the year.

    But his approach to music has changed since Spence and his husband, pianist Dylan Perez, became parents two years ago.

    “Fatherhood made me rethink everything – including how I hear and sing,” he recently told Gramophone Magazine.

    No stranger to the Proms, he’s back the Royal Albert Hall just a couple of weeks after playing title role in Carl Maria von Weber’s rarely performed opera Oberon.

    Asked whether he’d wear a kilt and sporran for the Last Night, Spence was circumspect.

    “That would be telling,” he told The Times. “Let’s just say people may see a bit of leg.”

  7. Yuja Wang will make her performance sing tonightpublished at 18:40 BST 12 September
    18:40 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    The highlight of tonight’s programme, for me at least, is the chance to watch Chinese piano prodigy Yuja Wang in full flight.

    A fantastically expressive musician, she’s known for her ability to tame rhythmically complex pieces and rich improvisational skills.

    Reviewing a 2012 concert appearance in San Francisco, music critic Joshua Kosman wrote that Wang is “quite simply, the most dazzlingly, uncannily gifted pianist in the concert world today, and there’s nothing left to do but sit back, listen and marvel at her artistry.”

    Tonight, she’ll play an electrifying rendition of Samuel Barber's Pulitzer Prize-winning Piano Concerto, a piece so technically demanding that it’s only been recorded a handful of times.

    The thunderous opening is full of hot-headed drama and intricate counterpoints, before giving way to an achingly melancholy mid-section.

    Based on previous performances, Wang will make it sing.

  14. A royal farewell for the Queen of Countrypublished at 17:49 BST 12 September
    17:49 BST 12 September

    Which venue could be more fitting than the Royal Albert Hall to pay tribute to country music royalty?

    Throughout her seven-decade career, Dolly Parton won 10 Grammy Awards, wrote in excess of 3,000 songs and sold more than 100 million records worldwide.

    Nearly three weeks after her death at the age of 80, the Last Night of the Proms is going to celebrate the Queen of Country with To Dolly, With Love, arranged by composer Fiona Brice.

    Sheridan Smith will join forces with Beverley Knight, country-pop duo Ward Thomas as well as the BBC Symphony Orchestra & Chorus and the BBC Singers for a medley of Parton's hits.

    Included in the tribute are the defiant anthem of female empowerment 9 to 5, the bittersweet I Will Always Love You and Jolene - her most popular song with more than 913 million streams on Spotify.

    It will kick off the second half of tonight’s concert.

  15. Long time viewer, first time Prommerpublished at 17:33 BST 12 September
    17:33 BST 12 September

    Rorey Bosotti
    At the Royal Albert Hall

    This is not my first time at the Royal Albert Hall, but it is my First Night of the Proms.

    Usually, I watch the Last Night from the comfort of my sofa, much to the delight of my neighbours who get a free - although very much not professional and certainly not requested - singalong rendition of some of the most famous pieces of classical music.

    But this year I'll actually be in the room where it all happens, much to my neighbour’s relief.

    After nearly two months, the Last Night of the Proms is the culmination of more than 80 concerts here at the Royal Albert Hall, along with several other performances across England and Wales.

    Conductor Sakari Oramo is our guide for the night on a journey starting off with Leonard Bernstein's Candide: Overture - a take on Voltaire's satirical novella in the vein of Offenbach and Gilbert and Sullivan - and ending with a traditional farewell to the tune of Auld Lang Syne.

    In between, a mix of classics, new compositions and a tribute to the late Queen of Country, Dolly Parton, who died last month aged 80.

    So warm up your vocal cords, stretch your legs and arms and join me on a night of music, singing, flag-waving and stomping.

  16. What are the BBC Proms?published at 17:22 BST 12 September
    17:22 BST 12 September

    The Last Night of the Proms is a concert that acts as the finale to the world’s biggest classical music festival.

    The Proms are a summer programme of concerts centred on London’s Royal Albert Hall, although there are other events around the country.

    The annual event was established in 1895 and is now run by the BBC.

    The 2026 Proms have featured some of the world’s best international orchestras and conductors.

    Highlights included the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra playing Elgar and Tchaikovsky, and the Los Angeles Philharmonic playing Beethoven.

    The season also featured some programmes that were a bit different (or a bit familiar, depending on your musical taste): Alan Menken’s Music for Disney, Miles Davis Centenary and Marvin Gaye - Prince of Soul.

    Tickets for the Last Night - featuring some of classical music’s top performers - are always a sell-out.

  17. Meet tonight’s conductor, Sakari Oramopublished at 17:13 BST 12 September
    17:13 BST 12 September

    Mark Savage
    Music correspondent

    Finland’s Sakari Oramo has been Chief Conductor of the BBC Symphony Orchestra since 2012, the longest tenure of anyone since Sir Adrian Boult, who founded the ensemble in 1930 and led it for the next 20 years.

    An accomplished violinist, he’s known for championing new and rarely performed works, as well as the elegance and passion of his musical interpretations.

    "It’s important to let the music just come to you without any kind of preconceived ideas," he says.

    Outside of the concert call, he’s a vintage car enthusiast – who once presented an entire radio segment on the cultural influence of the classic, hydro-pneumatically suspended Citroen DS.

  18. The classics, the modern and a tribute to a legend: What's on the menu tonight?published at 17:05 BST 12 September
    17:05 BST 12 September

    It's, as usual, a packed affair tonight - with plenty for music lovers tuning in.

    Marshalling performers and Prommers - a long-standing Proms tradition of booking tickets on the day of a performance for just £8 - though the evening is BBC Symphony Orchestra conductor Sakari Oramo, who's back for his seventh Last Night.

    Tonight’s show features pianist Yuja Wang - in her first Proms appearance since 2023 - and Scottish tenor Nicky Spence - whose skills effortlessly range from operatic roles to urbane, witty numbers.

    Highlights in the first half of the evening will include Bernstein’s sparkling Candide: Overture and Barber’s Pulitzer Prize-winning Piano Concerto, while opera lovers will get their moment with a rendition of Vidino divná, přesladká’ (Prince’s Aria) from Antonin Dvorak's lyric fairy tale Rusalka.

    But tonight is also one of remembrance. Last month, country music icon Dolly Parton died aged 80 and the Proms will pay tribute to her tonight. To Dolly, with Love is arranged by British composer Fiona Brice and features Sheridan Smith, Beverley Knight and English country band Ward Thomas.

    Also peppered in throughout the night are the world premieres of Academy Award-winning composer Anne Dudley's Iris of Life and Dithyramb by Canadian composer and conductor Samy Moussa.

    Bringing the BBC Proms 2026 season to an end will be, as per tradition, big classics including Elgar's Pomp and Circumstance, Arne's Rule! Britannia and Fantasia on British Sea-Songs.

    Make sure to prep your vocal cords if you plan to join in, it's going to get loud.

What are the BBC Proms?published at 17:22 BST 12 September

The Last Night of the Proms is a concert that acts as the finale to the world’s biggest classical music festival.

The Proms are a summer programme of concerts centred on London’s Royal Albert Hall, although there are other events around the country.

The annual event was established in 1895 and is now run by the BBC.

The 2026 Proms have featured some of the world’s best international orchestras and conductors.

Highlights included the Berlin Philharmonic Orchestra playing Elgar and Tchaikovsky, and the Los Angeles Philharmonic playing Beethoven.

The season also featured some programmes that were a bit different (or a bit familiar, depending on your musical taste): Alan Menken’s Music for Disney, Miles Davis Centenary and Marvin Gaye - Prince of Soul.

Tickets for the Last Night - featuring some of classical music’s top performers - are always a sell-out.

Finland’s Sakari Oramo has been Chief Conductor of the BBC Symphony Orchestra since 2012, the longest tenure of anyone since Sir Adrian Boult, who founded the ensemble in 1930 and led it for the next 20 years.
