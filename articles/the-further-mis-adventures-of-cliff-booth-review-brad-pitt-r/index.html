The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth review: Brad Pitt returns to his Oscar-winning role in a 'macho fantasy' ★★★☆☆

TheTools · 20.11.2026 19:48 · source · permalink

The sequel to Quentin Tarantino's Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, directed by David Fincher, is a "sleazy" 1970s-set caper where "Tinseltown glamour meets criminal seediness".

If you've ever wanted to hang out with Brad Pitt, now's your chance. In Quentin Tarantino's 2019 film, Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, Pitt played Cliff Booth, a supercool, super-tough stuntman who doubled for an actor played by Leonardo DiCaprio. Pitt won an Oscar for his performance, so it's not surprising that he and Tarantino are fond of the character. They've now brought Cliff back in his own spin-off film, The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, which is scripted by Tarantino and directed by David Fincher. And for most of the 143-minute running time, all he does is hang out with people.

There are four bursts of nerve-jangling, stomach-churning violence, as you might expect from a Tarantino-Fincher collaboration. But despite the oddly punctuated title – The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth – Cliff Booth doesn't have many misadventures. He doesn't have many adventures, for that matter. His defining quality is that he's supremely laidback, and this rambling crime caper is almost as laidback as he is. Luckily, it has some of his effortless charm, charisma and handsomeness, too.

The action unfolds in Los Angeles across one week in June 1977, eight years on from Cliff's fight with Charles Manson's acolytes in Once Upon a Time in Hollywood. Leonardo DiCaprio's actor from that film has relocated to Australia, so Cliff doesn't do stunts any more, and now lives what seems suspiciously like Tarantino's dream life. Always dressed in a Hawaiian shirt with half the buttons undone, he's a projectionist in the New Beverly Cinema – owned in reality by a certain Mr Q Tarantino. He tops up his income by mixing margaritas in a buzzing bar, where countless young women gaze adoringly at him. And in his spare time, he watches old westerns on television in the modest marina-side flat he shares with his lovably crotchety dad (Peter Weller).

Every now and then, though, Cliff has to scare off the low-life crooks who blackmail his film-industry buddies, drawing on his experience as a vicious jungle-fighter in World War Two.  When word of this sideline gets around, a television star, Charlie (Scott Caan), sends his agent Karen (Elizabeth Debicki) to arrange a meeting. Debicki is more elegantly statuesque than ever, which is saying something, and she gets laughs merely by raising her eyebrows, as Karen finds herself being appalled but intrigued by the cocksure Cliff. Caan, meanwhile, brings a welcome spark of manic energy to his scenes, his goofy eagerness contrasting nicely with Pitt's relaxed confidence.

Charlie, it turns out, is being threatened by a pair of Filipino hitmen (JB Tadena and Matt Groove) in matching black outfits, much like Vincent and Jules in Pulp Fiction. He needs Cliff to negotiate with them in his own unique way – but he doesn't explain his sticky situation until Cliff has been chatting, driving around, making cocktails, and, yes, hanging out for well over an hour. The film's mood and the milieu recall the dark comedy thrillers adapted from Elmore Leonard novels in the 1990s, namely Get Shorty, Out of Sight and Tarantino's own Jackie Brown. But you had to concentrate to keep track of Leonard's sprawling plots, whereas The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is a thin string of amusingly sleazy anecdotes, with barely enough story to fill an episode of Charlie's weekly TV series.

More than anything, the film is a loving tribute to the parts of 1970s LA where Tinseltown glamour met criminal seediness. Fincher and Tarantino luxuriate in the details of vintage sportscars, landline phones and historic cinemas (which is ironic for a production financed by a streaming service, Netflix). Their gorgeously designed nostalgia-fest is always easy to watch, and it's never boring, but some of its attitudes seem to come from the 1970s, too: it doesn't show much respect to any characters who aren't straight, white men. And it's definitely not the hyper-stylish, boundary-pushing, Oscar-worthy masterpiece that,  not so long ago, you might have assumed the writer of Pulp Fiction and the director of Fight Club would make together.

Whatever you think of Tarantino, he tends to be bullishly ambitious, putting his own provocative stamp on the western, the slavery drama, the war movie, and more. For his part, Fincher often pushes whichever genre he's working on to gloriously artistic extremes. But with The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, they're content to tell a mellow, meandering yarn, an antiquated macho fantasy with no depth and few innovations. At one point, Debicki delivers a speech about why Hollywood films are like cheeseburgers. It's fun, but Tarantino has written several better speeches about cheeseburgers already.

★★★☆☆

The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is released in the US and UK on 25 November, and will be available to stream on Netflix internationally from 23 December.
