The World After 9/11: An Atlas of the Issues That Shaped the 21st Century and the Future We Are Building (2) - WMagazín

English edition

Crisis medioambiental, transformaciones sociales, amenazas a la democracia, convivencia con la inteligencia artificial y preservación de lo que nos hace humanos son algunos de los desafíos del siglo XXI. /WMagazín

Por Winston Manrique Sabogal

Twenty-five years after the terrorist attacks in the United States, which serve as a historical turning point, we review some of the major problems and challenges facing humanity: the environment and climate crisis, feminism and diversity, threats to democracy, and the influence of AI and its regulation. We recommend essential books for a better understanding of each of these issues.

The first part of this special explored some of the major transformations of the external world that emerged after 9/11: wars and the new geopolitical order, security and surveillance, globalization and inequality, the internet and social media, and finally, data, algorithms, and artificial intelligence. (Read the first part HERE)

This second part shifts its focus. From power, technology, and large-scale structures, we move to the planet we inhabit, the rights we have won, the democracies that are trying to resist, and, finally, to the question that runs through the entire atlas: what future do we want to build, and what role do we want machines to play in it?

Because these twenty-five years have not only been a time of fractures and losses. They have also been years of achievements, new forms of consciousness, and struggles for a more egalitarian society. The future, as this journey reminds us, is not yet written.

9/11 didn’t create these events; it served as a historical turning point, accelerating or initiating major global transformations and providing a starting point for observing how these have changed the way we live, relate to one another, work, govern ourselves, and envision the future.

Climate, nature, and the environmental crisis: what have we done to the planet?

Since the 1970s, scientists have issued increasingly urgent warnings about a planet endangered by human activity. It’s a less immediate problem than terrorism, wars, economic crises, or the digital revolution, but it has relentless consequences for everyone, everywhere: global warming, biodiversity loss, pollution, ocean alteration, and a new awareness that the human development model has physical consequences for the planet.

The chosen book:

Life on a Little-Known Planet: Messages from a World in Transformation

Elizabeth Kolbert. (Random House)

In The Sixth Extinction, Elizabeth Kolbert explains how human activity is causing a mass extinction of species. In this new essay, she travels through territories where the climate crisis is already a reality and gathers the testimonies of those who experience it every day, from Greenland to the Peruvian Andes and New Zealand.

Kolbert creates a scientific, journalistic, and literary mosaic of the planet’s transformation and humanity’s self-destruction. The writer manages to harmonize the specialized with the literary, with writing populated by images and voices that astonish the reader and invite them to become more aware of this reality.

This is a journey of wonder, disbelief, and growing concern about what is happening, without, in reality, any measures being taken to prevent a major, accelerated change whose trajectory could lead to catastrophe. But the book also recalls the light and beauty of nature, and hope.

EXCERPT

“We live in an extraordinary time. This is true not only from a provincial American (or Australian) point of view, but from a planetary perspective. A recent study suggests that all living things—you, me, the birds and bees, the snake in the grass, the worm in the apple, as well as the splinter from that stick—are descended from a single-celled organism that evolved some four billion years ago. Since the days of LUCA (Last Known Common Ancestor), a lot has happened. The atmosphere has filled with oxygen. Continents have collided and drifted apart. The entire planet has frozen and thawed. Plants and animals have left the water to settle on land. The age of reptiles has given way to the age of mammals.

Yet, in the last four billion years, change has only very rarely occurred at the speed it is happening today. And there have probably never been so many different forms of change. Due to the actions of our species, which is, of course, our own, we are now experiencing a warming world, with oceans acidifying, forests shrinking, groundwater disappearing, microplastics proliferating, and so-called “forever chemicals” spreading. Every day, we transport thousands of species from one side of the world to the other, mostly along with goods. Those that find their new home agreeable—like cats in Australia—often wreak havoc. Many geologists believe that humankind has become the dominant force on the planet, to the point that we have entered a new era, the Anthropocene.

 

A second reading: Linking Ourselves to the Land. Peasant Contexts, by Jerónimo Aguado Martínez (Pol·len Edicions): Agriculture and culture, what the countryside has made of us and what we may lose by forgetting it.

The Rights Revolution: Women, Feminism, and Diversity

This quarter-century has not only been marked by wars, crises, inequalities, and new threats. It has also been a time of historic gains in rights and freedoms. The concept of equality, the situation of women, and the recognition of LGBTQIA+ people have undergone a profound transformation.

Feminism has placed issues that long remained on the margins at the center of the debate: violence against women, workplace equality, representation, the distribution of care work, the body, sexuality, and power relations. Movements like #MeToo have changed social, cultural, and labor conversations, while numerous countries have expanded rights and legal recognition.

But these advances are not irreversible. The backlash against these gains has also grown, making feminism, gender, and LGBTQIA+ rights one of the major cultural and political battlegrounds of our time.

The question, therefore, is what has really changed in these twenty-five years, and to what extent these gains have become part of society, or whether they remain an ongoing struggle.

The chosen book:

Feminismo e imaginarios de la globalización. Apuntes para una agenda feminista global 

Celia Amorós (Comares)

Philosopher Celia Amorós explores how to think about women and feminism in a globalized world shaped by technoscience. Her reflections allow us to examine one of the great social transformations of this quarter-century from a critical perspective: what progress has been made and, at the same time, what forms of inequality and domination persist or take on new expressions.

Amorós argues for the need for a global feminist agenda capable of responding to the material conditions of life for millions of women and to the new forms of patriarchal domination. Her analysis links feminism, globalization, technology, and politics, and posits women’s emancipation as one of the great unfinished projects of our time.

The book thus offers a philosophical and political perspective on feminism in the global era and allows us to broaden our view beyond legal achievements to question the real conditions of equality.

EXCERPT

“The intention is clear: to take a philosophical-feminist approach to the phenomenon of globalization. And to do so by identifying emerging subjects within this context and, more specifically, the role and future of women within it. Amorós proposes a synthesis between socialist feminism and radical feminism in the world of globalization, seeking to provide a political response to the challenges facing feminism in our era of globalization. This political reinterpretation goes hand in hand with her Enlightenment-inspired reinterpretation of emerging subjects—the ‘modest, transformed witnesses’ theorized by Donna Haraway. With the aim of reclaiming the Enlightenment perspective, Amorós leads us to reflect on some of the epistemological problems and political projects from which to consider the feminist movement and thought in our global era.

She does so because she is convinced that it is urgent to continue thinking about how to transform the material conditions of life for many, many women on our planet, women who, despite globalization, remain immersed in increasingly blatant forms of patriarchal subjugation. Thinking about this project of emancipation for women in our current planetary context is nothing less than the task to which Celia Amorós invites us with this book. It is, in short, about establishing the global theoretical agenda of feminism”.

 

Second reading: Who’s Afraid of Gender? Judith Butler. Translation: Alicia Martorell Linares (Paidós)

Erosion of democracy and rise of authoritarianism: where are we headed?

All these transformations end up having a reflection on political life and coexistence. Wars, security, globalization, inequality, the Internet, social media, algorithms, artificial intelligence, and the climate crisis have tested democracies and fueled new forms of polarization, distrust, and authoritarianism. That is why this atlas places one of its crucial chapters here, before the epilogue: because the state of democracy and the collective memory of societies manifest themselves, in one way or another, in the very fabric of our societies.

Democracy is a crossroads: the diversification of terrorism, threats to public safety, the pitfalls of globalization, the inability of some governments to solve contemporary problems, and the law of the jungle of the internet and social media, which facilitate all kinds of anti-democratic messages, erode the values ​​and ideals of the Enlightenment and of democracy itself.

The chosen book:

The Twilight of Democracy: The Seduction of Authoritarianism

Anne Applebaum. (Knopf Doubleday Publishing Group)

This is a historical and contemporary analysis of the attacks on liberal democracy in the West, attacks that threaten freedom, equality, rights, and coexistence. This is happening in parallel with the rise of ideas that contradict all of the above. The historian shows how polarization, nationalism, disinformation, and the loss of trust in institutions have opened the door to new forms of authoritarianism. “A detailed analysis of the earthquake shaking the world and a passionate defense of democratic values. A devastating analysis of the current political landscape,” said The Washington Post.

Applebaum alerts us all to this situation by exposing the traps of nationalism and autocracy and explains why political systems with simple and radical messages are so appealing.

EXCERPT

“We may be living through a turning point. My children and their friends—and all our friends, indeed, all of us who want to continue living in a world where we can confidently speak our minds, where rational debate is possible, where knowledge and experience are respected, where crossing borders is easy—may become—may become—the embodiment of one of history’s many dead ends. Perhaps we are doomed, like the brilliant, multiethnic Vienna of the Habsburgs or the creative, decadent Berlin of Weimar, to be swept away into irrelevance. It is possible that we are already witnessing the twilight of democracy; that our civilization is already veering toward anarchy or tyranny, as the ancient philosophers feared and, more recently, the founders of the United States; that in the 21st century a new generation of clerics, champions of anti-liberal or authoritarian ideas, will come to power, just as they did in the 20th; and that their worldviews, born of resentment, anger, or deep-seated messianic dreams may prevail. Perhaps new information technology will continue to undermine consensus, further dividing people and exacerbating polarization until only violence determines who rules. Perhaps the fear of disease will end up breeding a fear of freedom.

Or it is also possible that the coronavirus will inspire a new sense of global solidarity. We may renew and modernize our institutions. Perhaps the fact that everyone has lived through the same experiences at the same time—lockdown, quarantine, fear of contagion, fear of death—will lead to increased international cooperation. Perhaps scientists around the world will find new ways to collaborate outside the realm of politics. Perhaps the reality of illness and death will teach people to be wary of charlatans, liars, and purveyors of disinformation.

As exasperating as it may seem, we must accept that both futures are possible. There is no permanent political victory; nothing guarantees the enduring nature of any definition of ‘the nation,’ and no No elite of any kind—be they “populists,” “liberals,” or “aristocrats”—rules forever. From the vast distance of time, the history of ancient Egypt seems to us a monotonous tale of interchangeable pharaohs; but if we examine it more closely, we will see that it includes culturally luminous periods and eras of despotic darkness. Someday, our history will look like that too.”

 

A second reading: The Future of Democracy, by Daniel Innerarity (Galaxia Gutenberg): what can happen to democracy and how it can be renewed.

Epilogue

For a more humane future: how do we want to coexist with machines? If the Internet was the great technological transformation of the 25 years after 9/11, will artificial intelligence define the next 25? Undoubtedly, its influence will increase in all areas of life, for better and for worse: it is already present in the economy, politics, society, medicine, security, and culture.

The chosen book:

Ya están aquí. Cómo mantener nuestra humanidad en la era de los gemelos artificiales

Idoia Salazar (Ariel)

“To live with the inevitable, it’s best to create the coordinates and be in control. This essay offers an ethical and practical compass to guide us in the new era of universal AI assistants.

“One morning you’ll wake up and your day will have already begun without you. An intelligence will have reorganized your schedule, answered messages, and made decisions on your behalf. This will be the new normal. AI is ceasing to be a tool we ask for answers and becoming a presence capable of acting for us.

Universal AI Assistants are already being used in the corporate and governmental sectors, but they are about to become democratized and accessible to everyone. They will be able to get to know us, anticipate our needs, and manage parts of our daily lives.

Idoia Salazar guides us through the new leap in AI: from chatbots to agentic AI, from convenience to the risk of dependency, from the promise of help to the challenge of preserving our autonomy. On the horizon loom general artificial intelligence, the technological singularity, and a crucial question: how to coexist with intelligences that could know us better than we know ourselves.

An ethical and practical roadmap to understand what we can delegate, what we must protect, and how to remain human in the emerging new era.

A second reading: The Challenge of Being Human: The 8 Competencies for Thinking, Creating, and Leading in the Age of AI. César Bona (Ariel)

Heraclitus wrote more than two thousand years ago that “Everything flows, nothing remains.” Twenty-five centuries later, his words seem to describe this world in constant transformation, a whirlwind in which wars and power balances, the economy and society, our relationship with nature, democracy, and our way of thinking, communicating, and relating to machines are all in flux. But if everything flows, we must also ask ourselves what we want to remain. That is, perhaps, the great challenge of our time: to navigate this whirlwind of change without losing what makes us human.

And, above all, without losing the capacity to imagine where we want to go. “The problem is not that we need utopias, but that we need horizons,” says Manuel Cruz in *Resabiados y resentidos* (Bitter and Resentful). “Without them,” the philosopher continues, “we are condemned to a disoriented activism that, in the worst case, can resonate with irrationalist nihilisms of infamous memory and, in the least bad case, in a succession of blind stabs without any future prospects.”
