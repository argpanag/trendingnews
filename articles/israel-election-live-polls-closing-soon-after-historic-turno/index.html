What happens after polls close?

Polling stations across Israel are set to close in less than 35 minutes, when news tv stations will release their first exit polls giving preliminary estimates of how many seats each party has won.

The first lot of official results then won’t be released until after around 1am local time on Wednesday. And while the exit polls usually provide a reliable indication of how people have voted, they can also vary.

The current coalition is expected to lose its majority, but we won’t expect clear results quickly as the coalition nature of Israeli government means that even small changes in vote share will change coalition calculations. We therefore may not get fuller results until the next day or the day after.

The final tally is expected by 30 October and official election results are formally published a week after the election, on 4 November.

After ​the final tally is ​in, Israel’s president, Isaac Herzog, ⁠will consult party leaders on whom they want as prime minister and then tap the candidate he believes has the best chance ​of forming a government.

That nominee has up to 42 days to form ​a government.

No ⁠single party in Israel has ever won an outright majority, so governments are formed by a coalition of parties who can get to 61 seats – and negotiations on forming a government often take weeks. Every Knesset seat counts and ⁠small ​parties can have a big impact by becoming kingmakers.

If ​the nominee fails, others can get a chance to try. If no government emerges, another election is called, meaning ​Benjamin Netanyahu and his cabinet would remain in place as a “caretaker” government.

Voter turnout as of 8pm surpasses total of 2022 election day

It’s now just gone 9pm in Israel, and nationwide voter turnout as of 8pm was 74.3%, per the Central Elections Committee.

That is not only up from 66.3% at the same time in the 2022 election; that figure surpasses the 70.6% total recorded turnout on election day in 2022.

The governor of New York, home to the world’s largest urban Jewish community outside of Israel, has said that her job would get “a lot easier” if Benjamin Netanyahu loses this election.

In an interview with Politico, Kathy Hochul said:

I represent the largest [urban] Jewish population in the world outside of Israel. And I know they’re suffering. A lot of people are anxious about antisemitism, which is sky-high right now …

I went to Israel right after October 7. I saw a lot of pain there. But I actually cautioned the government there, in private conversations, not to overreach.

The world is with you when it comes to the brutal assault by … Hamas on innocent Israeli people. An overreach could change that dynamic, and that’s what’s happened.

We have to have leadership there that understands the security of Israel is paramount - critically important to me, but also the world - but to have a safe Palestinian state as well, which has been talked about my entire life.

Voter turnout by 6pm is highest on record

Voter turnout as of 6pm local time reached 66.7%, the highest ever by that hour since voting hours were first recorded, the Central Elections Committee said.

That is a 9% increase from the 2022 election at that time and Israel’s highest recorded turnout by 6pm.

Election committee rejects Netanyahu bloc request to extend voting hours

The Central Elections Committee decided not to extend voting hours beyond 10pm, rejecting a request submitted by Netanyahu bloc parties, Haaretz is reporting.

In a statement, the committee said it met this evening and decided “the current circumstances do not justify an extension”.

It added that the high turnout “reflects strong public engagement in the democratic process” and “no need to extend voting hours was identified”.

A reminder that, under Israeli law, the current rules do allow for a voter to cast their vote past the polling time, just as long they arrive at a station by 10pm.

As we’ve been reporting, a record number of Israelis cast their ballots in the first half of polling day, voting at polling stations across the country and in the Israeli-occupied West Bank.

By 2pm local time (it is now after 8pm), 44.9% of voters had cast their ballot – the highest turnout since record-keeping began in the 1970s.

Palestinian Israeli turn-out strong despite far-right's delaying tactics

In the Palestinian Christian town of Taybeh, Ahmed Tibi, the leader of another Joint List party, Ta’al, had a shouting match with one of the Israeli right wing party representatives over delays, complaining that a woman had fainted while waiting in line.

At a polling station in a Nazareth school this afternoon, an election worker, Bilal Sha’awan shrugged off the far right’s tactics as “childsplay”.

At 3pm there were no queues and a steady flow of voters. A representative of Itamar Ben-Gvir’s Jewish Power party, a young man dressed in the style of settler “hilltop youth”, dressed casually, with a knitted kippa and long sidelocks, strode past Sha’awan, but gave no comment.

Manar Hassan, an observer from Ra’am, the other significant Palestinian Israeli party, described the turnout as “amazing”.

“It’s never been this strong,” she said, adding that the reasons for the higher level participation were not hard to find.

“We witnessed a catastrophe in Gaza,” Hassan said. “Here we live in fear and anxiety. We need drastic change.”

Another hourly voter turn-out update. As has been well established by now this is the largest turnout on record (since the early 1970s when such things were first measured).

As of 6pm local time, 66.7% of eligible voters had cast a ballot. That’s a 9% increase since the last election in 2022.

We can also expect a surge in the Palestinian Israeli vote – which accounts for about 20% of the population – as they tend to vote after work.

We should hear shortly the decision of the Central Electoral Committee on whether they will grant the Netanyahu bloc’s request to extend voting hours past 10pm local time. At the moment, polls are due to close in about 2.5 hours.

Many Israelis abroad have flown back to vote out Netanyahu

Tens of thousands of Israelis who live overseas have flown back to Israel to vote. Around 800,000 eligible Israeli voters live outside the country, over 10 percent of the potential electorate, according to Ha’aretz newspaper, but only diplomats can cast a ballot overseas.

For everyone else, taking part in elections requires investing considerable amounts of time and money to fly back.

Yuval Schwarzfuchs, 26, travelled from Rio de Janeiro via Ethiopia, to vote for the Palestinian-led Joint List. “For me, its about equality for all,” he said. “I did what I had to do.”

He came to the Jerusalem polling station wearing a T-shirt with an image of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, the left-wing President of Brazil, who defied polls to win re-election at the weekend.

That victory gave some hope to opponents of Israel’s right-wing government.

“Netanyahu does have a good campaign but I think he will loose,” said Schwarzfuchs, who was at the polling station with his brother, who also voted for the Joint List.

Up to 67,000 voters may be returning to Israel, according to AID Coalition, a U.S.-based nonprofit focused on strengthening Israel’s democracy, so this constituency could be key in a tight race.

In Israel’s proportional representation system, a few thousand votes can have a significant impact on results and around 40,000 votes is expected to secure a seat in the Knesset.

AID coalition organised a “Fly&Vote” initiative to support Israelis who wanted to return for this election, and their figure is based on surveys and registration data. About half of returnees are flying in from the United States, with others from Europe, Canada, Australia and New Zealand.

The project is bipartisan, and not everyone returning to vote opposes the current government, but anecdotal evidence suggests many are opposition supporters who share the view that Israel faces an existential choice.

Monica Moria, 58, and her husband Ron Moria flew in to Tel Aviv from Barcelona. Then they took a train to the kibbutz Kabri in northern Israel, where she is registered to vote, where Moria cast her ballot for the most left-wing of the Jewish Israeli parties, the Democrats.

There were at least five other people at the kibbutz who had flown in to vote, including one who came from Australia and another from the United States.

More right-wing, Netanyahu-bloc parties request voting time extensions

Amid the record turnout, other parties in the Netanyahu coalition camp have joined his Likud party’s request for voting hours to be extended. At the moment, polls are due to close in just under three hours, by 10pm local time.

Shas, led by Aryeh Deri, and Amcha Yisrael, led by Ofer Winter, have requested one-hour extensions until 11pm.

Shas said it had requested the extension to “prevent a situation in which citizens waiting in line would be unable to vote.”

However the current rules do allow for a voter to cast their vote past the polling time, just as long they arrive at a station by 10pm.

What had polls shown?

Most polls show Netanyahu’s Likud and his current coalition parters losing their majority. That fall in voter support has been fairly consistent for more than two years.

They also show a clear anti-Netanyahu majority.

However, those opposition seats are divided between the bloc of Jewish Israeli parties, led by Eisenkot’s Yashar, and parties representing Palestinian citizens of Israel.

The Jewish Israeli parties are unlikely to have a majority on their own, but have mostly ruled out going into coalition with Palestinians.

Palestinian politicians have indicated they might support a minority government of the Jewish Israeli parties in order to unseat Netanyahu’s far-right coalition.

While Jewish Israeli and Palestinian Israeli parties teaming up to form a coalition is seen as a bit taboo – particularly after anti-Arab sentiment rose in the wake of the October 7 attacks – it is not unprecedented.

The Ra’am leader, Mansour Abbas, in 2021 took his party into a historic coalition government with Naftali Bennett, now part of the opposition bloc, and Netanyahu himself had previously started coalition negotiations with Abbas.

Polling from Channel 12 on the weekend showed too that among the ‘Change’ bloc – what Eisenkot, Bennett, Galan and Liberman are calling their alliance – 60% of voters would prefer a minority government formed with the support of Ra’am from the outside, compared to 20% who would favour repeat elections, and 10% who would want the opposition to unite with Netanyahu.

Opposition calls Netanyahu request to extend voting hours 'groundwork to challenge election integrity'

His Likud party said in the past hour they’d requested an extension to voting hours from electoral authorities.

He and far-right allies have been running a racist scare campaign today, urging their base to show up to a vote which they claimed was being “stolen”.

The main opposition party leading in the polls, Yashar, has come out saying it “firmly opposes” any extension to voting hours. Polls are open for roughly another 3.5 hours, until 10pm local time.

Gadi Eisenkot’s party called it an attempt to “lay the groundwork for allegations challenging the integrity of the election”.

The Central Election Committee (CEC) – the country’s electoral authorities – is meeting in an hour to consider the request.

There have also been several complaints filed by political parties in the anti-Netanyahu bloc to the committee today.

  • Yashar said it had contacted the CEC following reports some of its ballot slips had been removed or damaged at polling stations

Israelis are turning out in record high numbers to vote in an election that many describe as critical. The urgency among opposition voters is even higher than supporters of Netanyahu and his coalition, analysts say.

One of the voters who considers this an existential moment for Israel is Nir Honi, who changed his name by deed poll and has spent eight days inside a small stone circle outside the president’s residence, dressed in biblical- style clothing, in a one-man call for change.

His protest harks back to the story of a first-century Jewish scholar named Honi who convinced God to end a drought with a similar protest.

“(Honi) drew a circle around himself with a stick, and said ‘dear God, I will not move from here until you have mercy on your children and bring us rain’,” the newly renamed Nir Honi said. “My problem is not rain, its a more spiritual issue.”

“We have been in a continuous disaster since October 7th 2023,” he said.

We did not learn anything, we did not investigate, the same government continued and they want to continue for four more years.”

The Palestinian Israeli vote is a critical camp in this election, representing about one fifth of all eligible voters.

Their votes are expected to go to either Ra’am, led by Mansour Abbas, or Joint List, an electoral alliance of Palestinian-Israeli parties.

But for them them, change will not be straightforward, as my colleague Julian Borger in Nazareth writes.

Even if the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot wins more seats than Netanyahu’s coalition, it is not expected to win an outright majority on its own, and it has so far ruled out going into coalition with the Palestinian Israeli parties, who could well hold the balance of power ones the votes are counted.

Amjad Shbita, the general secretary of Hadash, the leading party in the Joint List coalition, thought that the Eisenkot’s approach might change after the elections.

“He cannot sit down [in coalition] with Netanyahu, and he won’t want to go back to another round of elections, so we think he can be flexible,” he said.

'We feel racism at all levels - today we have the opportunity to change the government' - Palestinian Israelis

In Nazareth, the largest Palestinian-majority town in Israel, the turnout at polling stations has been noticeably stronger than last elections.
By 2pm the turnout was over 32%, up from 28% at this time four years ago.
That was still below the average Jewish turnout across the country, but a surge of evening voting is expected here as voters come home from work.
At the headquarters of the Joint List, the biggest coalition of Palestinian-Israeli parties, the most pressing concern was the spoiler tactics of far-right election monitors at the predominantly Palestinian polling stations.
“They can say that they want to check all the voter numbers and identity and everything, so they can slow things down, so that people wait a long time and give up. That’s their intention,” Amjad Shbita, the general secretary of Hadash, a socialist movement which is the leading party in the Joint List coalition.
At the worst, these tactical ploys have slowed the process down so that it took 30 minutes to vote, rather than just a couple, he said.
The Joint List sent its workers out to the polling station to provide water and folding chairs so that voters would not lose patience and go home.
Shbita said that Palestinian Israel motivation is strong enough to overcome such obstacles.
“It is the first time for a long time that a government has stayed in office for its full term, four years,” he said.
“We are talking about one of the worst governments when it comes to Arab society. We feel racism at all levels of Israeli society, in hospitals, in buses, in public transportation, in the media. So people understand that today we have the opportunity to change the government.”
