Was that the sound of an AI bubble bursting?

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

Just how serious is the crash in AI-related equities, and where does the current capitulation stand compared to recent deflating/bursting bubbles?

Global strategist and keen observer of such things Gerard Minack has compiled an interesting list — noting that there's no agreed-upon AI benchmark, but for the exercise he chose the Goldman Sachs broad AI index:

  • AI 37% below its June peak.
  • Japan's 1980s bubble biggest correction was -26% in 1987 after the US market crash.
  • The biggest correction in the Technology, Media, Telecom cycle (AKA the dot com bubble) was -28% in 1998.

As Minack observes, "AI is getting smoked" at the moment, but it differs from the Japanese and dot com collapses, which were driven by external shocks.

"This AI sell-off reflects AI-specific concerns," he says.

"This AI sell-off reflects AI-specific concerns," he says.

Minack says another key difference is that the AI is an earnings bubble, while the other two were valuation bubbles.

"Valuation bubbles rarely reflate once they have sprung a serious leak.

"A 30%-plus decline is a major puncture.

"But in the current earnings bubble, the decline means that AI stocks are now optically cheap — some are extraordinarily cheap, implying an imminent large decline in earnings."

Australian business activity slows as new orders and output falls

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

The latest reading of activity in Australia's business heartland shows the pulse is weakening with the S&P Global "flash" Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) slipping deeper into contraction.

The flash, or preliminary, composite PMI reading came in at 48.7 points for November, down from 49.6 points in the previous month, where 50 is the break-even point between expanding and contracting activity.

The composite PMI combines activity in both the manufacturing and services sectors.

The contraction was driven by the sharpest decline in new order and output in five years.

S&P Global's key findings include:

  • Business activity fell at the sharpest pace in more than five years amid fresh reductions in both overall and overseas new business.
  • Spare capacity emerged and job shedding resumed.
  • Companies were also at their least optimistic in three years.
  • On the price front, the rates of both input cost and output price inflation softened in November as demand eased.

S&P Global economist Jingyi Pan said latest decline in demand reflected the impact of higher interest rates, while softening domestic and external conditions also contributed.

"Notably, inflationary pressures eased for the first time in several months as demand softened across the board, speaking to the effectiveness of the recent tightening of financial conditions," Pan said

"That said, employment also fell alongside a marked reduction in business confidence among private sector firms, pointing to more subdued output conditions in the near-term at a time when the elevated inflation has yet to be fully brought under control."

Key Event

ALS interim profit jumps on minerals testing business

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

The mining and engineering analysis firm ALS has reported a more than doubling in net profit thanks to strong demand for its services in the mining sector and an uplift in the value of its investment in Saudi Arabia.

Statutory net profit for the first half of FY27 jumped 110% to just shy of $300 million, a result that included a significant expansion in its investment in Saudi Arabia.

Underlying net profit — which strips out one-off items such as investment revaluations — rose 23.6% to $215 million, beating consensus analyst forecasts.

It was driven by a strong 25% increase in revenue in its mining/commodities division with a 35% expansion in margins.

The life sciences division, including food and environment testing, reported more modest growth.

ALS increased its interim dividend by 20% to 23.3 cents per share.

The company delivered a positive outlook targeting high-single-digit revenue growth and margin expansion, with the minerals business expected to deliver revenue growth of about 20%.

Bank results show trouble is brewing

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

The big global investment bank and broker JP Morgan says the recent reporting season for Australian banks shows "trouble is brewing", despite share price valuations holding up.

According to JP Morgan's veteran Australian bank analyst Andrew Triggs, the reporting season was "slightly disappointing" with profit downgrades of between 1% and 6% across the sector, with the exception of ANZ, which remained unchanged in outlook.

"The downgrades continued the general trend from May and August results, an about-face from the positive EPS (earnings per share) momentum seen through 2025 and up to Feb-26," Mr Triggs wrote in a note to clients.

"Stepping back, the banking market remains highly competitive, and macro risks are building (rising unemployment rate, weak business confidence/conditions, stagflation risks).

"We do not think these are adequately reflected in valuations, and we remain cautious on the majors," he said.

Copper comes a cropper

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

Copper had been enjoying a good year buoyed by speculation of ongoing demand in global electrification and data centres, not to mention supply issues from industrial action by miners keen to join in on the benefits of rising prices as well.

But with the AI trade arcing like a poorly insulated old generator, copper was always likely to short circuit as well.

On Friday, copper slumped to its lowest level since March and its steepest weekly drop in more than 19 months as appetite for investment in the AI sector continued to wane.

Benchmark three-month copper on the London Metal Exchange at one stage touched $US12,020/tonne, its lowest since March 24, to close down 2.9% to $US12,160/tonne.

The metal, tipped to have a huge role in AI infrastructure build-out, is down 9.3% this week and has lost about 14% this month.

"There's more and more evidence that the AI investment boom is waning and that the US Fed is going to have to get more aggressive with policy to control inflation," Capital.com analyst Kyle Rodda said.

"There's more and more evidence that the AI investment boom is waning and that the US Fed is going to have to get more aggressive with policy to control inflation," Capital.com analyst Kyle Rodda said.

Copper was being weighed down by a strengthening US dollar, Rodda added.

Capital Economics senior climate and commodities economist Kieran Tompkins noted in the ongoing tug-of-war between physical and financial price drivers, "the sharp fall in metals prices show that financial drivers were in the ascendancy this week".

"That said, a couple of physical signals demonstrate just how tight pockets of copper and aluminium markets have become, and this could offset any further downward pressure on prices if the tech sell-off continues," Tompkins wrote in a weekend note.

All the industrial metals on the LME had a tough Friday. Aluminium fell 1.4%, while Zinc, lead and nickel dropped 1.7%, 1.1% and 0.9% respectively.

Australian mining ASX sub-index fell almost 13% last week, its worst week since the early stages of the Covid rout in early 2020.

Judging by Friday's price action, a short-term rebound is unlikely.

From a local macro perspective, the focus this week is on Wednesday's October inflation data from the ABS.

The market's best idea for the headline Consumer Price Index is a chunky 0.4% rise, which would lift the annual rate from 4.3% to 4.8%.

Transport, or in this case the cost of fuel, is expected to be the biggest contributor.

The RBA's preferred measure, trimmed mean CPI, which excludes volatile categories, is also expected to rise by 0.4% quarter on quarter and take the annual pace down a notch from 3.7% to 3.6%, still too high for the RBA's liking.

The credit card surcharge change may well muddy things at the margin.

Overseas, the key data point will be the US core private final consumption deflator (aka the Fed's preferred measure of inflation) for October, which is forecast to show inflation creeping ever higher, from 3.1% (yoy) in September to 3.2% last month.

Wall Street retreat becomes a rout, ASX to fall again

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

Clunk. Friday's capitulation on Wall Street was savage, but not entirely unexpected.

For six consecutive sessions the market was in a doleful retreat.

On the seventh day it became a disorderly flight out of not only anything related to AI, but most other sectors, markets and commodities.

  • S&P 500: -3.7%
  • Dow: -1.9%
  • Nasdaq: -5.3%

It capped Wall Street's worst week in more than six years, with the S&P 500 shedding 8.6% to be down almost 12% since its October high.

 A "correction", a 10% or more fall from a peak, is the anodyne word for it. Those forced to sell to cover losses, or see their superannuation balances shrink, would probably use something stronger.

The once high-flying US tech sector, as measured by the Nasdaq index, is now lower than it was at the start of the year.

The pain was widespread. The broad Eurostoxx600 fell 2% and the emerging markets' index dropped 3.1%.

Add in Asia's decline earlier in the day and MSCI's gauge of global stocks shed 3.3%. Its weekly decline of 8.1% was the largest since early 2020.

There's no circuit breaker in sight for the ASX. Futures trading points to 0.6% loss on the ASX 200 today, having dropped more than 5% last week, its worst week in mid 2022.

Wall Street's so-called "fear gauge", the Cboe VIX index, jumped to a seven-month high.

ANTHROPIC NOT IMMUNE IN TECH SELL-OFF

Even demand for market's new "must have" stock, Anthropic, which houses, among other things, the Claude chatbot, fizzled out to an extent.

Having leapt by 40% in its first few hours as a listed entity on Thursday, it's been on the slide ever since.

It fell another 9% on Friday, and at $US88.30/share it has subsided back towards the $US83 level of its debut.

A sizzling quarterly report earlier in the week from the scandal-tainted chip maker Nvidia failed to change sentiment about the risks associated with artificial intelligence and the massive, and often circular, investments being made in the sector.

"Nvidia reported what might have been one of the greatest quarters in corporate earnings history; they did everything right," Horizon Investment Services CEO Chuck Carlson told Reuters.

"A lot of people, myself included, were looking to the Anthropic debut to some extent as a clearing event, and it wasn't."

The Philadelphia SE Semiconductor Index, which has powered much of the broader stock market's gains in recent months, was up more than 100% in mid-June. It's now down almost 20% since the start of the year.

Among AI hyperscalers, Amazon fell 3.9%, while Meta dropped 5.9%.

The flight to safety saw the yield on the benchmark US 10-year treasury notes tumble more than 10 basis points, while the US dollar index was little changed as higher-than-expected inflation data reinforced expectations of rate hike from the Fed next month.

However, the Aussie dollar slipped again — largely due to the belief that the next move from the RBA would be a cut, rather than another hike and its relationship with commodity prices that are also falling.

COMMODITIES DUMPED

Gold fell for its fifth straight session, down 2.5% to $US3,532/ounce, its lowest level in more than a year. Bullion fell almost 10% over the week.

It was followed down by other precious metals; silver (-4.1%), platinum (-3.4%) and palladium (-2.1).

"Higher interest rates, stronger dollar, the prospects for ongoing high inflation, which prompts the Fed to continue to raise interest rates — all of those factors that we have been seeing — negatively affect gold," High Ridge Futures director of metals trading David Meger said.

"The ongoing AI-related equity sell-off is prompting some forced liquidations of gold positions to cover losses," he noted.

Copper, which has been linked as a metallic proxy for the AI trade, continued its recent slide, falling back to levels seen back in March.

The LME copper benchmark dropped 2.9% to $US12,160/tonne on Friday to be down almost 10% over the week.

"Copper could remain under pressure if funds continue to cut positions or the macro backdrop deteriorates," ING commodity strategist Ewa Manthey said in a note.

"But the price correction has moved much faster than physical conditions."
