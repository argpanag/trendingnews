Iran's IRGC Chief Says Tehran Is More Prepared Than Ever as Trump Warns Possible Military Action After 2027

TheTools · 28.11.2026 03:19 · source · permalink

Ahmad Vahidi's warning comes as Qatar and Pakistan mediate nuclear talks and tensions remain high around the Strait of Hormuz.

The Briefing

  • IRGC Commander Ahmad Vahidi declared Wednesday that Iran's forces are "stronger and more prepared than ever," warning that Tehran stands ready to push back any adversary and expand future confrontations well beyond the region.
  • President Trump told Fox News he is inclined to resume large-scale military operations against Iran after the start of 2027 if diplomacy fails, with Qatar and Pakistan serving as active mediators between the two sides.
  • U.S. fighter jets have carried out multiple limited strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz, including operations reported on Nov. 2 and Nov. 17. Washington described the latest attack as a self-defense measure in response to Iranian attacks on U.S. personnel and assets.

Vahidi's Warning at the Military Exhibition

Speaking Wednesday before an exhibition documenting the military escalations of 2025 and 2026 between Iran, the United States, and Israel, IRGC Commander-in-Chief Major General Ahmad Vahidi declared that his forces remain fully prepared for any renewed confrontation. "The IRGC is stronger and more prepared than ever, and is ready to push the enemy back a considerable distance," Vahidi said, according to Iranian news agency Tasnim.

Vahidi framed the warning as confidence born from battlefield results rather than defensiveness. He argued the year's engagements had not weakened Tehran — quite the opposite. "Victories must not lead us to stop," he was quoted as saying by Tasnim News Agency, "as major horizons toward a great future have opened up for the armed forces."

Iran Claims Washington Is Strategically Paralyzed

Beyond projecting readiness, the IRGC chief went further, characterizing the U.S. military as caught in deep strategic uncertainty following what he described as serious battlefield setbacks. Vahidi claimed that Iran's adversaries were experiencing defeat and strategic uncertainty, arguing that the prospect of Iranian retaliation had constrained Washington's military options. His remarks at the formal military exhibition carried deliberate institutional weight — timed to coincide with renewed American pressure and an approaching diplomatic deadline.

The comments echoed warnings he issued earlier this month, when he cautioned Washington against further military action, saying the "enemy must learn from repeated defeats" or face "devastating and painful strikes."

Trump's Deadline and the Risk of Re-Escalation

The backdrop to Vahidi's statements is a rapidly narrowing diplomatic window. Speaking to Fox News on Sunday, Nov. 22, President Donald Trump said he was inclined to launch additional military strikes against Iran after the New Year if the two countries failed to reach an agreement. Trump described Iranian negotiators as "professional tap dancers" and expressed mounting frustration with the pace of talks, while making clear that the United States would not allow Tehran to acquire a nuclear weapon.

Qatar has been facilitating the exchange of messages between Washington and Tehran, while Pakistan has also played a significant role in diplomatic efforts surrounding the conflict. Negotiations center on Iran's nuclear program and the future of the Strait of Hormuz, which has become a critical chokepoint for global oil trade since Iranian forces began targeting commercial and military vessels in the waterway.

Qeshm Island and the Cycle of Retaliation

Earlier this month, American warplanes struck an Iranian missile launch site on Qeshm Island, located in the Strait of Hormuz, is strategically positioned near one of the world's most important maritime shipping routes. Washington described the action as a "self-defense" measure in response to IRGC attacks targeting U.S. personnel and assets. Vice President JD Vance, while pointing to "good progress" on the diplomatic track, has acknowledged that negotiations with Tehran remain difficult to predict, and has stressed that Trump retains both military and economic options on the table.

Regional Tensions Spread Across the Gulf

The confrontation has extended well beyond the two principal actors. U.S. diplomatic missions in the Middle East issued security alerts urging Americans to exercise heightened vigilance amid Iranian attacks targeting U.S. assets in Bahrain and Kuwait and Houthi attacks against Saudi Arabia, including strikes on civilian airports and other critical infrastructure.

Vahidi himself signaled that the next phase of conflict could reach far beyond the current theater.

A Familiar Standoff, Now With Higher Stakes

The mutual posturing between Tehran and Washington is not new, but the conditions surrounding it have shifted considerably. After more than a year of escalating confrontation, including Israel's June 2025 strikes on Iranian nuclear and military facilities and the broader U.S.-Israeli military campaign launched against Iran on Feb. 28, 2026, neither side has secured a lasting resolution to the conflict. Iran's leadership is now leaning into that ambiguity, arguing that the absence of a clear American victory validates its resistance strategy.

Whether Vahidi's declarations reflect Iran's actual military capabilities or are intended to strengthen Tehran's negotiating position remains unclear. What is not in dispute is that the window for a diplomatic resolution is closing fast — and both governments are watching the calendar.
