  2. Who is Lindsay Clancy and what is she accused of?published at 14:08 BST
    14:08 BST

    Lindsay Clancy is a 36-year-old Massachusetts mother and former labour and delivery nurse who is on trial for the murder of her three young children in 2023.

    Clancy is accused of strangling her kids - Cora, five, Dawson, three, and Callan, who was eight months - with fitness bands in their Massachusetts home before jumping out of a second-floor window in January 2023.

    The jump paralysed Clancy from the waist down and she has attended the trial in a wheelchair.

    She is charged with three counts of first-degree murder and three counts of assault and battery. Prosecutors allege she made a calculated decision to intentionally kill her children.

    Lawyers for Clancy, who does not dispute carrying out the killings but has pleaded not guilty to murder charges, say she was experiencing postpartum psychosis at the time.

    Throughout her murder trial, Clancy has been held at Tewksbury Hospital, a state-run public psychiatric hospital in Massachusetts, about 20 miles (32km) from Boston.

    Her trial, which began in July and which has been live streamed to the public, has stirred intense national interest as well as debate about postpartum depression and the mental health of new mothers.

  4. Lindsay Clancy's trial - a recappublished at 13:59 BST
    13:59 BST

    Lindsay Clancy is accused of murdering her three small children at their Massachusetts home in January 2023.

    Clancy, a 36-year-old former nurse, jumped from a second-floor window after the killings, leaving her paralysed.

    She does not dispute killing her children - Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and Callan, eight months. But the defence argues that Clancy was experiencing postpartum psychosis - a severe mental health condition that can develop after childbirth - and that for this reason she should not be held criminally responsible for the killings.

    The prosecution claimed Clancy made a calculated, intentional decision to kill them and knew right from wrong in that moment. She is charged with three counts of first-degree murder.

    After hearing weeks of testimony from dozens of witnesses, the jury began deliberating in the high-profile case last Thursday. They have not yet been able to reach a verdict.

    Lindsay Clancy’s lawyer Kevin Reddington has just arrived at the court.

    Instead, he asks all proceedings now be handled in open court.

    The judge still needs to review that motion and it's unclear if he will approve it.

  6. Defence, prosecution, family members - key things to knowpublished at 13:44 BST
    13:44 BST

    Since Lindsay Clancy's murder trial began more than a month ago, we've heard from both prosecutors and defence lawyers, as well as more than 70 witnesses - including medical practitioners and members of Clancy's family - who have testified about the circumstances surrounding the 2023 killings of her three children.

    Here are some of the key things we've heard in the trial:

    Clancy's defence team does not dispute that she killed her children, but has argued that she should not be held criminally responsible as she was experiencing postpartum psychosis at the time.

    The prosecution has argued that they were murdered, that Clancy's actions were premeditated and intentional, and that she does not meet the legal definition of insanity.

    Members of Clancy's family, including her ex-husband, mother and ex-mother-in-law have all testified that she was a loving mother who was experiencing a serious mental breakdown.

    Forensic psychologists spoke on behalf of both the defence - arguing Clancy did not understand the wrongfulness of her acts - and the prosecution, arguing Clancy was mentally unwell but understood killing her kids was wrong.

  7. Outside court, there's much speculation about a jury splitpublished at 13:39 BST
    13:39 BST

    Ione Wells
    Reporting from court in Massachusetts

    “11-1” is a phrase I’ve heard a lot in the queue here outside the court where this trial is taking place. People are assuming that is how the twelve-person jury is currently split in opinion.

    That’s because of a dramatic moment yesterday where Lindsay Clancy’s defence lawyer accused one juror of not following the law that someone must be proved guilty beyond reasonable doubt, after a note to the judge said the person wasn’t following his instructions.

    But, just a reminder, we - and the lawyers - don’t actually know yet what the jury think, which way they’re swaying, or what the split is.

    All we know for certain is they’ve been deadlocked so far.

  8. What happened yesterday?published at 13:32 BST
    13:32 BST

    Day six of jury deliberations in the Lindsay Clancy trial ended much like the five before - with the jury reporting that they were unable to reach a verdict and a direction from the judge to continue trying.

    However, there was one complication: a note from the jury that one of its own members was holding up proceedings.

    Here's what happened on Thursday:

    • Clancy's attorney Kevin Reddington called for the removal of a juror after a note to the judge said they were not following the judges directions around reasonable doubt
    • The judge denied this request but brought the jury back for renewed instructions on reasonable doubt
    • Clancy appeared emotional, putting her head in her lap after news came in of the jury's note
    • Conversation appeared tense in the courtroom, with Clancy's lawyer slamming down papers and crossing his arms in frustration during conversations with the judge
    • It's unclear if there's a split in the jury room. But Reddington told press that the singled out juror would be "identified" in an "inquiry" by the judge Friday morning

    If no unanimous verdict is reached, the judge could declare a mistrial, meaning proceedings would end without a verdict.

  9. Long deliberations aren't unusual for trials like this, expert sayspublished at 13:12 BST
    13:12 BST

    Sakshi Venkatraman
    US reporter

    While long deliberations may add complications, they're not entirely unusual, former federal prosecutor Mark Chutkow tells me.

    "Six days of jury deliberation after a six-week trial with complex mental health issues is not unusual," he told me on Thursday.

    What is interesting, according to Chutkow, is the defence's adamant request to remove the juror who was seemingly holding up proceedings.

    As a reminder, the jury sent a note to the judge yesterday that one of 12 members was not properly following the judge's instructions. Clancy's lawyer asked to remove that person, which the judge denied.

    It "suggests that the attorneys for both sides might think that the holdout juror is the only one favoring conviction and the rest of the jurors favor acquittal", Chutkow tells me.

    That may or may not be the case, Chutkow adds, noting that the defence may have filed that motion looking ahead to an appeal.
