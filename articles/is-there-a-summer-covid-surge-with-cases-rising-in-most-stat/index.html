Is There a Summer COVID Surge? With Cases Rising in Most States, Watch for These Symptoms

TheTools · 22.09.2026 13:20 · source · permalink

Caroline Kee

Health reporter

Although foodborne illnesses stole the spotlight all summer, another highly contagious virus has been spreading in the United States: coronavirus. After a long lull, COVID-19 cases are on the rise again in most parts of the country.

So if you’ve been plagued by coughing and sniffles lately, it may be time to take a COVID test.

As of Sept. 16, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that COVID infections are growing or likely growing in 27 states, stable in 10 states and declining in 13 states.

COVID-19 activity is still considered low, nationally, the CDC says. However, rates are increasing in many states, especially in the South and West.

Texas, California, Florida and Washington are currently reporting the highest levels of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater, per CDC data. Last month, a school district in south Texas was forced to close for several days due to the rapid spread of COVID.

The current wave is not unexpected, according to experts. The SARS-CoV-2 virus can spread year-round, but it has settled into a distinctive seasonal pattern in the U.S. since the pandemic began.

“We’ve had two waves (every year). One in the middle to late summer, and the other during the winter,” Dr. William Schaffner, an infectious disease specialist and professor of medicine at Vanderbilt University Medical Center, tells TODAY.com.

While the country was focused on a massive outbreak of cyclospora in July, COVID was creeping up too, data show.

Although the respiratory virus may not be top of mind for many Americans, says Schaffner, it’s certainly spreading — just in time for back-to-school season.

Fortunately, the updated COVID vaccines for 2026-2027 are now available. But you may have questions about how to get one.

Is the U.S. facing a COVID surge and when will cases peak? Who should get the new booster and when? Here’s what we know about the latest trends and how to stay safe.

2026 Summer COVID-19 Surge

The U.S. is experiencing a late summer COVID-19 wave that’s expected to last into early fall, experts say. “The national data is beginning to tick up, but it’s not a substantial increase yet,” says Schaffner.

According to the latest CDC analysis, the overall level of COVID activity in wastewater was still “low” nationally during the week ending Sept. 12, 2026.

However, CDC data show 12 states were reporting moderate, high or very high levels of COVID activity during the same period:

  • Alabama
  • California
  • Florida
  • Hawaii
  • Kentucky
  • Massachusetts
  • Mississippi
  • Nevada
  • North Carolina
  • South Carolina
  • Texas
  • Washington

The latest data from WastewaterSCAN, a Stanford University-run infectious disease monitoring program, show that the level of SARS-CoV-2 activity in wastewater is “high” nationwide, with a significant upward trend in the last 21 days.

“We’ve been seeing a pretty steady increase since late June across the (U.S.),” Amanda Bidwell, WastewaterSCAN Scientific Program Manager, tells TODAY.com. By late August, SARS-CoV-2 concentrations had jumped up 140% compared to July 2026.

“In the West and South we’ve surpassed the peak concentrations observed during the winter peak (2025-2026),” Bidwell added.

At least 40 states and territories in the U.S. are experiencing active COVID outbreaks, according to an Sept. 7 report from Pandemic Mitigation Collaborative’s data tracker.

“That is troubling, especially if you’re in one of those areas,” Michael Hoerger, Ph.D., assistant professor at Tulane University School of Medicine who leads the PMC.

However, the experts emphasize that the current wave appears to be less severe than what we’ve seen in previous years. In 2025, the U.S. experienced an intense summer surge fueled by the “Stratus” variant, which lasted into the fall.

“We are still seeing lower SARS-CoV-2 concentrations compared to this time last year,” says Bidwell.

During the week ending Sept. 12, 5% of COVID-19 tests came back positive — this same time last year, the weekly test positivity rate was about 11%, per CDC data.

It’s unclear how severe this late summer wave could become or when it may peak. “We’re expecting transmission to peak about a week into September,” says Hoerger, adding that the regional spread could shift as well.

Only time will tell, the experts note. As temperatures drop and millions of children return to school, this could create more opportunities for the virus to spread.

“We need to be very cautious,” says Schaffner.

Which COVID-19 Variants Are Spreading?

The current COVID wave is being driven by a mixture of familiar variants, says Bidwell. The latest WastewaterSCAN data is detecting XFG, LF.7, NB.1.8.1, and BA.3.2.

XFG.1.1 and its sublineages accounted for nearly half of all cases in the U.S. in July, according to the latest data from the CDC.

XFG, aka Stratus, is a highly contagious recombinant strain which overtook the Nimbus (NB.1.8.1) variant last summer, Dr. Albert Ko, professor of public health, epidemiology and medicine at Yale School of Public Health, tells TODAY.com.

“It’s still within the family of past omicron variants (from) this past year, so it’s not a big jump in terms of being different. That’s probably why we’re not seeing a big peak this summer,” Ko adds.

Less than 10% of cases are being fueled by the BA.3.2 variant, aka Cicada, per CDC data. The heavily mutated strain emerged earlier this year and began spreading rapidly worldwide. However, Cicada hasn’t seemed to take off in the U.S., the experts note.

While there hasn’t been a major shift or mutation in the latest variants, the virus may still evolve in the future, Hoerger notes.

“The fact that we are not seeing an entirely new variant doesn’t mean COVID is not going to cause severe disease,” Ko says.

COVID Symptoms 2026

The symptoms of the COVID-19 variants fueling the summer wave are similar to those caused by other omicron strains. These usually appear within two to 14 days of exposure to the virus.

Common signs of COVID-19 in 2026, per the CDC, include:

  • Cough
  • Sore throat
  • Congestion
  • Fatigue
  • Fever
  • Chills
  • Shortness of breath
  • Headache
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Diarrhea

The symptoms of COVID and other common respiratory viruses are very similar, which is why testing is important.

If you test negative using a rapid at-home antigen test, the CDC recommends taking another test after 48 hours to be confident that you aren’t infected.

Most people recover from COVID-19 on their own, but some groups are at higher risk of severe illness or hospitalization, says Schaffner.

These include adults over the age of 65, infants, individuals with weak immune systems or underlying conditions, and people who have never gotten a COVID vaccine.

The infection can be treated with antivirals, which reduce symptom severity and duration when taken early.

COVID Quarantine Guidelines for 2026

If you get sick with COVID-19, the CDC recommends staying home and isolating from others who aren’t sick until you feel better.

You can go back to your normal activities once your symptoms have improved and you’ve been fever-free without medication for at least 24 hours, the CDC says.

While recovering, the experts recommend practicing social distancing, especially from immunocompromised people, and wearing a well-fitted N95 respirator or KN95.

If you don’t have COVID-19, you can take actions to protect yourself, says Schaffner. These include wearing a mask in crowded indoor settings, avoiding contact with sick people and staying up to date on vaccines.

Is the New COVID-19 Booster Available?

For the last few years, the COVID-19 vaccine has been reformulated every summer to provide protection against the latest strains circulating.

In May, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration voted to recommend the updated COVID vaccine for 2026-2027 target the XFG variant, NBC News reported previously.

In August, the FDA approved the monovalent 2026-2027 COVID vaccines from Moderna, Pfizer-BioNTech, and Novavax-Sanofi.

“The new shots will cover what’s anticipated to be circulating this fall and winter,” says Schaffner.

The updated COVID shots are expected to be rolled out at pharmacies, clinics and hospitals in September, experts say.

“It could be later if there’s any kind of snag,” says Hoerger. “That’s frustrating because we’ve got outbreaks happening now,” he adds.

Last year, the CDC shifted its COVID-19 vaccine guidelines from a universal recommendation for everyone ages 6 months or older to a shared clinical-decision making model.

“This means you should talk with your healthcare provider, whether it’s a doctor or nurse, or a pharmacist first,” says Ko.

The CDC has not yet issued recommendations around the 2026-2027 COVID vaccines.

The FDA has approved the new shots for all adults ages 65 and older and younger people who have one or more underlying health conditions. However, anyone else who falls outside of the approval criteria can still get a vaccine “off-label” after having a conversation with their provider, Ko explains.

The COVID vaccine protects people from severe illness, hospitalization and death, per the CDC. It can also lower the risk of developing long COVID, Hoerger adds.

That’s why most medical experts recommend that everyone who is eligible get a new COVID booster in September or October. It’s especially important for high-risk groups and pregnant women to get vaccinated, Schaffner adds.

“At this point, many of us have been exposed multiple times to COVID, so we are building up immunity. But there is a benefit to getting vaccines, especially before the winter,” says Ko.
