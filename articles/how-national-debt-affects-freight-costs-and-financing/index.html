How National Debt Affects Freight Costs and Financing

TheTools · 18.10.2026 10:42 · source · permalink

Key takeaways

  • U.S. gross federal debt passed $40 trillion in August 2026; about $32.4 trillion of it is held by the public.

  • Net interest on the debt now runs above $1 trillion a year and eats roughly 18.5% of federal revenue, a record.

  • Heavy Treasury borrowing competes with private borrowers for capital ("crowding out"), adding upward pressure on interest rates, though the near-term rate story is still mostly inflation and the Fed.

  • Freight feels it first: truck loans start around 7.9% to 8.9%, Class 8 orders are down year over year, small carriers are under strain, and warehouse development costs more to finance.

  • Anyone planning a fleet replacement cycle or a long-term warehouse lease should model both Fed policy and the structural deficit.

On last week's Freightonomics show, we discussed CBO rule-of-thumb numbers on how much an increase in the interest rate (as shown by the Effective Federal Funds Rate) would impact the debt. I had in my mind publicly held debt when I said it was somewhere in the neighborhood of $34T. Turns out I transposed a couple numbers and was thinking 34.2 (getting old stinks). It's actually $32.4T, which is still not good. I looked this morning to see what the total U.S. debt total currently is. That number is worse yet.

Source: usdebtclock.org, 09/23/2026

Gross federal debt crossed $40 trillion in mid-August 2026, months earlier than most forecasters had penciled in, as the Washington Post reported at the time, and it has held in that neighborhood since, touching $40.12 trillion in Treasury's own daily debt-to-the-penny data as of September 21. Of that total, $32.4 trillion is debt held by the public and $7.7 trillion is intragovernmental, per Congress' Joint Economic Committee Debt Dashboard. Debt held by the public alone is now running close to 100% of GDP (Debt Clock's 122.75% is gross federal debt, public plus intragovernmental), and the Congressional Budget Office projects that ratio to reach 120% by 2036 and 175% within thirty years, surpassing the 1946 postwar record of 106% along the way. The debt has roughly doubled in under a decade.

Before we go further, we need to talk about scale. "Trillion" looks fine on paper, but it is difficult to wrap our heads around just how big that number is. To be perfectly honest, one almost needs a metaphor to understand the metaphor, but I'm going to try my best. I don't want to bore you with too many conversions, but we need to establish some foundations for this. Let's assume that each dollar of federal debt is one drop of water, 1/20th of a milliliter, or 1/100th of a teaspoon. On my kid's medicine syringe, 1 tsp = 5 ml; so that math checks out. Following our earlier assumption, that means each teaspoon is a $100 bill of debt. According to World Aquatics, a long-run Olympic-sized swimming pool is 50m x 25m x 2.5m or about 164'1" x 82' x 8'2". This means it holds 3,125,000 liters of water, or 825,538 gallons. That means each pool holds 62.5 billion drops. For a federal debt of $40.1T, that means almost 642 pools. Imagine trying to swim that, much less drink that much. If you drank a gallon of water a day, it would take you 2,260 years to down all that. (See what I meant about the metaphor needing a metaphor?)
