ACT's top cop backs controversial laws in battle against bikies

TheTools · 27.09.2026 04:42 · source · permalink

  • Topic:Crime

Topic:Crime

The Comancheros are one of five outlaw motorcycle gangs in the ACT. (Supplied: Australian Federal Police)

In short:

The ACT's chief police officer is publicly backing anti-consorting laws.

The laws are in place in most other Australian jurisdictions, but Labor-led ACT governments have argued they conflict with the Human Rights Act.

Police and experts argue now is the time to introduce the laws in the territory.

ACT Chief Police Officer Scott Lee has no doubt about the damage outlaw bikie gangs are causing in Canberra.

"Outlaw motorcycle gangs exist for one reason, and that's in relation to organised crime"

While raw numbers in the Canberra region do not match those in Sydney and Melbourne, there has been a notable increase in the past few years.

Deputy Commissioner Lee said there were currently five outlaw motorcycle gangs (OMCGs) in the ACT, with about 45 members and approximately 20 associates.

ACT Chief Police Officer Scott Lee says the growing number of OMCG members is concerning. (ABC News: Ian Cutmore)

"So that number has grown, which is a concern for us," he said.

ACT Policing is stepping up its fight against this growing threat in a bid to disrupt the gangs' criminal activities.

Land of opportunity?

For decades the ACT had one established bikie gang that ruled the roost — the Rebels.

But since the early 2010s, most Rebels have "patched over" to other groups, including the Bandidos, the Hells Angels, the Finks, and the Comancheros.

Several now have established "chapters" or bases in the ACT, rather than using Canberra solely as a transit corridor.

Deputy Commissioner Lee thinks the primary reason is that Canberra is a growing city, and market.

"Where you have those prosperous communities, they're attractive propositions for investment, but they're also attractive propositions for crime," he said.

The Bandidos are one of five outlaw motorcycle gangs in the ACT. (ABC News: Henry Zwartz)

Associate Professor Mark Lauchs has been researching OMCG activities in Australia for decades.

"They're increasing in Canberra, because it's much harder for them to operate elsewhere in the country," he said.

Anti-consorting laws

One measure Deputy Commissioner Lee advocates for is anti-consorting laws, which have been a controversial debate in the ACT.

"We support that legislation," he said.

"We've seen the benefits of it in other jurisdictions where it has an ability to disrupt associations, or certainly overt associations, and the ability for these people to gather."

Deputy Commissioner Lee emphasised that anti-consorting laws preventing outlaw motorcycle gangs from meeting in public were not a magical cure-all.

"OMCGs continue to exist in every jurisdiction," he said.

"But where you have those real overt showings of OMCGs, what we have with the national runs, it would disrupt that type of activity."

ACT Chief Police Officer Scott Lee is advocating for anti-consorting laws, but says they are not a cure-all to OMCGs. (ABC News: Ian Cutmore)

The ACT has repeatedly resisted introducing these laws, with previous Labor-led governments arguing they conflict with the ACT Human Rights Act.

Associate Professor Lauchs agreed that "good, strong, well-targeted" anti-consorting laws were needed, but he said it was reasonable the ACT had resisted until now.

"The arguments [against anti-consorting laws] aren't wrong. There are human rights issues there that have to be addressed," he said.

"However, being right is not enough in this case, because everybody else did it.

"So, you became the eventual inevitable victim of everybody else's laws."

An attractive market

Sergeant Mark Alexander leads the specialist ongoing Task Force Invidia, which investigates, infiltrates and disrupts the gangs.

He said the groups were "really sophisticated, and their operational security is quite a challenge for us to try and monitor".

"They are an undercurrent and a really strong presence in the ACT in illicit commodities,"

Associate Professor Mark Lauchs is an expert in outlaw motorcycle gangs. (LinkedIn: Mark Lauchs)

Those commodities include the usual suspects — drugs, guns, weapons and extortion.

Associate Professor Lauchs argued that the illicit drug trade was the big draw for outlaw gangs.

"Is Canberra the greatest per capita usage in Australia? No, but it's a sufficiently high usage rate that there's a marketplace," he said.

"And when there's a marketplace for cocaine, there's going to be a marketplace for bikies to be involved."

'Billions and billions'

This illicit economy is substantial; earlier this month, police arrested several ACT gang members alleged to have trafficked 70kg of cocaine with a street value of approximately $10 million. 

"When you extrapolate that out to the [national] illicit drug market, we're talking billions and billions of dollars in terms of the illicit commodities that are moved into our country," Deputy Commissioner Lee said.

He explained the gangs had established transnational connections to overseas chapters in Europe, South America and other source regions.

ACT Policing Sergeant Mark Alexander says aside from top brass in OMCGs, most members do not make very much money. (ABC News: Adam Shirley)

Chapters are also constantly trying to recruit new members, particularly vulnerable young men seeking "status".

"They see outlaw motorcycle gangs as money and power," Sergeant Alexander said.

But police say except for OMCG top brass, most gang members take extreme risks for little reward.

"They are not making a lot of money. They are very poor. Sometimes they will struggle to pay the rent,"

Disrupting the cycle

New proposed laws may see a ban on OMCG members wearing the colours or patches of the gang. (Supplied: Australian Federal Police)

New laws announced by the ACT government in February will give police more power to search for illegal firearms without needing a warrant.

Another change announced by the ACT government in March this year — just prior to a national Bandidos meeting in Canberra — was a ban on 'colours' or 'patches'. 

"Colours are obviously worn to intimidate the community. They bring with it a connection to that OMCG,"

"It is something I've been supportive of with government, and I've advocated with government for the banning of colours here in the ACT."

Both proposals are yet to be debated in the ACT Legislative Assembly.

Constantly evolving

Officers rely on anonymous tips from the public to police outlaw motorcycle gangs. (Supplied: Australian Federal Police)

Whatever laws change, police say bikie gangs will adapt, adopting new tactics and strategies to keep business going.

"We are seeing a more underground meeting[s]… or it's in public areas, bars, clubs, restaurants, but they're not wearing their colours," Sergeant Mark Alexander said.

So, in the police's evolving fight against the gangs, they are clear one thing will never change: the need for public assistance.

"We can't be on every street corner keeping an eye on these people,"

"We rely on anonymous tips."

And Deputy Commissioner Lee urged the public to come forward.

"We need your help."
