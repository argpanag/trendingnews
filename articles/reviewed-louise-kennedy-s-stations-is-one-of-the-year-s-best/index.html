Reviewed: Louise Kennedy's Stations is one of the year's best

TheTools · 26.09.2026 06:00 · source · permalink

There's a lot to be said for writers and artists who perfect a particular kind of narrative. Those who recognise that not every piece of storytelling has to be some convoluted exercise in experimentation or postmodern trickery.

Hilary Mantel, for example, will be remembered for the way she evoked some of the most pivotal moments in European history. Seamus Heaney will be remembered for the dignity and complexity with which he imbued Irish rural life. Sally Rooney will be remembered for her scalpel-like dissections of post-Celtic Tiger Irish society. And Louise Kennedy, I dare say, will be remembered for her stories about lovers who are bound to one another, in spite – and sometimes because – of their irreconcilable differences.

If the unfurling of the relationship at the centre of Kennedy’s new novel Stations feels familiar, then, it’s only because we are in the company of a writer who is at her most comfortable when she is pulling apart the meaty chambers of the human heart. The story follows Róisín McCaffrey and Red Camelin as they grow together, drift apart, reconcile and finally lose touch over the course of almost 30 years.

"After he left she remembered the schoolbook Mags had taken in, I love Red Camelin printed inside the cover. IF ** KI NG LO VE RE DC AM EL IN she spelled as she fell asleep."

Kennedy’s protagonists shine here – feel real – precisely because she resists any temptation to simplify or make them palatable. With all their messy quirks and foibles intact, they read more like the vivid character studies one would expect to find in a Gay Talese profile than fictional constructs from a story about 1980s Ireland.

Red is introduced as the rebellious, troubled, brilliant eldest son of an outwardly conservative (and likely abusive) family dynamic. Róisín begins the novel as a refugee of sorts; one whose father was forced to move their family south to escape the danger he encountered as a Catholic member of the Royal Ulster Constabulary (RUC).

"Her parents had sold the south to her as a sanctuary for beleaguered six-county Catholics but it turned out most southerners were either uninterested or appalled by the north. Even the hunger strike was virtually ignored."

The purity, the spareness of Kennedy’s prose is something she has perfected over three books, and can sometimes conceal – at other times accentuate – the depth and lucidity of her observations. About place. About the claustrophobic nuances of an Ireland fractured by the bitter complexities of its own contemporary history.

Kennedy’s eye for historical detail and her way of drawing the reader in to inhabit a particular time have already been remarked upon in her sensational debut novel Trespasses, but they are every bit as vivid and well-executed here.

'A pair of New Romantics in white shirts’ makes an appearance at a school disco. "… Since the Stardust inferno," Kennedy tells us. "Exits were always unlocked."

Stations is, in many ways, more subtle than Trespasses, though its moments of heartbreak are all the more potent for their quietude. It is delightful to bear witness to this milestone in Kennedy’s career. I only hope that she can continue to devastate as only she knows how.

Stations is published by Bloomsbury
