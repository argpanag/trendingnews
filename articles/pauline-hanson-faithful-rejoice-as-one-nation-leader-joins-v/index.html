Teal challengers urge VEC to investigate donations to Liberal Party

R

By Rebecca Trigger

By Rebecca Trigger

Teal independents for Kew and Hawthorn have written to the Victorian Electoral Commissioner asking for an urgent investigation into donations to the Victorian Liberal Party in the lead-up to the election.

The ABC earlier this week revealed companies owned or linked by directorships to property developers and the construction and investment industries had made a series of donations which, in total, were well above the legal cap permitted to be given to one political party by an individual.

Electoral laws cap donations between April 15 and November 28 at just over $5,000 per person or company, or “related bodies corporate”.

In one instance, the ABC found a property developer had used three companies he solely owned and which were registered at his firm's address to donate $5,000 each, as well as making a personal donation in his own name.

In another, 20 firms linked to two developers by directorship and/or shareholdings donated close to $100,000 dollars total over the course of a week.

However, the VEC says common ownership by a single individual does not mean, by itself, that a group of companies would meet the strict criteria of “related bodies corporate” as defined by the existing laws.

A spokesperson for one tranche of donations told the ABC each company acted with integrity and full disclosure, and that each was lawful.

The Victorian Liberal Party has been contacted for comment. But it has previously said in relation to the donations: "The Liberal Party operates in compliance with the requirements of the Electoral Act”.

But teal independent Sophie Torney, who is challenging Liberal leader Jess Wilson in the seat of Kew on Saturday, has asked VEC Commissioner Sven Bluemmel to examine each transaction identified by the ABC, and identify the source of the funds to ascertain whether the donations should have been counted together.

She is also asking the VEC to investigate whether the donations complied with another section of the Act, 270, which states:

"A person must not enter into, or carry out, a scheme, whether alone or with any other person, with the intention of circumventing a prohibition or requirement under this Part."

Policy in focus: Treaty

J

By Judd Boaz

By Judd Boaz

Victoria's Treaty has loomed as one of the major talking points in the Victorian election.

Labor signed Australia's first modern treaty agreement with the country's Indigenous people in 2025. Treaty legislation led to the creation of the new democratically elected body Gellung Warl.

When members of parliament introduce new bills in Victoria, they are now required to include a statement on how the bill fits with the principles of Treaty and whether the First Peoples' Assembly was consulted. However, Gellung Warl has no powers to veto Victorian policy or legislation.

The government has also invested in the Self Determination Fund through $65 million of initial funding over three tranches, which is overseen by Gellung Warl.

The Coalition has pledged to introduce legislation to repeal Treaty within its first 100 days in office if elected and move back to a model where policy ideas on Indigenous affairs are managed by a department reporting to a minister.

Parliamentary Budget Office analysis shows the opposition's planned scrapping of Treaty would return roughly $948 million to the budget over a decade from 2025/26 to 2035/36.

One Nation has similarly pledged to scrap Treaty, while the Greens strongly support keeping Victoria's Treaty and the signing of further local and statewide treaties.

The Coalition and One Nation's position of tearing up Treaty has dismayed many Indigenous community leaders, who have argued it would be a backwards step for First Nations people seeking to overcome the disadvantages created by colonisation.

Explaining the Coalition's proposed stamp duty cuts

J

By Judd Boaz

By Judd Boaz

I saw that the costing documents released by the PBO for the liberals around the stamp duty exception also mentions that the First Home Owners Grant would be removed, have they actually costed this, and if so why is no-one talking about this?

- Aiden

I saw that the costing documents released by the PBO for the liberals around the stamp duty exception also mentions that the First Home Owners Grant would be removed, have they actually costed this, and if so why is no-one talking about this?

- Aiden

Hi Aiden,

As you mention, the Coalition plans to lift the stamp duty threshold for first home buyers to $1 million incrementally over the next four years.

The policy is currently costed at $4.6 billion over the next 10 years, and involves scrapping the $10,000 First Home Owner Grant.

What it actually means for home buyers, though, depends on a range of factors.

The First Home Owner Grant only applies for new homes up to a value of $750,000.

October figures from data firm Cotality show Melbourne's median dwelling price is $773,027.

Under Victoria's stamp duty laws, that means the buyer of a median home would need to pay $2,870 plus 6 per cent of the dutiable value above $130,000 — or about $41,452 in stamp duty.

With the Coalition's plans to raise the threshold, it would mean someone buying their first home at the median price would be $41,452 better off.

People buying a new home valued at $600,000 or less are already exempt from duty, and so would not benefit from the Coalition policy and would miss out on the grant.

The Coalition stamp duty policy comes at a cost, with the PBO costings predicting the budget will be $781.4 million worse off by the end of the Coalition's first term, if they are elected.

Seats in focus: Sunbury

C

By Casey Briggs, ABC Chief Elections and Data Analyst

By Casey Briggs, ABC Chief Elections and Data Analyst

Sunbury was created ahead of the 2014 election, when the electoral boundaries in Melbourne's west were reconfigured to account for large population growth.

Its territory was previously covered by the regional seat of Macedon and the outer-suburban seat of Yuroke, both traditional Labor seats.

Current member Josh Bull has been the seat's only representative.

Bull's first victory in 2014 was somewhat tight but his margin blew out to more than 14 per cent on a two-party basis in Labor's landslide 2018 election.

In 2022, however, it came back down and his primary vote dropped to 43 per cent.

The seat is widely considered to be vulnerable going into this election and its demographics indicate it may be particularly suited to One Nation.
