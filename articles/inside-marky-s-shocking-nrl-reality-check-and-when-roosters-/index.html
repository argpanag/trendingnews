Inside Marky’s ‘shocking’ NRL reality check... and when Roosters great knew he was ‘freakish’

Mark Nawaqanitawase can expect a civic reception when he traipses back into the world of rugby union in the coming weeks.

Before then, the mercurial Roosters winger is hopeful of giving the rugby league world his very own send-off, as the ‘sadness’ of his NRL departure begins to sink in.

As Nawaqanitawase prepares to farewell the Roosters with the ultimate parting gift – a potential premiership trophy from Sunday’s grand final against Newcastle – former club enforcer Jared Waerea-Hargreaves has reflected on the moment he realised the 26-year-old had the gumption to be one of the game’s best.

In this foxsports.com.au feature, Nawaqanitawase also speaks about his emotions departing the NRL, while another club great Cooper Cronk shares why the rugby-bound star has revolutionised the NRL in the space of two-and-a-bit years.

THE HARD-NOSED ROOSTERS ENVIRONMENT THAT CREATED A STAR

Irrespective of the reception Nawaqanitawase receives when he joins Japanese rugby union side Saitama Wild Knights, it will likely be a very different scenario to his highly publicised code defection from the 15-man game to the Sydney Roosters midway through the 2024 NRL season.

Back then, Roosters enforcer Waerea-Hargreaves welcomed Marky Mark to the club in his own indomitable style: with opens arms as well as open hands.

“I guess when you’ve such a talent that comes to the club, you want to expose him to what he’s about to endure,” the Roosters premiership-winning forward reflected with a hearty chuckle this week.

Nawaqanitawase had just represented Australia in rugby sevens at the 2024 Paris Olympics before joining the Roosters on a two-year deal from 2025. He was brought into train with the Roosters much earlier than that, with his first day involving some wrestling sessions alongside team personnel.

In time he would be pitched against club hard men JWH and Dylan Napa on the wrestling mats, and they were sure to welcome the new recruit accordingly – showing him (in the most endearing way) that wrestling took on another intensity in professional rugby league.

“You talk about the intensity of rugby league and the intensity of sessions. That was one of the early sessions that Mark, myself, Naps and few other guys got to enjoy,” Waerea-Hargreaves said.

“It was just showing a sign of respect and trying to train at an intensity; going after each other and set the standards. You’re trying to show a new guy that you’re welcoming him into the club and a way of showing him respect and asking him, ‘Are you capable enough of enduring this for as long as you want?’.”

Two things were established on the wrestling mats that day. In Mark’s own words the sessions confirmed he was “shocking” at wrestling and gave him room to improve. Secondly, the new recruit wasn’t shy on spirit or toughness.

“He didn’t want to quit,” JWH remembered. “He took it as a lesson. He was super positive about it and saying, ‘OK, this is the standard’.

“The rugby union sessions probably aren’t as intense as the league ones. But we wanted to welcome him in with open arms. All I can remember was him taking it as a positive thing.”

Nawaqanitawase’s mental fortitude ultimately gave Roosters coach Trent Robinson ‘no option’ but to consider him for first grade before the end of that 2024 season, and much earlier than originally planned.

The new recruit quickly impressed Roosters coaching staff and players with his meticulous approach, as well as some of his otherworldly instinctive talents on the training paddock.

“He signed with us towards the back end of the season, and you knew that he was a hell of talent,” JWH reflected. “When you got to see him day-in, day-out on the training paddock, it wasn’t a surprise when you saw what he did on the weekend.

“I was fortunate to play with him in the one game at the back end of 2024. He scored on debut against the Bunnies in the corner.”

While Nawaqanitawase boasted a stacked resume in rugby union, his potential in the NRL remained relatively unknown before 2024 – even if he had spent a short amount of time training in a Roosters pathways program 10 years earlier.

The eventual cross-code international made rugby union his focus from a young age, revealing this week that his mum “would never let me” trial in representative rugby league sides in those formative days.

In any case, Waerea-Hargreaves knew pretty quickly that his then-new teammate had the chops to make it in the NRL, and to be special on his own terms.

“If anything, he’s done it on his own. When you’ve got a guy that can produce such amazing things, he pretty much picks himself. Robbo couldn’t not pick him. I know he really wanted to give him an opportunity or taste of first grade before going into the 2025 season, and lucky enough he was able to do that,” he said.

“To see the freakish ability and to see what this guy does week in, week out – I don’t know who to compare him to.”

THE COOL HEAD BEHIND FREAKISH ON-FIELD TALENTS

The consensus is that Mark Nawaqanitawase has helped develop a new blueprint for NRL wingers with his ability to turn games upside down through 2026.

Whether it be at club level, or State of Origin, the Roosters flyer is treated as a different kind of threat from opposition defences.

According to Roosters premiership winner Cooper Cronk, Nawaqanitawase’s instinctive reads are among the most incisive in the NRL. Combined with impressive handling skills, it makes the 26-year-old dangerous any time he has the ball in his hands.

“You almost have to treat him like a halfback,” Cronk said. “As soon as there’s space you just felt something was going to happen.

“When the great players have time, they tear you apart. The thought has always been when there is a great playmaker, shut down their time and space. Mark is in that category now because if Greg Marzhew and the left edge defence of the Newcastle Knights, if he gets a ball and they freeze, it’s like they put concrete in their shoes.

“Mark will tear you apart. He will step off his right, he’ll push the outside, he’ll flick the ball around. He just carries a rugby league ball like it’s a ping-pong ball and can just hold it out like a magical wand.”

For Waerea-Hargreaves, who has regularly spent time with Nawaqanitawase in a development role at the Sydney Roosters this season, the 26-year-old’s freakish ability comes down to his demeanour.

“The man he is, the humility in the man – he’s such a positive guy. This Fijian nature that comes out in him, it’s pretty special,” he said.

“That’s where I believe he gets this sixth sense and the ability to perform like this. Because of his personality. It’s his personality that expresses his talent.

“He never really thinks anything is too hard. There’s nothing mentally really stopping him doing anything.

“He was in his prime when he was playing for the Wallabies. He was playing for the Sevens, he was playing Wallabies. I think his mentality about things, the way he does things is in such a positive manner. That’s what got him the opportunity at the end of 2024, and the past two seasons: his personality, the way he expresses himself as a man, and the humble and positive man he is, when he steps out on the footy field, it shows who he is.

“That Fijian, freakish talent that he has, we’ve always seen what Fijian players are capable of producing on the field. He’s got that in spades.”

The Fijian outlook means Nawaqanitawase’s approach to Sunday’s grand final isn’t being clouded by outside noise whatsoever. He is simply treating the occasion as another game, and another opportunity to put his best foot forward.

“I just do my job,” he replied this week when asked about the pressure of game day. “It’s another week so I don’t think it’s a different game too much.

“The atmosphere will be amazing and I know the Knights will be up for it. We just have to make sure we do our job and the rest will do itself.

“I try not to buy into the big thing as much, because it might be a distraction. I don’t really get caught up in last game or grand final.”

The other unspoken element in Nawaqanitawase’s freakish ability is his upbringing. This week, he credited all the backyard footy he played as a kid being a key factor in his free-spirited approach.

“My childhood and my upbringing is definitely a big reason, I think, as to why I can have some fun out there. I have a lot of fun and try new things and it has made me who I am,” he said.

“My parents just said, ‘Go outside and play’. Mum definitely encouraged it and I was lucky enough to grow up in the same neighbourhood as my cousins so I was playing every day.”

WHY YOU’D NEVER RULE OUT AN NRL RETURN

When NRL boss Peter V’landys interrupted an interview with Nawaqanitawase during this week’s Dally M Medal gala celebrations, telling viewers that he cannot be lost to the game, it doused fuel on hopes that the Roosters flyer could one day return to top-flight rugby league.

Before then the cross-code star has plenty in front of him, first in Japanese rugby union, and then to fulfil his dream of playing for the Wallabies in the 2027 Rugby World Cup.

Nawaqanitawase said the penny hadn’t quite dropped on him leaving the NRL when foxsports.com.au spoke to him this week, but he was hopeful of soaking up the week’s celebrations and reminiscing with team mates during quieter moments, including over a coffee.

“It’s the last week with everyone, which is sad. But it hasn’t hit me yet; I’m still excited,” he said. “It’s going to be a long week, so I’m looking forward to it.

“I want to go out there and do everything I can.”

For JWH, Marky Mark’s presence in this week’s grand final is tinged with some sadness, as he will leave an enormous hole in the NRL and at the Roosters.

“To see him leave at the end of the week, it’s such a shame. But who knows. He’s a guy that loves a challenge, and he’s going to go fulfil some dreams in rugby union, with the Wallabies, travelling the world.

“Who knows, he’s still young. Fingers crossed he comes back to the Roosters.”

Irrespective of Nawaqanitawase’s future, Waerea-Hargreaves said his transformation from wrestling partner to NRL superstar – and a good friend – filled him with admiration, both as a player and a person.

“To see him transition into the player he is at the moment – just the instinctive, natural ability, to see how much he’s developed – it’s impressive,” he said.

“There’s not too many athletes that can do what he does, and he does it every single week. He’s a hell of a talent.

“It’s kind of sad to see the club and the game losing such a talent. Gee I wish he was staying.”
