I’m a Swiftie and even I think these new songs are terrible

TheTools · 28.09.2026 06:28 · source · permalink

The Life of a Showgirl: Encore Edition is just as derivative and flat as the original album. I’d rather see the wedding pics, please Taylor

2/5

It’s been 11 months since Taylor Swift released her last album, The Life of a Showgirl, and just three since her last single. Showgirl was critically panned and, despite breaking the record for the biggest-selling debut week for an album in history, spawned no tangible hits. It is also derided by many of her own fans.

So Swift has done what Swift does best: write a few more songs for a “deluxe” version and “surprise release” it, ready to win those same fans back. What she doesn’t seem to have realised is that the music must be good to do so – and the songs on The Life of a Showgirl: Encore Edition are just as derivative and flat as those that populated the original.

The Encore Edition features four new tracks, written after Showgirl’s release in October 2025 and produced in Sweden with Max Martin and Shellback. Taking the lead is Patient Zero, a violin-driven slice of melancholia about a toxic ex already believed to be the actor and singer Joe Jonas, whom she dated in 2008. But a more realistic reading looks to a 2024 Los Angeles Times article which labelled Swift “patient zero for emotional contagion” in relation to her relationship with the NFL player Travis Kelce (they married in July, and I don’t know one Swift fan who wouldn’t rather have been treated to a picture of her wedding dress than new music).

Cleveland! is bouncier and zippier. The full power of Swift’s vengeful pen comes into focus as she lambasts her critics who say she’s no longer hot enough, dresses “like trash” despite her billions, and has an “annoying” smile and awful dance moves. As she sings on the chorus, Kelce loves her despite these supposed failings: “My baby likes what he likes […] So, hate all you like / This is the love of my life.” It’s striking that the love songs Swift writes for Kelce are rooted in public perception rather than her inner feelings; she cares more about how the world sees them together than how she sees him. This is miles away from the sensitive songwriter who, while dating English actor Joe Alwyn between 2016 and 2023, wrote some of 21st century pop’s most intensely romantic love songs: King of My Heart, New Year’s Day, Call It What You Want.

Pink Clouding is an unconvincing attempt to echo the pop brilliance of Swift’s earlier work with Martin and Shellback, especially on 1989 (2014). Its lyrics shudder along at a stop-start pace, building to a chorus about her feeling “pink cloudin’ from the thrill of it / Skydiving as you wrapped your arms around my body”. The bridge almost sounds like a bad rap, the sort that X Factor contestants would randomly insert into auditions back in 2012 to convince Simon Cowell they were hip.

The final song, Babylon, is the strongest – but one dreams of snatching its production duties from Martin and Shellback’s meddling, messy hands and handing them back to Swift’s early collaborator, Nathan Chapman, who understood that her strengths will always lie in country-pop. There have been rumours that Swift’s next proper album will be country – she’s been holding meetings with Nashville bigwigs and got back on to country radio with her Oscar-touted, yee-hawing theme song for Toy Story 5, I Knew It, I Knew You. After listening to The Life of a Showgirl: Encore Edition, I’m praying those rumours are true.

Following thinly veiled criticism from fellow pop stars who consider her too commercially competitive (Billie Eilish, Charli XCX, Olivia Rodrigo), it is telling that Swift waited until one week after rising country star Ella Langley broke the US record for the longest number one of all time with Choosin’ Texas to release this. She knows she would have been accused of being a selfish, vindictive woman-hater otherwise. Smart.

For, this being Swift, The Life of a Showgirl: Encore Edition will immediately rush to the top of the charts. Therein lies the biggest problem. She no longer has the incentive to take creative risks. No producer or record label boss would dare offer a critical opinion. It’s a crying shame. Modern pop’s greatest voice risks rubbishing her own legacy because she’s scared to take a day off.
