When it comes to the battle for control of the U.S. Senate, Republicans are mostly playing defense. The lion's share of the races that will decide which party secures a majority in November are in red states — including Texas, Iowa and Ohio — where the GOP is hoping to hold on against ascendant Democratic challengers. 

Minnesota is one of the few states in the country where the party is in a position to go on offense and potentially turn a blue seat red. 

In pursuit of that goal, the GOP has put its hopes in Michele Tafoya, a former sports broadcaster and NFL sideline reporter turned politician who polls suggest has a real chance of winning in a state that has been dominated by Democrats in recent election cycles. 

Tafoya is running for the seat currently held by Democrat Tina Smith, who is not pursuing re-election. Instead her opponent will be Minnesota Lt. Gov. Peggy Flannagan, a progressive who supports policies like Medicare for All and abolishing Immigration and Customs Enforcement. 

Minnesota has been at the center of some of the biggest controversies of President Trump's second term, particularly his administration's sweeping immigration crackdown. Though a majority of Minnesotans disapprove of Trump's performance as president, Tafoya has steadily chipped away at Flanagan's lead in polls since the primary.

Here's what you need to know about who she is, what she stands for and how she went from the sidelines to the center of one of the midterms' most pivotal races.  

Who is Michele Tafoya?

Tafoya was born and raised in Southern California. Her father was an aeronautical engineer, and her mother was a school teacher. She has Hispanic heritage on her father's side. 

She married her husband, Mark Vandersall, in 2000. They have two children. Tafoya has been open about her struggles with infertility, which included several miscarriages. Her daughter, Oliva, was adopted from Colombia as a nod to her father's lineage. 

After launching her sports broadcasting career in Los Angeles, she moved to Minnesota in the early 1990s to take a job at the KFAN sports radio station in Minneapolis. Before long, she moved on to a national role with CBS, which proved to be the jumping-off point for a decades-long career that made her one of the country's most recognizable sports broadcasters. She covered five Super Bowls, the NBA and the Olympics — winning five Emmys for her work along the way. 

Michele Tafoya looks on during Super Bowl LVI, the final game of her storied broadcasting career.

Tafoya's last broadcast was during Super Bowl LVI in 2022. Her political career began the very next day. Less than 12 hours after the game ended, she announced that she would co-chair the campaign of Republican Kendall Qualls in his bid to become Minnesota governor.

How did she go from NFL sidelines to the campaign trail?

Tafoya has said she always had strong political views, but made a specific effort to keep them out of her work as a broadcaster. 

"I was not as free to be as vocal about world events that I'm concerned about," she told The Athletic in 2022. "It's not because I was told to shut up. I want to be very clear about that. But look, if you're on a show like Sunday Night Football … the last thing they want to do is invite controversy."

Tafoya was more open about her beliefs outside on social media, where she would regularly promote work from conservative outlets. But she also made statements that broke from the GOP mainstream, including support for efforts to curb climate change. In an interview with Sports Illustrated in 2015, she described herself as a "pro-choice" conservative with "definite libertarian strains."

After retiring from broadcasting, Tafoya made several appearances as a speaker at Republican political events, including the Conservative Political Action Conference. She was also a guest host on The View, taking on the role of the conservative-leaning voice in contrast to the show's more liberal regular panelists.

In 2022, Tafoya launched a podcast that frequently touched on political topics and featured Republican politicians as guests. The most recent episode, released in January, includes an interview with former My Pillow CEO Mike Lindell, who unsuccessfully ran for Minnesota governor this year despite an endorsement from President Trump.

Tafoya formally announced her Senate bid a few weeks later. In August, she comfortably won a crowded Republican primary to set up a head-to-head race against Flanagan in November.  

What does she stand for?

In her pitch to Minnesota voters, Tafoya has leaned into her sports background.

"I'm not gonna stay on the sidelines any longer," she said while standing on a snow-covered football field in her campaign announcement video.

Like other swing-state Republicans across the country, Tafoya has worked to find a balance between supporting Trump and pushing back on some of his most unpopular policies. 

She has supported the president throughout the war with Iran, at one point telling Minnesotans to "take one less trip to Starbucks" to help offset the rising cost of gas. More recently, though, she has joined a growing list of GOP candidates who have called for Trump to end the conflict. 

"No president of either party should have a blank check to wage war without the consent of Congress or the buy-in of the American people. That includes President Donald Trump," Tafoya told Twin Cities local news KSTP on Saturday. 

Tafoya added that she would be "inclined to vote to end this war" if given the opportunity in the Senate. She has also said she didn't agree with ICE's tactics in Minneapolis and described Trump's tariff policies as "hit or miss."

Her policy views mostly align with mainstream Republican positions. She backs strong immigration enforcement and Trump's mass deportation campaign, a "tough on crime" approach to public safety, tax cuts, and laws barring transgender athletes from competing in women's and girls' sports. On abortion, she says she agrees with the Supreme Court's decision overturning Roe v. Wade, but personally believes there should be "reasonable access" in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy. 

Tafoya's own opinions of Trump have evolved as well. In 2022, she published an open letter urging Trump not to run for a second term, describing his previous time in the White House as "absolute mayhem." She also said on her podcast around the same time that there was much about Trump that she found "difficult to accept."

But during the 2024 presidential campaign, Tafoya said she was supporting Trump with "more enthusiasm" than before. She also said she felt "sort of agnostic" on the Jan. 6 assault on the U.S. Capitol. She celebrated Trump's victory later that year, describing it as "morning in America" — a reference to Ronald Reagan's famous phrase from the 1980s. 

Tafoya has mostly left Trump out of her current campaign ads and speeches, instead focusing her messaging on issues like fraud, affordability and public safety. The president opted not to endorse a candidate in Minnesota's Republican primary and has yet to throw his support behind Tafoya in the general election.

What are her chances?

Tafoya is one of the few Republicans in major races across the country who is outperforming expectations in the polls. 

In August, Flanagan held an average lead of about seven points, but Tafoya has been closing the gap. More recent surveys suggest the race is essentially up for grabs. Still, a win by Tafoya would likely be considered a significant upset. Two of the most respected non-partisan elections forecasters — The Cook Political Report and The Center for Politics — list Minnesota's Senate race as a "likely" win for Democrats.
