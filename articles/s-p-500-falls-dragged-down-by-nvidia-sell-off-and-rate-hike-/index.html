S&P 500 falls, dragged down by Nvidia sell-off and rate-hike fears after hot inflation data: Live updates

TheTools · 09.11.2026 23:05 · source · permalink

Stocks fell on Tuesday after October inflation data came in hotter than expected and a key Federal Reserve official hinted at the possibility of a half-point rise in rates next month.

The S&P 500 lost 0.38% to end at 7,520.93, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average declined 157.86 points, or 0.30%, to 52,231.89. The Nasdaq Composite fell 0.74% and closed at 25,065.87.

Nvidia dragged down the three major averages, falling more than 5%. The stock sat out Monday's tech-fueled rally following CEO Jensen Huang's admission over the weekend to an affair with an alleged Chinese spy.

The consumer price index rose 0.4% in October, above the gain of 0.3% that economists polled by Dow Jones expected. Core CPI — which excludes volatile food and energy costs — increased 0.3% in October, whereas economists had called for a 0.2% advance. Annual figures were not be provided for Tuesday's report because of the 2025 government shutdown.

Treasury yields jumped as traders reacted to the report, fearing that interest rate increases could now be more likely in 2027. The 10-year Treasury note yield in particular advanced back above 5% before later moving just below that level.

Earlier Tuesday, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in a speech in London that he thinks a December rate hike is likely and opened the door for a possible half-point increase amid persistent  inflationary pressures.

"While the precise timing of the hikes doesn't matter, ensuring that the promised hikes are implemented in a reasonable period of time does," he said.

Traders now see a December rate hike as a near certainty and a small chance of a supersized increase. They also expect at least a quarter-point hike at some point next year.

"There's an extrapolation that maybe the Fed's going to have to hike rates more aggressively next year," said Tom Hainlin, national investment strategist at U.S. Bank Asset Management. He later added, "The CPI print said perhaps inflation is going to be a little stickier for a little longer."

Tuesday's retreat came one day after the S&P 500 posted its best day since April. The push and pull come after a rocky week for markets, in which traders grew concerned about the potential for a slowdown in data center buildouts after Democrats posted big victories in the midterm elections.

U.S. equities fell on Tuesday.

The S&P 500 declined 0.38% to finish the day at 7,520.93, while the Nasdaq Composite shed 0.74% to 25,065.87. The Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 157.86 points, or 0.3%, and closed at 52,231.89.

The national average price of regular gasoline fell below $4 a gallon for the first time since July, reaching $3.978 a gallon on Tuesday.

Prices are down about 17 cents from a week ago and 39 cents from a month ago, according to AAA. Lower crude prices and normal autumn seasonality have contributed to the decline.

Brent briefly fell below $90 a barrel in hopes of easing U.S.-Iran tensions and recovering oil flows around the Strait of Hormuz.

The Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller, however, warned Tuesday that earlier energy shocks may continue affecting prices into 2027.

"While I am pleased to see energy prices down from their high rates over the past week or so, they are still quite elevated relative to the start of the year, and the ultimate effect on inflation will depend on what levels are sustained," he said.

Although further interest-rate increases could pressure bond prices in the near term, Wells Fargo strategists believe today's higher yields offer more income and a stronger starting point for long-term returns.

The firm examined five-, seven- and 10-year periods and found that the relationship between starting yields and subsequent annualized returns was strongest over approximately seven years. Higher income may also help cushion bond-price declines when market rates rise.

"Investors do not need to wait for the peak in interest rates to find opportunities in fixed income," wrote Luis Alvarado, co-head of global fixed-income strategy at Wells Fargo Investment Institute.

Wells Fargo favors short- and intermediate-term bonds, which offer attractive income without the greater interest-rate sensitivity of longer maturities. High-quality bonds may also provide diversification, reduce portfolio volatility and support cash-flow planning.

Separately, the firm remains neutral on information technology following the October sell-off while favoring communication services, industrials and materials as broader beneficiaries of AI investment.

Dallas Federal Reserve President Lorie Logan said Tuesday that she expects two more interest rate hikes will be needed as the central bank struggles to bring inflation lower.

"Tighter policy will be necessary to appropriately balance the outlook and risks for inflation and employment. And it is important to achieve that balance promptly," the policymaker said during an energy conference in Houston. "The longer above-target inflation persists, the greater the risk that it becomes baked into behavior."

Earlier in the day, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he expects a quarter percentage point hike would be appropriate at the next policy meeting in December. He even raised the notion that the Fed may have to raise by half a point should inflation numbers come in higher than expected, as they did in Tuesday's consumer price index report.

However, Logan said she thinks two more quarter-point moves would be appropriate before the Fed could take a break.

"The exact pace of increases could depend on the near-term economic and financial environment," she said. "After two more increases are in place, it would make sense in my view to pause and examine economic and financial developments to determine what further action may be appropriate. The goal should be to make policy modestly restrictive so we can sustainably deliver both maximum employment and stable prices."

Logan is a voter this year on the rate-setting Federal Open Market Committee, though she will move into a nonvoting role in 2027.

— Jeff Cox

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co. on Tuesday reported October revenue of 586.89 billion New Taiwan dollars ($17.88 billion), up 59.7% from a year earlier and up 14.7% from the previous month.

The world's largest contract chipmaker benefited from strong demand for advanced semiconductors, as major technology companies continue to invest heavily in artificial intelligence.

German semiconductor maker Infineon posted an 11.5% year-on-year jump in annual revenue for its 2026 fiscal year, with revenues coming in at 16.35 billion euros ($18.36 billion) – in line with analyst estimates.

Operating profit reached 2.2 billion euros, up from 1.5 billion euros the previous year.

In guidance issued alongside its results on Tuesday morning, Infineon forecast full-year revenue of around 20 billion euros for 2027.

"Infineon closed the fiscal year 2026 with another record quarter and enters the new fiscal year with continued strong momentum," CEO Jochen Hanebeck said in a statement. "Supported by an ongoing cyclical recovery and strong demand in our structural growth areas, we expect a significant increase in revenue and profitability in fiscal year 2027. China's exports and imports surged more than expected in October, according to official data released Tuesday, while its surplus shrank sharply, undershooting estimates.

Goods exports soared by 35.2% last month from a year earlier, exceeding expectations for a 33.5% jump in a Reuters poll but slowing from the 36.3% growth in September.

Imports climbed by 41.6% last month, customs data showed, jumping from 31% in September and sharply beating Reuters-polled analysts' estimates of a 32% rise.

The trade surplus dropped to $105.3 billion in October, the lowest level since April, according to LSEG data, with year-to-date figure at $1.04 trillion, compared to a record $1.2 trillion for the full year 2025. Calls from trading partners have grown louder for Beijing to boost domestic demand and balance its economy.
