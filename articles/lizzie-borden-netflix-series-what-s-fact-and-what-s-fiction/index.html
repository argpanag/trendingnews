Lizzie Borden Netflix Series: What’s Fact and What’s Fiction?

TheTools · 21.09.2026 16:44 · source · permalink

The new Ryan Murphy series “solves” the infamous 1892 double murder; but history remains far messier and much less certain

Lauren Conlin

• 4 hours ago

“Lizzie Borden took an axe, and gave her mother forty whacks…”

Nearly everyone knows the rhyme. But if you finished Netflix’s Monster: The Lizzie Borden Story thinking you finally know what actually happened inside the Borden home on Aug. 4, 1892… well, you may want to put down the hatchet.

The latest installment of Ryan Murphy and Ian Brennan’s Monster anthology stars Ella Beatty as Lizzie Borden, the 32-year-old Massachusetts woman accused of brutally murdering her father, Andrew Borden, and stepmother, Abby Borden.

The real Borden was tried for the killings and acquitted in 1893. No one else was ever convicted, leaving one of America’s most notorious double murders officially unsolved.

Netflix’s version doesn’t leave much to the imagination.

It depicts Lizzie as the killer and builds an elaborate story around her, including a sexual relationship with the family’s maid, Bridget “Maggie” Sullivan, a murder conspiracy between the two women and an abusive home life that helps explain Lizzie’s transformation into a killer.

It’s great television, but not necessarily great or accurate history.

Even Beatty, daughter of Hollywood royalty Warren Beatty and Annette Bening, has acknowledged the exaggeration, telling Netflix in an interview that she used historical material to help construct the “very fictionalized version” of Lizzie created for the series.

So, what really happened… and what was cooked up for Monster?

FACT OR FICTION: Lizzie killed her father and stepmother

VERDICT: UNKNOWN.

This is the big one. Netflix essentially “solves” a murder that history hasn’t.

Andrew and Abby Borden were indeed murdered inside their home at 92 Second Street in Fall River, Massachusetts, on Aug. 4, 1892. Abby was killed upstairs first. Andrew returned home later that morning and was subsequently found butchered on a sofa downstairs.

Lizzie became the primary suspect, was arrested one week later and stood trial for murder. On June 20, 1893, a jury acquitted her.

The prosecution had no eyewitness to either murder, no confession and serious problems with its physical evidence. The case against Lizzie was circumstantial, and some potentially damaging evidence was excluded from her trial (Borden apparently bought poison leading up to the murders, but it was excluded from being introduced).

Netflix openly chooses a side: it’s Lizzie did it. More than 130 years later, nobody can say with certainty who killed Andrew and Abby Borden.

FACT OR FICTION: Lizzie and Bridget Sullivan were lovers.

VERDICT: FICTION (at minimum, it’s completely unproven).

Vicky Krieps plays Bridget Sullivan, the Bordens’ Irish maid, as more than an employee.

In Monster, Bridget and Lizzie develop a sexual relationship and eventually become partners in murder. The real Bridget Sullivan absolutely existed. She was also inside the Borden house on the morning of the murders and became an important witness.

What we don’t have is evidence that she and Lizzie were lovers. There is also no historical evidence establishing that Bridget helped murder the Bordens.

Historian and Lizzie Borden author Cara Robertson told Business Insider that the idea of a sexual relationship between the women can be traced to later fictional depictions of the case; one prominent version appeared in Evan Hunter’s 1984 novel Lizzie. The real Bridget said she spent part of that morning washing windows before eventually going upstairs to rest.

Netflix turns that alibi on its head, depicting the window washing as part of the women’s plan. Great twist, but no proof it happened.

FACT OR FICTION: Andrew Borden sexually abused Lizzie.

VERDICT: UNPROVEN.

Monster depicts an abusive and deeply dysfunctional Borden household and uses that environment to help explain its version of Lizzie. There have long been theories that Andrew sexually abused one or both of his daughters, but there is no reliable contemporary evidence establishing that he did.

What is documented is family tension, including arguments involving Andrew’s money and property and Lizzie and Emma’s deteriorating relationship with their stepmother.

FACT OR FICTION: Lizzie tried poisoning her family

VERDICT: PARTLY TRUE (with a very large Netflix embellishment).

This one is particularly interesting because Monster takes several real events and stitches them together into something much more sinister.

Members of the Borden household really did become sick shortly before the murders.

And an employee at the drug store later claimed Lizzie attempted to purchase prussic acid, a deadly poison, shortly before her father and stepmother were killed. Prosecutors were prevented from presenting the alleged prussic-acid purchase to the jury.

Netflix goes further: the show depicts Lizzie and Bridget intentionally serving Andrew and Abby spoiled oysters as part of a failed effort to poison them, resulting in the series’ memorably disgusting vomiting and diarrhea sequence.

That poisoning plot is dramatization, not a historical event.

FACT OR FICTION: Lizzie burned a dress after the murders,

VERDICT: TRUE.

Several days after the murders, family friend Alice Russell witnessed Lizzie burning a dress in the kitchen stove. Lizzie’s explanation was essentially innocent; the dress had been ruined by paint and was no longer useful.

Naturally, prosecutors found the timing suspicious. There was no proof that the dress was the clothing worn by the killer or that it was covered in blood.

Monster takes that historical fact and gives viewers its own answer to what the dress-burning meant. Netflix also acknowledges that the series transforms questionable evidence into narrative fact.

FACT OR FICTION: Lizzie murdered Bertha Manchester.

VERDICT: FICTION.

This is one of Monster’s wildest historical detours. Bertha Manchester was a real person, and she really was murdered.

But Netflix moves her killing around in the timeline and transforms it into a sort of murderous “test run” for Lizzie and Bridget before the Borden killings.

That did not happen.

Manchester was actually murdered after Andrew and Abby Borden, shortly before Lizzie’s trial began.

A Portuguese immigrant, José Correa de Mello, was convicted of Manchester’s murder, although that conviction was later determined to have been wrongful. There is no established evidence that Lizzie Borden murdered Manchester.

FACT OR FICTION: Actress Nance O’Neil helped Lizzie survive her trial.

VERDICT: FICTION… but the friendship was real.

Nance O’Neil may seem like one of those characters writers invented to spice things up, but she wasn’t.

Born Gertrude Lamson, O’Neil became a successful stage actress and really did develop a close relationship with Lizzie. There’s just one gigantic problem with the Netflix timeline: they apparently didn’t meet until around 1904, which is roughly 11 years after Lizzie was acquitted.

So O’Neil couldn’t have been secretly coaching Lizzie through her 1893 murder trial, as Netflix depicted.

Brennan has been unusually candid about why the show changed it: trials can be difficult to dramatize, and bringing the actress into Lizzie’s courtroom story gave the writers another way to tell it.

Whether the real Lizzie and Nance were romantically or sexually involved is another mystery.

Their friendship was unquestionably close and attracted attention, but surviving evidence doesn’t establish that they were lovers.

FACT OR FICTION: The victims’ skulls were actually brought into court.

VERDICT: HORRIFYINGLY TRUE.

Andrew and Abby’s skulls were actually used as evidence during Lizzie’s trial. The victims’ heads had been removed during the investigation and their skulls cleaned for examination.

When they appeared during the proceedings, Lizzie reportedly became physically overwhelmed.

For all the liberties Netflix takes with the case, the spectacle surrounding the real Borden trial was unusual, even by today’s true-crime standards.

FACT OR FICTION: Lizzie got away with murder because Victorian men couldn’t believe a woman could do it.

VERDICT: IT’S COMPLICATED.

Gender undoubtedly hovered over the trial. Lizzie was an unmarried, churchgoing woman from a respectable New England family, and her defense had every reason to present her in a way that contrasted with the brutality of the murders.

Monster has O’Neil coach Lizzie on performing that femininity for the jury. O’Neil wasn’t actually there, but Netflix notes that Lizzie’s attorneys really did focus on her femininity and social position. But reducing the acquittal to “men couldn’t believe a woman could kill” ignores genuine weaknesses in the prosecution’s case.

There was no eyewitness. No confession. No definitive murder weapon tied to Lizzie. The prosecution’s poison evidence was excluded, as was Lizzie’s inquest testimony.

The jury wasn’t simply deciding whether Lizzie seemed capable of murder. They had to decide whether prosecutors had proved she committed these murders beyond a reasonable doubt. They decided that prosecutors had not proved their case.

The truth may actually be why we’re still obsessed. Netflix does give Lizzie Borden something history never did: a definitive story.

The Netflix version of Lizzie has motives, lovers, accomplices, and secrets… And viewers get to watch her commit the crime that made her infamous.

The real Lizzie is considerably more frustrating.

She lived inside the house where two people were slaughtered in broad daylight. Her behavior raised questions. She allegedly tried to purchase poison before the murders. She burned a dress afterward.

And yet investigators never found the piece of evidence that could conclusively answer the question people have been asking since 1892: Did Lizzie Borden actually do it?

She walked out of court a free woman, moved into a larger Fall River home she called Maplecroft and lived for another 34 years.

She died on June 1, 1927, at 66 years old. No confession, and without anyone else ever being convicted of the murders.

134 years later…we’re still talking about Lizzie Borden.
