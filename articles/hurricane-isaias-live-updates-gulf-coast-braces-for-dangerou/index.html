Hurricane Isaias live updates: Gulf Coast braces for dangerous storm | CNN

Here's the latest

  • Record-shattering Isaias continues to strengthen en route to landfall on the northern Gulf Coast by this weekend. The storm rapidly intensified into the Atlantic season’s first hurricane on Wednesday night.
  • Isaias could near Category 3 strength, but will weaken some before making landfall as a larger hurricane by early Saturday morning. Feet of storm surge, up to 10 inches of rain, tornadoes and destructive winds are expected.
  • States of emergency have been issued in parts of Alabama and Florida, where Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis is urging residents to prepare. Mandatory evacuation orders have been issued in two counties in the Florida panhandle.

Isaias starts to strengthen again

Isaias is a Category 1 hurricane with sustained winds of 85 mph, according to the National Hurricane Center’s 10 a.m. CT update. That’s an increase of 5 mph from just three hours ago and shows that the hurricane is starting to bulk up again.

Isaias continues to battle some storm-disrupting winds in the atmosphere and dry air that led to its brief strength plateau earlier this morning. But it’s still forecast to reach Category 2 strength this evening and peak as a high-end Category 2 with 110 mph winds overnight Thursday or Friday morning.

Sun rises on Hurricane Isaias for the first time

The sun has come up to reveal Hurricane Isaias and its swirling clouds above the Gulf’s record-warm water.

True color satellite imagery shows us what a storm looks like with the naked eye from space — think of it as what you’d see if you were on the space station.

But you need sunlight to illuminate the clouds and use this imagery. The storm’s bright white clouds appear in the loop as the satellite captures the moment of this morning’s sunrise.

What to expect, city by city

Here’s a look at Isaias’ impacts and when to expect them in cities along the Gulf Coast.

Mobile/Gulf Shores, Alabama

  • Peak impact: Late Friday afternoon to Saturday morning. High tide is at 10 p.m. ET Friday.
  • Rainfall: 6 to 10 inches, starting early Friday afternoon with the heaviest rain Friday evening through night.
  • Wind gusts: Tropical storm-force gusts (39+ mph) from midday Friday to Saturday morning. Expect hurricane-force gusts (74+ mph) from 9 p.m. ET Friday to 2 a.m. ET Saturday. Peak gusts will hit 80 to 100 mph.

Pensacola, Florida

  • Peak impact: Late Friday afternoon to Saturday morning. High tide is at 11 p.m. ET Friday.
  • Rainfall: 6 to 10 inches, starting early Friday afternoon, with the heaviest Friday evening through Friday night.
  • Wind gusts: Tropical storm-force gusts from mid-afternoon Friday to Saturday morning. Expect hurricane-force gusts from 9 p.m. ET Friday to 2 a.m. ET Saturday. Peak gusts will hit 80 to 100 mph.

New Orleans

  • Peak impact: Friday morning to Saturday morning
  • Rainfall: Around 1 inch, starting Friday morning with the heaviest rain on Friday night.
  • Wind gusts: Tropical storm-force gusts from late Friday morning to Friday evening. Peak gusts Friday evening will be 35 to 45 mph.

Isaias will bring some inland flood risks, but it's no Helene

For those who lived through Helene, another hurricane bringing rain to the mountains may stir understandable anxiety. But Isaias’ forecast points to a more limited flood threat.

Parts of the southern Appalachians could see 3 to 5 inches Saturday, with locally significant flooding possible.

During Helene, some mountain areas received up to 10 inches before the hurricane arrived. Moisture streaming ahead of Helene fueled a separate area of persistent downpours, known as a “predecessor rain event,” or PRE.

The hurricane then dumped torrential rain onto saturated ground, with mountain slopes enhancing the rainfall. The combined multiday event piled up 20 to 30 inches of rain in some parts of western North Carolina.

Isaias’ quick movement should help limit rainfall totals, though locally higher amounts remain possible. Residents should keep watching for forecast changes as the storm draws closer.

Isaias could weaken before landfall. That will expand its danger

Isaias is forecast to run into some storm-disrupting wind shear the closer it gets to the US coast. This shear — changes in wind speed or direction at different levels of the atmosphere — is going to push Isaias a little off-kilter, focusing the worst of its wind and rain on the north side of the storm.

This lopsidedness will weaken Isaias — but also expand the reach of its winds.

A stronger storm tends to stay compact, like an ice skater spinning. Try to knock that same skater off balance, they’ll put out their arms to steady themself.

That’s basically the same thing we’re expecting with Isaias’ winds — instead of staying confined closer to the center, they’ll stretch out farther. More places could be hit with Isaias’ strong winds as a result, even if the storm is a bit weaker overall.

First US hurricane landfall in 2 years possible

If Isaias makes landfall as a hurricane, it will mark the first hurricane landfall anywhere in the United States in two years.

No hurricane has come ashore in the US since 2024’s Hurricane Milton. Milton slammed into Florida’s Gulf Coast as a Category 3 major hurricane on October 9, 2024.

Isaias is forecast to weaken, but still remain a formidable storm before landfall Friday night or very early Saturday morning. It could come ashore as a Category 1 or a lower-end Category 2 hurricane before quickly becoming a tropical storm after landfall.

Regardless of its strength, Isaias will be the fourth named storm to make landfall in the US this season. The three others have been short-lived tropical storms: Arthur impacted Texas on June 17; Bertha hit Louisiana on July 22; and Edouard made landfall near the Texas-Louisiana border on September 1.

School districts along Alabama's coast announce closures ahead of Isaias

All Mobile County and Baldwin County Public schools in Alabama will be closed on Friday as Hurricane Isaias is expected to impact the northern Gulf Coast this weekend.

“We do not like to do this, because it affects so many, but we have to take all factors into consideration and of course, the most important, the safety of our students and our staff,” Baldwin County Public Schools Superintendent Marty McRae said announcing the closure on Wednesday.

All of Baldwin County Public Schools’ afterschool and extracurricular activities scheduled for Friday and Saturday are also canceled.

“I cannot risk anyone’s safety,” Mobile County Public Schools Superintendent Chresal Threadgill said in his closure announcement. “The weather conditions currently remain extremely unpredictable.”

Isaias' strength holds steady for now

Isaias remains a Category 1 hurricane with sustained winds of 80 mph, according to the National Hurricane Center’s 7 a.m. CT update.

The hurricane has maintained its strength over the past few hours but is still set to strengthen today. It’s forecast to peak as a high-end Category 2 hurricane sometime Thursday night.

A Hurricane Hunter aircraft is currently flying into Isaias, so we’ll get a more precise measurement of its current strength soon.

Some Florida public schools are closing for the storm

At least 14 Florida Panhandle counties are closing public schools Friday, and many have closed Thursday, according to the state’s Department of Education.

Florida State University’s campus in Panama City is also closed.

Which side of a hurricane is the most dangerous?

The right side of a hurricane, relative to its forward motion, typically packs the most extreme conditions, and it’s all because of their counterclockwise rotation.

Wind speeds are amplified because the rotation pushes air in the same direction as the storm is moving. The area with the most extreme winds and intense storms sits just to the right of a hurricane’s eye, in the eye wall.

Because of boosted winds, tornadoes also occur more frequently on the right side. Faster winds increase areas of wind shear — a change in wind speed or direction with height — which tornadoes need to spin up.

Similarly, surge is also enhanced. As storms approach land, wind piles up and pushes seawater towards the coast on the right side of a hurricane. On the left side of a hurricane, wind does the opposite and can actually drain bays.

This will all be true for Isaias but may be even more so because it’s expected to become lopsided as it battles wind shear near the Gulf Coast. More thunderstorms and rain will likely be concentrated on the hurricane’s eastern half.

Isaias could knock out power well inland

Isaias is guaranteed to knock out power and down trees where it makes landfall along the coast, but outages could extend further inland into parts of the Southeast.

Isaias will be moving fast and grow larger before it moves inland. Those are two of the factors that will fuel wind gusts of 30 to 40 mph that will spread northward into parts of central and northern Alabama, northern Georgia and northeast Mississippi on Saturday.

Those gusts are nowhere near as fierce as what the coast will see, but it could be enough to cause at least isolated to scattered power outages, particularly in Alabama.

Florida residents stock up ahead of the storm

As Tropical Storm Isaias heads towards the Gulf Coast, many residents began preparing days in advance.

Residents flocked to grocery stores and gas stations in Pensacola, Florida to stock up in preparation causing long lines at some locations.

Fans — and Trump — hope Isaias doesn't dampen Alabama-Georgia face-off

As Hurricane Isaias approaches the Gulf Coast, all eyes are on how it will impact one of the biggest games of the college football season.

The storm could make landfall Saturday, hours before No. 2 Georgia plays No. 6 Alabama in Tuscaloosa in a game that President Donald Trump is planning to attend.

While the storm could prompt other schools in the region to cancel games, change kickoff times, or even move games to other locations, Alabama said “there are no plans to alter the contest” in a statement Wednesday evening.

When asked whether the storm will change his travel plans to Alabama, Trump said, “We’ll have to see.”

Team officials offered few details on contingency plans if the storm were to intensify.

Even if the game goes on as planned, meteorologists predict players and spectators may feel some effects of the storm.

Heavy rain and gusty winds could make for a soggy, blustery Saturday evening in Tuscaloosa.

A faster-moving storm could allow conditions to improve before or during the game, but that’s not the most likely outcome right now. The timing will be key.

How climate change is supercharging hurricane strength

When Isaias rapidly intensified into a hurricane last night, it was undergoing a phenomenon that is becoming more common as the planet heats up.

Rapid intensification is when a storm’s sustained winds increase by at least 35 mph within 24 hours. The main ingredient a storm needs for this is ocean heat, which gives it the energy to build ferocious strength.

Global oceans absorb around 90% of the excess heat humans produce by burning oil, coal and gas — and they are warming at an alarming pace. The Gulf is no exception. Isaias is traveling over water that’s at record high temperatures for this time of year.

And because warmer air is able to hold more moisture, this could also supercharge the amount of rain Isaias is able to carry.

A 2024 study by the nonprofit research group Climate Central found climate change had boosted the intensity of most Atlantic hurricanes between 2019 and 2023.

Last year, four hurricanes underwent rapid intensification, including Category 5 Melissa, which tore across the Caribbean, killing more than 90 people.

Isaias continues to strengthen

Hurricane Isaias’s maximum sustained winds have increased to 80 mph, according to the 5 a.m. ET advisory from the National Hurricane Center.

Isaias forecast to strengthen into a Category 2 over the Gulf later today, then peak overnight at near Category 3, or major hurricane, intensity. How strong it gets will depend on how much Isaias can fend off increasing winds in the atmosphere that can rip storms apart.

Isaias is still expected to weaken some before landfall on Friday night, but that won’t reduce its danger. A life-threatening storm surge is expected at the coast and strong winds could down trees and knock out power well inland across parts of the South.

How residents are preparing for the hurricane

A state of emergency has been declared across Florida and Alabama.

Hurricane breaks a record

Isaias is now the first hurricane of the 2026 Atlantic season and has shattered the record for the latest first hurricane formation in the basin.

The previous record holders were 2002’s Hurricane Gustav and 2013’s Hurricane Humberto, both of which formed on September 11.

It’s also the first hurricane in the Atlantic basin in nearly a year: Hurricane Melissa was 2025’s final hurricane and it formed on October 25.

Gulf water is record warm for this time of year, helping to fuel the storm, but hostile atmospheric conditions near the coast are forecast to weaken Isaias a bit before it makes landfall Friday night or early Saturday morning. It’s still expected to come ashore as a high-end Category 2 hurricane with 110 mph winds.

Flashes of lightning punctuate Isaias’ swirling clouds

Flurries of lightning flashed through Isaias as it strengthened into the first hurricane of the season overnight, satellite imagery from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the Cooperative Institute for Research in the Atmosphere showed.

Isaias will grow larger as it nears the coast

Isaias will grow larger as it nears the coast, which means windy conditions could be felt across a wide area of the South, especially Friday night through Saturday.

Tropical storm-force winds — 39 mph or greater — could arrive on the coast by midday Friday. Hurricane conditions are expected near and just inland from where the center makes landfall by late Friday.

The wind could down trees and knock out power both at the coast and over 100 miles inland in parts of the Florida Panhandle, Alabama and Mississippi.

Beach homes along Alabama Gulf Coast urged to evacuate

Popular vacation spots along Alabama’s southern shores are battening down ahead of the expected impacts of Isaias, which is forecast to make landfall on the US Gulf Coast later this week.

Dauphin Island has issued a voluntary evacuation order starting Thursday evening for approximately 350 to 400 homes, city Public Safety Supervisor Troy Gorlott told CNN. The recommended evacuations include primarily low beach areas in the west end of the island that are made up of vacation homes and rentals, Gorlott said.

People in coastal and low-lying areas in neighboring Baldwin County are also “encouraged to begin preparing and relocate if needed,” the county’s emergency management agency said.

Both Baldwin and Dauphin Island’s Mobile County are among the 40 Alabama counties under a state of emergency Gov. Kay Ivey declared Wednesday.
