Britain’s blackout survival plan? Listen to the BBC

TheTools · 11.11.2026 02:37 · source · permalink

Corporation reveals its strategy for broadcasting vital information to the public if the power goes off

We have been told to keep FM radios in our emergency kits for use in the event of a national blackout. But what would we be listening to, and how would it be broadcast?

The BBC has revealed that, in the event of a country-wide power cut, a 100-person team would be tasked with keeping the lines of communication with the public open via an emergency radio station.

Last month, the Government unveiled its “Be Ready” campaign, urging the public to stockpile three days’ worth of non-perishable food, bottled water, blankets and a wind-up radio.

Wes Streeting, the Defence Secretary, asked Britons to prepare for the possibility of disruption caused by natural disasters or cyber attacks by hostile states.

Given that so many vital services rely on electricity, if there were a “Black Sky” event that caused a significant power outage and the internet went down, the old technology of copper-wire landline telephones and FM radio could well be the only communication channels left standing.

Although officials have stressed that there is no imminent threat to the UK National Grid, the BBC has its own plan for such an event.

A report from the broadcaster said that only a small number of staff knew the full details, but it was understood that about 100 staff would “run a special emergency radio studio”.

This would be operated via secure lines of communication that were not dependent on the internet, and there would be enough fuel, water and food to ensure that it could operate without resupply for at least three days.

This emergency service would broadcast pre-arranged government messaging, with chief medical officers, such as Prof Sir Chris Whitty, explaining what people should do in the event of an emergency.

It would include instructions and advice on how to find and treat water so it was safe to drink in the event of disruption to the supply, as well as how to deal with food in the house and how to dispose of lavatory waste without flushing the lavatory.

The BBC would use reserves of diesel to power its FM radio output, which would be transmitted via radio towers that were isolated from the internet. Each would be equipped with a tank containing up to 40,000 litres (8,800 gallons) of diesel, allowing the generators to work for at least three days.

Meanwhile, other information would be broadcast via satellite vehicles by a select number of journalists at emergency centres around the country. This would carry on for as long as the diesel lasted.

The teams would be using satellite phones to keep in touch, and if these stopped working, there would be pre-agreed protocols to follow and pre-arranged meet-up points, the BBC report said.

A source told the BBC: “It was originally thought we should plan for five to seven days. The latest advice from Neso [the National Energy System Operator] is that they will probably be able to get the power back on in two to three days.”

While the facility to broadcast via FM radio is believed to be key, the other communication channel that would be unaffected by a power cut is the copper-wire landline system. However, this will be switched off on Jan 31 next year because the Government says it has become unreliable and difficult to repair.

After the Government issued its advice to prepare for emergencies, Robert Dyas reported a 160 per cent increase in sales of radios compared with the week before, according to BBC Newsnight, while Maplin said sales of radios had increased by 92 per cent, torches by 284 per cent, and power banks by 106 per cent.

The advice issued last month comprised the first stage of a three-year campaign designed to ensure the public were better prepared for emergencies.
