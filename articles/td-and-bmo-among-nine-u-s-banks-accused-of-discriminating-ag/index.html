TD and BMO among nine U.S. banks accused of discriminating against MAGA and conservative customers

TheTools · 14.10.2026 14:32 · source · permalink

TD and BMO are among nine U.S. lenders accused by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency of having denied or limited access to financial services for some customers.Andrew Lahodynskyj/The Canadian Press

Toronto-Dominion Bank TD-T and Bank of Montreal BMO-T are among a list of nine lenders identified by a major U.S. banking regulator as having “denied, debanked and discriminated” against customers and businesses on political grounds, following President Donald Trump’s calls to stem these activities.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) said Wednesday that between 2020 and 2025, nine banks maintained practices and policies that denied or limited access to financial services due to political or religious beliefs. The OCC launched its review last year after Mr. Trump requested an investigation.

Along with the two large Canadian banks, the OCC identified the world’s largest bank, JPMorgan Chase & Co. JPM-N, as well as Bank of America Corp. BAC-N, Citigroup Inc. C-N, Wells Fargo & Co. WFC-N, U.S. Bancorp USB-N, Capital One Financial Corp. COF-N and PNC Financial Services Group Inc. PNC-N

“Today’s report provides irrefutable evidence that the nation’s largest banks have denied, debanked and discriminated against customers and lawful businesses,” OCC Comptroller of the Currency Jonathan Gould said in a statement.

“Americans should have access to financial services regardless of their beliefs, affiliations, or political views.”

In response to a request for comment, BMO referred The Globe and Mail to the Bank Policy Institute (BPI), a U.S. banking industry advocacy group.

“We support efforts to establish clear and consistent standards that promote access while maintaining sound risk management, including the administration’s recent efforts to prioritize supervisory attention on material financial risk and rather than reputational risk,” the BPI said in a statement.

TD did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

JPMorgan said its policies prohibit discrimination based on political or religious views. The OCC’s report includes “a limited number of historical interactions” across JPMorgan’s nearly 90 million customers and small businesses, according to the bank.

“We take this seriously, learned from it, and improved the way we do business,” JPMorgan spokesperson Trish Wexler said in a statement.

“We agree with the Administration on a broader point: years of operating in a regulatory environment that often second-guessed decisions contributed to a culture of caution across the banking industry. That system needs fixing.”

Debanking is a standard practice at banks, including among Canadian lenders in their domestic market, typically aimed at protecting their loan books from risky consumer and business activities.

When a lender detects an unusual transaction that indicates a client may be linked to money laundering, terrorist financing, fraud, or other crimes, it will launch an investigation and, in some cases, shut down the account. This could also include activities that do not align with a bank’s environmental, social and governance guidelines.

In some cases, lenders have come under fire for stretching these practices to broader political and religious views.

Britain’s financial regulator launched a probe into debanking in 2023 after Brexit advocate Nigel Farage claimed that his accounts at a bank were closed because of his political views. The regulator later published a review of its findings, stating it found no evidence that banks were rejecting accounts due to political beliefs.

Last year, Mr. Trump signed an executive order targeting debanking practices that result in some customers being denied access to financial services for ideological reasons.

Mr. Trump has accused banks of discriminating against him in the past. In a CNBC interview in 2025, he said JPMorgan told him he had 20 days to move large amounts of money to a different bank. He claimed he went to Bank of America to deposit more than $1-billion, but was told the bank couldn’t provide an account.

In the OCC’s report, it found that broad reputation risk policies lacked clear standards and objective criteria. These practices disadvantaged certain customers based on their political, religious or social views, the regulator said.

The OCC also said that the banks maintained policies that restricted certain industry access to financial services, including oil and gas exploration, coal mining or coal-powered plants, firearms or ammunition manufacturing or distribution, digital asset activities and others.

The regulator’s report includes examples at each lender of discrimination it deems unacceptable, including BMO’s approach to firearms. In a 2022 credit request, the bank evaluated the risks associated with a customer that manufactured empty firearm magazines. A senior risk officer approved maintaining the relationship, but required monitoring of the business’ sales and established a threshold on sales of the products deemed risky.

In an example concerning TD, the OCC said the bank required the escalation of loan requests – which could include flagging to more senior staff for assessment – by a nonprofit organization that promote pro-life views. The group’s national director was flagged as having a “high political and media profile,” according to the report.

In another example, the OCC said payment processor WePay – which was used by JPMorgan – closed the account of a merchant in 2021 that was selling merchandise promoting Mr. Trump’s Make America Great Again (MAGA) brand. WePay said the merchant’s merchandise “promote[d] hate, violence, or racial intolerance,” according to the report.

While JPMorgan employees did not provide input on the decision to close the account, the reasoning for the decision was explained to bank employees shortly after, the regulator said. The report includes several examples including WePay.

“A process used by WePay, a small technology company we acquired years ago and have since wound down, did not meet our expectations,” Ms. Wexler said. “We identified it, corrected it, and the business no longer operates.”

The OCC did not announce any penalties for the banks in its report, but said it is assessing further regulatory and supervisory changes to prevent these issues from happening again.

In response to the OCC’s report, the U.S. consumer financial services watchdog said Wednesday it will address any “harm” caused to banking customers. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) also said it will “fully investigate” whether the lenders have complied with federal consumer financial laws.
