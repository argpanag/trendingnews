Sorry Matt (and Ed) … A Welcome to Wrexham rise through the football pyramid has already happened in Australia, but no one knew about it then and many still don’t. 

A little-known outer suburban club climbed from obscurity out of Victoria’s lowest division to the national league in almost even time. 

It began before streaming services, YouTube, the internet, VHS and even colour TV, when soccer was definitely called soccer. 

Had this happened in American sports, it would be a movie starring Gene Hackman. But this is Australia, where football history is lucky to rate a footnote amongst fans and foes alike. That club is my club: Mooroolbark.

Even though I should be searching for jobs, I couldn’t pass up the opportunity to pen a tome to the club that has stolen my heart. 

How Mooroolbark ended up in the Philips Soccer League, as the NSL was initially known for sponsorship reasons, is folklore. But as Matt said last week, it’s a cautionary tale for clubs with big aspirations, don’t overextend yourselves, otherwise the fall could be as big as the ascent.

Mooroolbark plays at Esther Park, a compact ground in the shadow of Mt Dandenong and its TV towers. Some 34 kilometres from Melbourne’s CBD, the outer eastern suburb sits near the gateway to the Yarra Valley, a region famed for its wineries and viticulture. 

The name was initially thought to mean ‘red earth’ in the Wurundjeri language, but that interpretation is now open to debate. 

What’s undisputed is that the pronunciation of Mooroolbark is a tongue twister for those from interstate. When I lived in NSW, for some reason most people pronounced it ‘Moo-roola-bark’.

 Mooroolbark should be famous for its soccer club. Nevertheless, its main claim to fame is the Five Ways, a series of cojoined roundabouts that defy description and confuse the uninitiated driver.  

The club was founded as Mooroolbark United in late 1962 by Tony van der Ploeg and Rinus de Visser, two soccer enthusiasts from the Netherlands. Initially playing invitation and friendly matches, the Barkers became affiliated with the Victorian Soccer Federation in 1964. 

Four promotions between 1969 and 1974, three as champions, lifted the club into the exalted company of the Victorian State League (today’s NPL Victoria) alongside heavyweights Hellas, Alexander and JUST. After treading water in its first top-flight season, the club finished third in both 1975 and 1976.

The push for a national league, on the table since the early 1960s, finally gathered steam. Led from Sydney by Frank Lowy of Hakoah Eastern Suburbs and Alex Pongrass of St George Budapest, the VSF’s embargo on clubs joining seriously threatened the national league’s chances of getting off the ground. 

No Victorian representation would kill it dead in the water. State parochialism would win over national interests. But all was not lost. To use the grandiloquent words of Les Murray: “Along rode Mooroolbark, like Galahad on a white horse, to the rescue.” 

Modest Mooroolbark broke the deadlock in June 1976 by submitting their application to the Australian Soccer Federation. 

This immediately opened the floodgates and other clubs applied as well, namely the Big 3 of Hellas, Alexander and JUST along with George Cross, Juventus, Sunshine City and Essendon Lions (Croatia). 

VSF board members described the move as a “breach of faith” whilst Metropolitan League chairman John Barclay said he was “disgusted” with Mooroolbark. Barclay added that Victoria had been “outgeneralled, outfought and out politicked” in its attempts to prevent leading clubs joining the national league. 

Three names were forwarded as part of its successful bid; Mooroolbark United, Maroondah United and believe it or not, Manchester United (Victoria). The club ambitiously described itself as the Manchester United of Victorian soccer and was hoping to sign George Best. 

However, its closest connection was Manchester Road, the main thoroughfare running north-south through the suburb.  

By then, the senior club had become resolutely British in outlook while presenting itself as a district side to attract local support. Mooroolbark’s two key figures were president Tom Bailey, a successful carpet store entrepreneur, and secretary Malcolm Jones, who had the gift of the gab when promoting the side. 

Jones told Soccer Action in September 1976: “With our connections and our enthusiasm, we’ll be the top club in Australia within three years.” Poignantly, foundation clubs had a three-year grace period before a form of promotion and relegation took effect.

Occasionally labelled a Dutch club by the interstate press, by 1977 Mooroolbark had little Dutch about it beyond red and white stripes reminiscent of PSV Eindhoven and a change strip that mimicked Ajax Amsterdam. 

After impressive performances at state level, the Barkers aimed to light up the Philips League. 

But a 5-0 opening-round thrashing by Western Suburbs at the Sydney Sports Ground was a sign of things to come. Frequently scheduled on Saturdays, then a non-traditional matchday, Mooroolbark enjoyed more 0-10 Network television exposure on Philips Top Soccer than their ladder position might have suggested. 

Records would show Mooroolbark finished last in 1977 on 15 points. The nadir, a 10-3 loss away to Adelaide City. A spirited finish to the season masked serious troubles on and off the pitch. 

With Newcastle KB United waiting in the wings, PSL managing director John Frank axed Mooroolbark in January 1978 because it “failed to refute certain findings of a sub-committee set up to investigate the club.” 

After missing two show cause deadlines, Frank said the club had inadequate administration, an unsuitable ground, unpaid player fees and failed to honour commitments made in its league membership application. A proposed takeover by a group loosely affiliated with the Croatian community lacked support and never progressed beyond tentative talks. 

Essentially bankrupt, and on the cusp of folding, Mooroolbark quickly reorganised itself and dropped the seldomly-used United from its name. 

The VSF in its wisdom, and most likely as payback, ruled Mooroolbark’s senior team would replace its relegated reserves side in Metropolitan League 3 for 1978 – the equivalent of the Victorian fourth division. 

By 1981 there would be two more relegations. A resurgence brought a two-year stay in the renamed Victorian Premier League as the 1990s began, before four relegations in the next 10 seasons. Since then, the club has been marking time, waiting to fulfil its potential as a sleeping giant of Victorian football. 

People assume I’m from Mooroolbark because of my support, but I grew up three suburbs away. Impressed by the club’s backstory, I first arrived in the early 2000s looking to be a player. 

But after a pre-season I quickly realised I would have made Michael Mifsud look like Messi, so then I had a stint as senior men’s team manager. A move to Sydney made me realise absence makes the heart grow fonder and I began what would be a series of Mooroolbark-embossed Australian flags.

Yet upon my return, I would traverse the diverse landscapes of Football Victoria clubland visiting different grounds and sampling the food, as I became football media adjacent. I realised, however, I was sick and tired of watching teams I don’t give a shit about, so I am back in the fold as a Barker. 

Next year marks 50 years since the NSL began, and the club is planning a series of commemorations to celebrate the milestone. Having recently been elected club secretary for a two-year term, plenty is planned, so watch this space.
