    A UK government spokesperson says the threats reported in the "early hours" of Thursday - described by the transport secretary earlier as "malicious hoaxes" - have been "resolved".

  2. Airlines receive bomb threats every day, but this is different - aviation expertpublished at 13:58 GMT
    13:58 GMT

    Gabriela Pomeroy
    Live reporter

    The aviation industry "faces multiple bomb threats every single day", says Philip Baum, professor of aviation security at the University of Coventry.

    But he is "surprised" at this week's incidents, which are "different as the threats have been classified by the authorities as specific".

    That does not mean that every aircraft subject to a threat is diverted, he says.

    A flight is diverted "only when the threat is credible and the authorities believe - from the source or nature of the threat - that there is a potential of an explosive device on board."

    Baum adds that "most people who put bombs on planes to do not tell people about it before - they claim it after the event".

    But the concern is that "if you issue enough hoax threats, then complacency could set in and a real threat could be ignored - or they could be a diversion".

    Dublin airport is "operating as normal" after an Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome was diverted to a French military base near Paris following a bomb threat, according to a spokesperson.

    He adds that a flight leaving Dublin Airport for Rome this morning "did divert to an airport in Paris", but "other than that, it’s a normal day" and "people getting on their way" at the airport.

  14. With bomb threats, the cost to sender is low - but the cost to airlines can be far higherpublished at 11:28 GMT
    11:28 GMT

    By Thomas Copeland

    An aviation safety expert has told BBC Verify this week’s bomb threats are "most likely a hoax, but the system is right to treat it as real".

    Marco Chan, senior lecturer in aviation operations at Kingston University and former Cathay Pacific pilot, says hoax threats are seen by some experts as "possibly part of a pattern of cheap, deniable disruption" to air travel.

    He adds that "hoax waves can also come from copycats or a handful of individuals", but that "three days of incidents is too small a sample to separate co-ordination from contagion".

    Chan says the "cost to the sender is almost nothing" compared to the significant impact on airlines, airports and air forces.

    "That asymmetry is the point of the tactic, whatever the motive."

    In this case of this morning’s Aer Lingus flight which was diverted to a military airfield in northern France, Chan says that "diversion, escort, and isolation" at a military site shows the "high cost of false alarms to avoid a single missed attack".

  19. On-board messages reveal threat was passed on by air traffic controlpublished at 11:01 GMT
    11:01 GMTBreaking

    By Emma Pengelly

    Aer Lingus flight El402, from Dublin to Rome, sent a series of messages between the aircraft and ground stations.

    These messages have been interpreted by aviation safety expert Marco Chan, who says the messages in the log give "a clear account of the diversion".

    The ACARS (Aircraft Communications Addressing and Reporting System) messages were published by Airframes and can be considered reliable for what was transmitted and when.

    • At 07:51 GMT - The aircraft reports it is diverting to Paris Charles de Gaulle airport
    • 07:54 - The crew tell Dublin operations that air traffic control has passed on a bomb-on-board threat
    • 08:03 - The crew reports that the fuel level required to proceed to Rome is no longer assured
    • 08:28 - Weather reports for Toulouse, Brest and Nantes, suggesting alternate destinations are being considered
    • 08:40 - Plane reports diversion to Evreux Air Base, west of Paris, on government order

    Chan, who is also a former airline pilot, says that although ACARS spoofing - the act of sending false messages - is possible, corroboration between the messages and known details of the affected flight makes it very unlikely in this case.

More now from Bulgaria, where the air force says it successfully carried out an "air policing" mission following bomb threats to three flights.

As we just reported, three Ryanair flights to Sofia, from Copenhagen, Madrid and London Stansted, were reportedly targeted by the suspected bomb threats.

The air force says MiG-29 fighter jets took off from Bulgaria's Graf Ignatievo Air Base at 10:11 local time (08:11 GMT) to "intercept and escort the civilian aircraft".

"One landed at Plovdiv Airport, and the other two at Vasil Levski Airport in Sofia," the air force adds.

11:08 GMT

Mircea Barbu
In Bucharest

In Bulgaria, the Ministry of Transport has confirmed that bomb threats were made against three aircraft arriving in the country.

Two of the aircraft have already landed at Vasil Levski Airport. The flight from Madrid landed at 10:15, while the flight from Copenhagen arrived in Sofia at 10:19. No explosive devices have been found.

The third aircraft, arriving from London Stansted, is yet to land and is expected to arrive at Plovdiv Airport.

All three aircraft are operated by Ryanair.

On-board messages reveal threat was passed on by air traffic controlpublished at 11:01 GMT
11:01 GMTBreaking

By Emma Pengelly

Aer Lingus flight El402, from Dublin to Rome, sent a series of messages between the aircraft and ground stations.

These messages have been interpreted by aviation safety expert Marco Chan, who says the messages in the log give "a clear account of the diversion".

The ACARS (Aircraft Communications Addressing and Reporting System) messages were published by Airframes and can be considered reliable for what was transmitted and when.

  • At 07:51 GMT - The aircraft reports it is diverting to Paris Charles de Gaulle airport
  • 07:54 - The crew tell Dublin operations that air traffic control has passed on a bomb-on-board threat
  • 08:03 - The crew reports that the fuel level required to proceed to Rome is no longer assured
  • 08:28 - Weather reports for Toulouse, Brest and Nantes, suggesting alternate destinations are being considered
  • 08:40 - Plane reports diversion to Evreux Air Base, west of Paris, on government order

Chan, who is also a former airline pilot, says that although ACARS spoofing - the act of sending false messages - is possible, corroboration between the messages and known details of the affected flight makes it very unlikely in this case.

Flight into Rome was escorted by Italian fighter jets after bomb threatpublished at 10:51 GMT
10:51 GMTBreaking

Davide Ghiglione
In Rome

A spokesperson for Aeroporti di Roma tells the BBC that security checks have now been completed on a Finnair flight from Helsinki that landed safely at Rome Fiumicino Airport at around 09:30 this morning, following anonymous reports of a suspected bomb threat.

The aircraft was escorted into the airport by Italian Air Force fighter jets, while bomb disposal operations were launched in line with standard security procedures.

Police bomb disposal experts carried out the inspections, which returned a negative result, with no explosives found on board, according to information received from the airport.

The spokesperson said the security checks proceeded as normal and had no impact on airport operations.
