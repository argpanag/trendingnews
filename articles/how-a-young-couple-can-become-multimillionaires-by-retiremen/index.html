How a young couple can become multimillionaires by retirement age, according to an expert | CNN Business

TheTools · 21.11.2026 11:00 · source · permalink

When you have decades to make money and save, achieving long-term financial goals is certainly possible with a little consistency and strategy.

In this week’s Ask the Expert, certified financial planner Douglas Boneparth offers one young couple some advice about how they can retire as multimillionaires and, if the numbers make sense, own rental property.

A reader from Georgia wrote: “Have about $108,000 saved. $75 grand in a Roth. Rest in mutual fund. Any advice on what to do with the money? Would like to retire a multimillionaire and own rental property. Me, 34; wife, 29. Make $125,000 yearly. $30 grand emergency fund in a mutual fund.”

DOUG: I love this question because you and your wife are still young, you’re making $125,000 a year, you already have $108,000 saved, and you have a fully funded emergency fund. You’re doing a lot right here, so let’s build on it.

First, a quick note on your emergency savings sitting in a mutual fund. I hope you mean a money market fund, which invests in high-quality, short-term government debt. Because if it’s any other type of mutual fund, depending on what it invests in, that may not be the right home for money you need to access quickly and without risk. Emergency funds are meant to be stable and liquid. So take a second look if you’re not sure.

Now, to your bigger goals. Retiring as multimillionaires is achievable and time is your greatest asset right now (I mean that literally). The math of compound growth rewards people who start early and stay consistent, and you’ve already started.

Keep maxing out that Roth IRA every year. Tax-free growth over the next 30-plus years is one of the most powerful tools available to you, and at your income level, you should take full advantage of it while you can.

If you have access to a 401(k) through work, that’s the next place to focus, especially if there’s an employer match on the table.

On the rental property dream, we covered this in a recent issue, so I’ll keep it brief here. It absolutely can be a wealth-building tool but go in with eyes open. Run the real numbers, not the optimistic ones, and make sure you’re financially ready to carry the property if things don’t go as planned. Rental income is great. Becoming a reluctant landlord with a vacancy and a broken HVAC in the same month is less great.

You’re in a strong position. Stay consistent. Keep investing. And let time do the heavy lifting.

Tip of the week: Ask your credit card issuer for a lower rate

Surveys suggest most consumers who ask their bank to knock points off their credit card interest rate do get rate reductions, according to Matt Schulz in his book “Ask Questions, Save Money, Make More.”

Your best chance of getting your rate lowered is if you have had the card for a while, have a history of paying on time and have good credit.

Also helpful? Be nice when you ask, said Schulz, LendingTree’s chief consumer finance analyst. No one likes a crank.

When you’re carrying a balance from month to month, a lower rate will reduce your total debt payments.

Schulz offered this example: Say you have a $5,000 balance at 24%. If you are only able to pay $200 a month, by having an issuer reduce your rate to 19%, you will save more than $500 in overall interest payments and pay off your debt three months faster.

If you’re able to pay more than $200 a month, your savings and paydown schedule are even better.

But he recommends waiting to ask for a lower credit card rate if you’re about to apply for a big loan (eg, for a home or car). That’s because the card issuer may pull your credit report before making a decision, and that may temporarily knock your credit score down a few points. That, in turn, could harm your ability to get the best rate on your mortgage or auto loan.
