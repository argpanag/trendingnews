Nine Stock Falls 5.4% After Premier League Renewal—But the Dividend Distorts the Move

TheTools · 10.09.2026 20:42 · source · permalink

Nine Entertainment shares closed at A$0.79 on Thursday, down 5.4%, after the Australian media group locked up Premier League rights through the 2033/34 season. The screen looked harsher than the market’s actual verdict: NEC went ex-dividend for A$0.03 on the same day. Against the A$0.805 theoretical ex-dividend price, the fall was closer to 1.9%.

That distinction matters, but it does not make the deal automatically bullish. The renewal gives Stan Sport an unusually long runway and holds the initial step-up in rights fees in check. It also commits Nine to years of content inflation at a time when shareholders need streaming profit—not merely subscriber growth—to offset pressure in traditional television.

What Nine actually bought

Nine said it extended exclusive Australian streaming and broadcast rights for six seasons, from 2028/29 through 2033/34. Every Premier League match will remain live and exclusive on Stan Sport. Two seasons are still left on the existing agreement.

The accompanying ASX filing contains the investor-relevant terms. Nine expects the FY29 rights fee to be broadly in line with FY28, followed by an increase of roughly 3% a year across the extension. The absolute fee was not disclosed.

There is another moving part. Contributions inherited from the old Optus arrangement will end. Nine says removing legacy discounts for former Optus subscribers, along with cost and revenue initiatives, should offset that effect. Until management provides the underlying dollars, investors cannot independently test the claim.

Why Stan can afford the bet—for now

The first year of Premier League coverage produced evidence that the property can earn its place. In Nine’s FY26 results, Stan revenue rose 16% to A$569.0 million and EBITDA climbed 34% to A$80.6 million. Its margin widened to 14.2% from 12.3%, even as costs rose 13% to A$488.4 million, primarily because of higher sports investment.

Average Stan Sport subscriptions increased almost 50%, with the Premier League the main driver. Total Stan paying subscribers were about 2.3 million, average subscriptions rose 4%, and average revenue per user increased 8% as more customers took the sports add-on and a July price increase flowed through.

Those numbers are the strongest argument for the extension. Recent revenue and ARPU growth comfortably exceeded the future 3% rights-cost escalator, while Stan EBITDA has more than doubled from A$40 million in FY21 to A$81 million in FY26. A fixed destination for English football also reduces churn risk and gives Nine time to build advertising around Stan Sport.

The share-price move needs an ex-dividend adjustment

NEC fell from Wednesday’s A$0.835 close to A$0.79, touching A$0.775 intraday on 14.2 million shares. That was roughly 81% more volume than the previous session, according to delayed ASX price data.

But Thursday was also the ex-date for Nine’s A$0.03 unfranked final dividend, payable October 22. A shareholder buying after the cutoff no longer receives that cash, so a mechanical price adjustment was expected. The stock’s A$0.79 finish was only A$0.015 below the dividend-adjusted reference of A$0.805. The market was cautious, not nearly as emphatically negative as the raw 5.4% decline suggests.

The metric NEC investors should watch

The renewal will work if Stan can keep incremental subscription and advertising revenue ahead of incremental sports costs. Subscriber growth alone is a poor scorecard: premium rights can pull users into a service while consuming the economics they create.

The cleanest test is therefore Stan’s EBITDA margin. It improved 1.9 percentage points in FY26 despite the first season of Premier League expense. Continued expansion would show that price increases, lower discounts and advertising are absorbing the rights bill. A reversal—especially if Sport subscriptions flatten after the initial migration from Optus—would turn the long contract from an asset into a fixed-cost burden.

Nine’s wider balance sheet adds discipline to that test. FY26 continuing-business EBITDA rose 17% to A$378.8 million, but net debt finished at about A$658 million after the QMS acquisition. That is manageable at 1.7 times leverage, yet it leaves less room for a sports-rights mistake than Stan’s subscriber headline implies.

For NEC holders, the deal removes a strategic uncertainty and caps near-term fee inflation. It does not remove execution risk. The next proof point is whether Stan can preserve its 14.2% margin as the Premier League matures from a powerful acquisition tool into an expensive retention product.
