EXPLAINED | Why Are Neighbours Taking Elon Musk’s AI Power Plant to Court?

TheTools · 01.11.2026 16:45 · source · permalink

Elon Musk’s artificial intelligence company xAI has failed to get a pollution lawsuit thrown out. On October 30, US District Judge Debra M. Brown allowed claims brought by the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, or NAACP, and its Mississippi branch against xAI and subsidiary MZX Tech to proceed.

The dispute concerns gas turbines in Southaven, Mississippi, supplying electricity to the Colossus 2 data centre across the state line in Memphis, Tennessee. The centre supports Grok, xAI’s chatbot. The plaintiffs allege that the companies built and operated the turbines without required air permits.

The judge rejected the companies’ dismissal arguments. The ruling does not settle liability or award damages.

Because the computers behind it need electricity.

A data centre houses servers that store information and perform calculations. AI systems use computing power both to train models and to generate responses. Running that equipment requires a dependable electricity supply.

Producing power close to a data centre gives its operator a dedicated source of electricity. The commercial attraction is speed: companies racing to expand AI can build generation alongside computing capacity. Reuters has documented how this rush for off-grid power, serving individual customers rather than the wider electricity network, can outpace environmental oversight.

At Southaven, natural gas supplies the fuel. Burning it produces hot gases that turn turbine blades. The rotating machinery drives a generator, producing electricity for the computers. Exhaust gases leave the equipment, creating the pollution-control problem at the heart of this dispute.

Air pollution, noise and what prolonged exposure could mean for their health.

The NAACP’s complaint describes members who live, work, worship and study near the plant. It says concerns about emissions have led some to spend less time outdoors and invite vulnerable relatives over less often. These are the plaintiffs’ accounts.

One concern is nitrogen oxides, gases associated with burning fuel. Nitrogen dioxide can irritate airways and aggravate asthma. Nitrogen oxides also help form harmful ozone and fine particles in the atmosphere. Children, older people and people with asthma can be particularly vulnerable.

Those established health risks do not, by themselves, prove that this plant caused a particular resident’s illness. That requires evidence about actual emissions, exposure and health outcomes.

Because the company argued that their portability and limited intended use placed them outside the permit requirements at issue.

Mississippi’s environmental regulator had accepted an exemption for turbines remaining attached to portable units and staying on site for no more than a year. The company relied on that interpretation.

The federal judge rejected the mobile-source defence, finding that the turbines did not qualify for the claimed exemption under the applicable rules.

The distinction matters beyond the label. Air permits can require scrutiny of emissions and pollution-control equipment before major sources are built and operated. The plaintiffs argue that bypassing those requirements deprived neighbouring communities of protections they should have received.

It says it is investing in emissions controls, reducing noise and replacing temporary generation with a permitted permanent power plant.

In a July 30 site update, the company listed 69 temporary turbines and said it had agreed a removal timetable with Mississippi’s regulator. It also said local air-quality monitoring met or exceeded federal standards. That is the company’s assessment; the update does not independently establish residents’ exposure.

The regulator’s agreement records a separate March permit authorising construction of 41 permanent turbines. That permission should not be confused with authorisation for the disputed temporary equipment.

A permit for the permanent plant does not settle whether the earlier equipment was lawfully operated. The dispute turns on which equipment required approvals and whether the claimed exemption was valid.

Because it says pollution threatens its members and surrounding communities, including predominantly Black neighbourhoods.

Its argument concerns who bears the local costs of infrastructure serving a much wider customer base. The computers may serve distant users, while people living nearby experience the plant’s noise and emissions.

The Clean Air Act allows qualifying citizens and organisations to bring certain enforcement cases. xAI challenged the NAACP’s right to proceed and the constitutionality of that mechanism. The judge rejected those arguments at this stage.

India is explicitly asking states to consider those constraints before facilitating data centres.

NITI Aayog’s Data Centre Evaluation Framework, released on October 14, calls for assessing electricity-grid readiness, promoting renewable power backed by storage, and examining local water stress and impacts on public supplies. It also addresses land, environmental sustainability and the economic value of projects.

The framework is a planning tool for states. It does not establish that Indian projects face the same allegations as Southaven.

The useful comparison is practical: a data-centre investment announcement needs to be assessed alongside the power supply, cooling arrangements, pollution controls and infrastructure needed to support it.

The lawsuit can proceed, and the plaintiffs are also seeking an injunction, a court order restricting operations. Their lawyers’ October 30 update said that request remained undecided. The dismissal ruling itself did not order a shutdown.

The next questions concern what the court orders, which turbines remain operational, and how the transition to permanent power changes emissions. Evidence from permits, operating records and independent monitoring will be crucial to establishing what the plant actually releases and what nearby residents breathe.
