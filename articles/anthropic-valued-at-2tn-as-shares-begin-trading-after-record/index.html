Who are Anthropic’s seven co-founders, now worth $180bn?

Dan Milmo and Aisha Down

The paper fortunes of Anthropic’s seven co-founders reached a total of more than $180bn on Thursday as shares in their company started trading at a price ahead of expectations.

The opening price of $111.50 per share valued the entrepreneurs’ stakes – including chief executive Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela – at between $26.5bn and $27bn each. The founders have pledged to donate 80% of their shareholdings in Anthropic to philanthropic causes, which would equate to around $150bn at the current share price.

Other investors generating considerable wealth from the float and the late share price surge include the Hollywood actor Ashton Kutcher and Madonna’s manager, Guy Oseary, whose venture capital firm, Sound Ventures, reportedly owns 0.5% as an early investor. That stake would be worth $10bn at the opening price.

Anthropic has also committed 5% of its shares to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, which will “explore large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations”. The company has said some of the proceeds could be used to address AI’s impact on the job market, though fears the technology will lead to significant job losses have yet to be realised.

Anthropic’s IPO is widely expected to channel vast sums into the coffers of a powerful social movement called effective altruism, via the many millions of dollars that its 5,300 staff will have made from the flotation. EA, as it is known, crystallised on university campuses in the US and UK in the early 2010s. It encourages its members to donate a substantial portion of their income to a set of specific causes which it deems high-priority. These include things like pandemic prevention, biorisk, nuclear safety and – overwhelmingly – the risks posed by advanced AI.

Anthropic’s co-founders, including Dario Amodei, are associated with EA. Amodei took a pledge to donate his income through the organisation Giving What We Can, which is associated with the movement. However, he now disavows his association and has said he does not consider himself an effective altruist.

In 2023, he told The New York Times’ Hard Fork podcast he was “sympathetic to a number of the ideas” promoted by EA, but against “dividing people up into tribes”.

Richard Partington and Kalyeena Makortoff

Booming demand for artificial intelligence will force the world’s most powerful central banks to raise interest rates, the head of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) has warned.

Kristalina Georgieva, the managing director of the Washington-based fund, said demand for AI hardware and related technology products was pushing up consumer prices around the world.

She said that, alongside the energy shock from the war in the Middle East, this was adding to inflationary pressures around the globe.

“This calls for a tightening of monetary policy in many economies,” she said.

Speaking at the 2026 Apec summit in Shenzhen, Georgieva warned that both factors would push global inflation to 4.7% this year. She added that the rapid rollout of AI was also contributing to stronger than expected economic growth in several countries, despite the headwinds from the Iran war.

Georgieva said that while the energy shock would eventually dissipate, “AI is for ever” – making it a significant driver of world trade, competitiveness, productivity and economic growth.

AI hardware and related tech products now account for more than one-tenth of the world goods trade, a share that is rapidly rising, she said. But some countries were benefiting more from investment than others.

“To put it simply: you snooze, you lose,” she added. “Countries need digital infrastructure, labour markets and workers that are ready for AI, and risk mitigation – especially to control financial stability and cybersecurity risks.”

Lisa Bonos in San Francisco

Sameer Shariff, co-founder and CEO of English language education company Cambly, noticed a buzz of energy among Anthropic employees as they were leaving the company’s headquarters in San Francisco yesterday.

Over the past two years, a few employees have left his company to join Anthropic. He’s been thinking about them today.

“I’m happy for them. It’s a big day for the market and for AI,” Shariff said, while chatting with a friend at Joe and the Juice in downtown San Francisco on Thursday. So far, “Anthropic has been funded by all these investors with the inside scoop. But this is a test of how Wall Street responds … all of a sudden the general public has access to participate.”

Shariff said he’s curious how the next six to 12 months will go. “I’m a big fan,” he added.

As we await the close of market valuation of Anthropic at the end of its first day on the market, Rebecca Walser, chief investment officer at US-based Walser Wealth Management, wrote: “Anthropic’s IPO is exactly what the markets need to revive overall sentiment, which has been depressed with all of the negativity in the AI complex over the past several weeks.”

Walser added: “It’s possible that Anthropic could see volatility and downside moves during the Jensen Huang Senate testimony after Thanksgiving, because that is likely to reignite AI fears.

“But if that happens, it could be a good entry point for investors who want to buy Anthropic.”

Lisa Bonos in San Francisco

On a drizzly morning in downtown San Francisco after a massive storm the day before, the mood was sleepy in the Andytown coffee shop atop Salesforce Tower Park. When asked about Anthropic’s IPO today, one barista said “What’s that?” — a clue that not everyone in this tech hub is obsessed with AI.

Anthropic workers do frequent the coffee shop, but they hadn’t seen any today, her colleague said. One patron, smiled when asked if he worked at the maker of Claude. “No, but I wish I did,” he said.

Out on the street, foot traffic seemed lighter than normal, likely due to the weather. But still some tech workers trickled into their offices at 9:30 a.m.

“I wish I was in that building,” a Meta worker, who declined to give his name, said entering his downtown San Francisco office and gesturing to one of Anthropic’s offices across the street. His friends who work at Anthropic have “big plans for what to do with their newfound wads of cash,” he said. “Some people are planning to backpack Asia,” he said. But mostly, they plan to keep working and liquidate slowly, he added.

Anthropic’s market value could exceed that of Meta Platforms, the parent company of Facebook and Instagram, and make it more valuable than Coca-Cola, IBM, Starbucks, Delta Air Lines and General Motors combined, AP reported.

At that size, Anthropic could quickly join indexes that track wide swaths of the stock market, which in turn could bring in even more investment.

The company expects up to 20% of its Class A shares in the IPO to go to what are called “retail investors.”

But as part of the IPO, Anthropic is creating a special class of stock for CEO Dario Amodei, President Daniela Amodei and five other co-founders, giving them 50.1% of the voting power of all the company’s outstanding shares.

That means the founders as a group could overshadow every other shareholder and dictate issues put before all investors, like how to vote on a potential sale of the company.

“Roll up, roll up, who wants to buy shares in a company whose technology “could pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity”, as the prospectus had it? Virtually everybody, as it turned out. Anthropic flew out of the blocks on its first day as a listed company.

From the point of view of the promoters, the result was as good as it gets. The final pricing for the shares at $83 apiece was meaningfully higher than the indicated range of $70 to $78, but still left room for a big initial “pop”. There were positive vibes all round – especially for the battalion of investment banks collecting $550m of underwriting fees.

And, to be fair, it wasn’t nailed-on that the show would go so swimmingly. There was a late risk that investor sentiment towards the AI universe could be jinxed – or Jensened – by the soap opera at Nvidia. More generally, there was a worry that the AI bubble, if that’s what it is, could burst when somebody tried to unload too much stock on the stock market in one go.

Anthropic is losing substantial amounts of money, according to its investor prospectus. In the first half of 2026, the company reported losses of $104bn in the first nine months of 2026. Those costs will continue to grow as the firm works to secure more compute. The company spent about $18.8bn on AI infrastructure in the first nine months of 2026.

Anthropic’s IPO will influence how Wall Street feels about other AI companies

Anthropic has become the first frontier AI lab to be publicly listed, enjoying a premium on its share price as a result of beating OpenAI to market.

The startup’s performance will serve as a bellwether for the AI industry and influence investors’ appetite for the massive costs of developing advanced AI.

Evan Schlossman, a principal at Neostellar, a publicly traded venture capital firm, said that investors are looking at whether Anthropic’s growth rate is sustainable and offset some of the costs of developing the AI.

“You’ve seen an astronomical growth rate in terms of the top line,” said Evan Schlossman, a principal at Neostellar, a publicly traded venture capital firm. “The question investors are going to be looking at leading up to the IPO, and once it’s public, is what happens to that growth rate.”

Further to that, all the major US stock market indexes are down, after an early morning rally quickly evaporated.

As of a few minutes ago, the Nasdaq sank 0.8%, the S&P 500 0.99%, and the Dow Jones 1.49%, and Anthropic’s share was ticking around $98.

In the last 45 minutes since opening, Anthropic shares dipped to a low of $95, before rebounding to just above $100, where it is now hovering.

Anthropic’s initial public offering left more than half of the institutional investors bidding for stock without any shares, Bloomberg (paywall) reports, citing people familiar with the matter.

Per the report: “Allocations of the shares were heavily skewed toward US-based investors, with a large proportion of orders coming in from outside the country getting no allocation, they said. About 1,200 institutional investors placed orders, some of the people said.”

“It’s typically quite hard to get served on US IPOs when you’re located in Europe, particularly in very sought after deals like this one,” Christophe Pouchoy, a technology fund manager at La Financiere de l’Echiquier, told Bloomberg.

A 34% ‘pop’ for a newly floated stock is not terribly unusual.

And while it suggests Anthropic could have raised more money in its IPO (by selling its shares at a higher price), there are sometimes good reasons not to do that.

Underwriters often decide to price an IPO below value, to attract buyers and ensure strong demand.

Dr. Paulina Roszkowska, Lecturer in Finance at Bayes Business School in London, argues that Anthropic’s board was right to “leave something on the table for their new shareholders”, explaining:

“Resist the temptation to squeeze out the highest possible price. Should an AI correction take 20 or 30 per cent off the shares in 2026/2027, investors will be less forgiving if management sold them perfection.

“After all, they may well need these investors again. With heavy losses and enormous spending plans, today’s generous valuation could become tomorrow’s fundraising problem. Investors remember who left something on the table and who took the table too.
