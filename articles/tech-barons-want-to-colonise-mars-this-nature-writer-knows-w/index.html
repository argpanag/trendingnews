Tech barons want to colonise Mars. This nature writer knows why that's a bad idea

TheTools · 28.11.2026 09:57 · source · permalink

After a weekend which saw the latest COP climate talks end conspicuously without a deal I’m not sure whether author and environmental campaigner Robert Macfarlane’s first Reith lecture on Radio 4 on Tuesday morning could be considered good or bad timing.

At a moment when climate change seems a clear and present danger there still appears to be no real will to tackle it. Does that mean Macfarlane is shouting into the wind?

But then what is the alternative? Despair, Macfarlane argued, is a luxury. Hope is a discipline.

The gist of Macfarlane’s first lecture was to challenge what he saw as human exceptionalism; our idea that we are in some way separate from the flora and fauna we share the planet with.

“It’s become so invisible to us,” he suggested. “It is the default planetary operating system.” But it is, at heart, he suggested, a pernicious idea.

“This story that humans are innately separate from and superior to all other species is a tenacious and a powerful one, but it is only a story,” Macfarlane argued. “It is not a universal or an innate pathology, and as such it can be and is being contested by other stories.”

Those are the stories he wants to tell. In this first lecture that desire took him from the ancient epic of Gilgamesh to the idea of tech barons colonising Mars, via medieval theology, the Enlightenment and the current Artemis space mission.

Macfarlane described the moment we are living through right now as an emergency - a time of flash floods and brutal heatwaves and species loss on a catastrophic scale.

Global wild vertebrate animal populations have declined by an average of 73 per cent in 50 years, he said. Which means? “Three-quarters of backboned wild animals gone in my lifetime,” he explained.

How do we address it? Probably not through talking shops like COP which seem incapable of delivering consequential change, Macfarlane suggested. And, he added, let’s not disappear off to live on another planet. The colonisation of Mars is an “oligarch’s rapture dream”, he argued.

“It offers a new life to a chosen few once Earth’s functions have been degraded to the point of inhabitability.”

In short, we can’t let the richest among us chuck our own planet in the bin.

What he’s calling for is a reengagement with our own sense of empathy and compassion. “The best antidote to despair is repair.”

Easier said than done as the Q&A after the lecture reminded us, with questions about the possible impact of his ideas on the economy and the ongoing cost of living crisis. Can we see the big picture if we can’t see through the reality of bills and food costs?

Meanwhile, we keep consuming and consuming and consuming. How is your Christmas shopping going, by the way?

Naga Munchetty discussed heavy menstrual bleeding on her BBC 5 Live programme. (Image: Dean Calma / IAEA Imagebank / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0)

Listening to Naga Munchetty’s mid-morning show on 5 Live at lunchtime also on Tuesday I was reminded that she’s off to host the station’s breakfast show in January. I wonder, does that mean her regular women’s health slot That Time of the Month will be downgraded or even disappear? It’s hard to imagine a fast-moving breakfast show will be willing or able to set aside half an hour for an extensive chat on heavy menstrual bleeding as Tuesday’s mid-morning show did.

It would be a shame if it disappears altogether. I think this has been one of the real pluses of Munchetty’s time on 5 Live - a platform for discussion of women’s health that doesn’t feel siloed from the usual mixture of politics and news headlines.

Because this stuff matters. “Every minute somewhere in the US a woman needs a blood transfusion because of her periods,” Edinburgh University’s Dr Jackie Maybin told Munchetty this week.

That said, Tuesday’s half hour slot was full of positivity. Dr Maybin argued that we have reached an “inflection point” in women’s health. New treatments are emerging for heavy menstrual bleeding that don’t override the entire reproductive system. And there is money finally being properly invested in research into women’s health. “Funders around the world are really stepping up.”

Here’s hoping.

Finally, I was driving home from Glasgow on Sunday night listening to 6 Music’s Dream Time, presented by Zakia Sewell. It was a special edition with music by and for kids to mark World Children’s Day. The result was joyous. At one point a children’s school choir sang David Bowie’s Space Oddity, followed immediately by a performance of Mozart’s Minuet and Trio in G Major, K. 1e/f, written when the composer was just five years old. That kind of juxtaposition is the great, unexpected thrill of radio that an algorithm just can’t give you.

Listen Out For: Another Country with Rianne Downey, Radio Scotland, Tuesday, December 1, 8pm

Just a mention for Radio Scotland’s regular Americana and alt-country slot. Ricky Ross has made it his own over the years, but sitting in tonight is Scottish singer-songwriter (and regular Paul Heaton collaborator) Rianne Downey, who should bring a different flavour to the show.
