Which tea is best for heart health? Experts reveal all

TheTools · 21.10.2026 10:21 · source · permalink

For a nation of tea drinkers, the fresh verdict delivered by scientists is a welcome one: a cuppa is good for your heart.

Experts say Britain's favourite beverage is linked to an 11 per cent lower risk of deadly conditions like heart disease.

It's the latest study to tie the drink – around 100million cups of which are sipped every day in the UK – to a healthier heart.  

Researchers at Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China followed 486,000 people, comparing their tea habits with their risk of vascular disease, the term for conditions affecting the blood vessels. 

Around 15,000 were diagnosed over nine years. 

People who drank their tea 'burning hot' faced a higher risk, while, intriguingly, those who drank it at a moderate strength were better protected.

The best protected group, however, were those who drank six to eight cups a day. 

But some scientists say classic English Breakfast is just one of a roster of teas that are vital for protecting you from the most common deadly diseases. Here, they reveal which brew is the best.

The latest study follows a series of papers showing similar benefits of our national hot drink.

A 2024 review involving nearly two million people, published in the journal Epidemiology and Health, found that those drinking the most black tea had a 14 per cent lower risk of death from heart disease.

Researchers said one-and-a-half to three cups a day was the 'sweet spot' for benefits.

Another 2021 review suggested that the plant compounds flavonoids – in particular flavan-3-ols – found in tea, were a possible reason behind tea's  benefits for heart health. 

Studies suggest flavonoids can improve the way the blood vessels function by relaxing them, reducing pressure on the heart to pump blood around the body.

The review also concluded that consistently having two cups of unsweetened tea per day offered the correct levels of flavonoids to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease, including heart failure and stroke.

'Flavonoids may support the health and function of our blood vessels,' says Nichola Ludlam-Raine, dietitian and author of How Not To Eat Ultra-Processed.

'Randomised controlled trials have found small reductions in blood pressure with regular black tea consumption.'

Ms Ludlam-Raine also says that what we add to our tea matters too in terms of our heart health.

'A traditional cup of tea with a splash of milk can absolutely form part of a healthy balanced diet,' she said.

'But regularly adding several teaspoons of sugar increases our intake of free sugars without adding much nutritionally. It's the overall dietary pattern that matters most for heart health.'

Green tea comes from the same plant as ordinary black tea, Camellia sinensis, but is processed differently. 

The leaves are steamed or pan-fired soon after picking, which helps preserve their green colour and many of their natural plant compounds.

As well as flavonoids, it contains powerful antioxidants called catechins – the best known being epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) – that have been linked to better heart health. 

Lab research in 2018 found that EGCG could help break up the fatty plaques that block blood vessels and lead to dangerous complications. 

At the time, however, experts at the British Heart Foundation (BHF) urged caution. 

'Our bodies are very good at breaking down EGCG, so swapping your cuppa for green tea is unlikely to make a big difference with respect to your heart health,' said Professor Jeremy Pearson, the former associate medical director at the BHF.

But Ms Ludlam-Raine says that for most healthy adults, two to four cups a day can still provide benefits.

'That amount provides a useful source of polyphenols (plant compounds that can improve blood vessel function and reduce high cholesterol) without pushing caffeine intake too high, especially compared with several cups of coffee,' she added.

Also known as red bush tea, rooibos is caffeine-free – unlike black and green tea. 

Experts warn that too much caffeine can be bad for the heart, although tea is generally well within safe levels.

International guidance suggests more than 400mg a day could harm heart health but you would need eight to 13 cups of tea to reach that limit.

Even still, rooibos may come in handy for anyone looking to reduce their caffeine intake while protecting their heart health.

A 2023 scientific review in the Journal of Public Health in Africa found that people who drank it for several weeks lowered their cholesterol – a fatty substance that can clog the arteries and lead to heart attack and stroke – by nearly 10 per cent. 

Meanwhile, a 2020 review of 54 studies found that rooibos tea consumption also reduced harmful oxidative stress – a chemical reaction in the body that can damage blood vessels.

'It also appeared to possess other promising general effects on blood sugar levels, bone, liver, cognitive and respiratory health too,' says Dr Emma Derbyshire from the Tea Advisory Panel.

'It is commonly enjoyed both for its flavour and as a gentle, hydrating beverage that can be consumed throughout the day and is a great drink for the evening prior to sleep.'

Berry and fruit teas are caffeine-free too – and may also play a role in heart health, experts say.

This is due to the presence of polyphenols.

However, the amounts in a cup of tea are small compared with eating the fruit fresh. 

'Fruit teas are naturally aromatic and often have a sweet or tangy flavour without added sugar,' says Dr Derbyshire.

'They are often used as an alternative to sugary soft drinks, offering flavour and variety without caffeine.'

While liquorice tea has been linked to anti-inflammatory and antibacterial benefits, it is not recommended for heart health.

It contains a natural compound called glycyrrhizin, which can make the body retain salt and lose potassium. 

That can lead to higher blood pressure, irregular heart rhythms and fluid retention. 

For that reason, Dr Derbyshire urges caution.

'Regular or high intake may affect blood pressure and potassium balance in sensitive individuals particularly those with [high blood pressure] or kidney conditions,' she says.

'For this reason, it is generally recommended as an occasional rather than daily tea, especially in people with cardiovascular risk factors.'

While the evidence for tea is intriguing, coffee is often thought to be better for the heart. 

However, because of its higher caffeine content, some experts advise tea instead. 

Like tea, coffee contains polyphenols, particularly chlorogenic acids, which have been found to protect blood vessels from damage, says Ro Huntriss, a dietitian and chief nutrition officer at the weight-loss platform Simple Life App. 

A 2026 meta-analysis of 13 studies and more than 600,000 people found that regularly drinking a moderate amount of coffee was linked to an 8 per cent lower risk of heart failure. 

'Moderate consumption is around two to four cups daily,' adds Ms Huntriss. 'Decaf counts too, as it retains many of the same healthful compounds.'

However, most of the studies linking coffee – and tea for that matter – to a lower heart risk are observational, meaning they can't prove cause and effect. 

Diet, lifestyle, smoking and other factors could have played a part. 

That was true of the latest tea study too: the people who benefited most did not smoke and were physically active.  

'There's no single food or drink that will transform our heart health; our overall diet and lifestyle matter much more,' says Dell Stanford, senior dietitian at the BHF.
