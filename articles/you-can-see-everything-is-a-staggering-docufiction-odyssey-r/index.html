You Can See Everything is 'a staggering docufiction odyssey' — read Empire's review

TheTools · 18.11.2026 21:14 · source · permalink

Elizabeth Holmes has big eyes. Cartoonishly large, sapphire orbs that you can get utterly lost in if you look long enough. Nathan Fielder — partnering with Primetime’s Lance Oppenheim as co-directors on this era-defining masterpiece of documentary cinema — loses himself a little in those eyes too. For Fielder, who has made a career of obliterating the boundaries of truth and fiction (see his satirical TV semi-docs Nathan For You and The Rehearsal), finds his obstinate match in someone determined to write her own narrative.

In the weeks before she is sent to prison for defrauding investors of half a billion dollars, Theranos founder Holmes and her husband Billy Evans invite Fielder, Oppenheim and a small crew into their home with the offer of “radical transparency” — though it quickly becomes evident there’s a limit to their openness. Fielder takes on the role of the resolute, sceptical interviewer, and Holmes makes for an evasive yet fascinating subject. She is so adamant that she hasn’t committed a crime that she seems to believe her own con. A confession that she used to never let people see her cry is paired with an artfully timed tear that slips down her face. At one point, she claims that she has never told a lie. She’s a master storyteller living in an alternate reality of her own making.

This is a riveting matryoshka doll of a movie that grows richer as it reveals layer upon thematic layer.

Oppenheim’s vibrant direction builds on Fielder’s ostensible mission to unpack who Elizabeth Holmes really is. Shots oscillate between the wide, beachside vistas of the couple’s isolated San Diego rental and extreme close-ups so tight that every pore is visible. The camera feels like an investigative tool itself, sometimes taking a closer look and sometimes stepping back to observe the bigger picture.

And that’s just the first half of You Can See Everything. From there, this three-hour behemoth splits wide open, once Holmes is imprisoned and Evans assumes her place as an unnerving subject in his own right. Perspectives shift, and the difference between authenticity and performance becomes all but imperceptible. This is a riveting matryoshka doll of a movie that grows richer as it reveals layer upon thematic layer. It is about, among other things, the futility of documentary filmmaking as a record of truth, the impossibility of human objectivity, and art’s ability to shift our perception of reality.

The fact that Holmes remains an enigma to the very end is precisely the point. You can take a look at everything. But the magic trick of this miraculous film is that the more you see, the less you truly know.
