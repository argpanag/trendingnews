Texas FHA fraud case: Recheck 2019-23 loans for fake income

Two Texas women are charged in a scheme involving falsified loan documents that could end up costing the Federal Housing Administration almost $6 million, illustrating how mortgages originated years earlier may still carry risk. 
The Department of Justice filed indictments against Houston-area residents Tammi Hunnicutt and Taylor Dickson, who made separate appearances in courtrooms in the Southern District of Texas this month. Hunnicutt and Dickson both face 11 counts for their alleged involvement in a scheme that defrauded lenders and federal agencies in a four-year period between late 2019 and 2023. 

According to filing documents, the two women submitted falsified pay stubs, tax forms and bank statements in their attempts to misrepresent the employment and income status of likely ineligible customers that would help them qualify for either conventional or FHA mortgages guaranteed by the U.S. government.

"By misrepresenting borrowers' financial qualifications, their actions exposed the FHA insurance program to approximately $5,890,112.30 in potential losses," said Grant Permenter, special agent in charge at the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's inspector general office, in a press release.

Details surrounding the current status or performance of the liens in question were not disclosed, nor were the identities of the lenders victimized. 

The prevalence of document fraud risk

Despite technological advancements this decade that are showing success in making asset, income and employment verification processes more efficient, the indictments are the latest to land in headlines in 2026, underscoring the lengths scammers have gone to commit fraud, with the financial damage only becoming apparent years later.

"Mortgage fraud undermines the integrity of our financial system and puts lenders, taxpayers and legitimate homebuyers at risk," said U.S. Attorney Aaron F. Reitz in the release. 

If the allegations are proven, Hunnicutt and Dickson each face up to 20 years behind bars and a potential $250,000 penalty for conspiracy or wire fraud. Both women could also see an additional 30 years in prison and a maximum $1 million fine for false statements to a mortgage lender.  

Long a source of potential fraud given the relative ease for perpetrators to create letters, pay stubs and other forms that look convincing, the Texas case showed how scammers could exploit flaws in the system to con lenders. The indictments come just a few months after two Florida fraudsters pleaded guilty to charges of fabricating pay stubs and employers that led to the wrongful origination of conventional mortgages between 2022 and 2025.

Recent technology developments show companies recognize the need for improvements to data verification processes and are taking steps to upgrade or add automation that could mitigate or eliminate such risk. 

This past summer saw a significant merger in the segment between the Truework platform parent Checkr and Truv that combines asset, income and employment verification capabilities under one roof. Similarly, verification software provider Informative Research unveiled a new version of its AccountChek product, which was rebuilt from the ground up, the company said.
