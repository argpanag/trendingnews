Champion athlete soils herself mid-race and keeps going, prompting fitness competition to change rules

A popular global fitness competition is changing its rules after an explosive incident at a Sept. 12 competition.

Australian professional athlete Joanna Wietrzyk won the race at HYROX Beijing — but the victory did not come without struggle.

During the race, Wietrzyk experienced fecal incontinence but continued competing. Despite the incident, judges and officials did not instruct her to stop, prompting concerns about potential health and safety risks for other competitors.

The incident has sparked online debate — some people have supported Wietrzyk for her perseverance, while others suggested that her accident posed a potential biohazard.

Wietrzyk reportedly addressed the incident in a social media post, which has since been deleted.

"Go hard or go home … a win is a win. In all seriousness, thank you for all the support. I’ll be back soon x," she wrote, according to multiple news outlets.

The athlete has since faced criticism from those who argue that her decision posed a health risk to other competitors.

Wietrzyk’s coach, Chris Bayens, posted a statement on social media in her defense. "What happened was an extraordinary set of circumstances that were unforeseen, rapidly evolving and almost impossible to fully comprehend in real time," he wrote on Instagram.

"Decisions were made under competition conditions by an athlete, judges and officials, all responding to a situation none of them could reasonably have prepared for."

Bayens continued that these decisions can be reviewed and lessons can be learned, but "judging someone’s intent, character or morality through the clarity of hindsight is neither fair nor honest."

"Sport occasionally produces moments that are imperfect and deeply human," he said. "Discussion is legitimate. The hatred and personal attacks that have followed are not."

Moritz Furste, co-founder of HYROX, also released a statement following the incident.

"I apologize to all that were directly or indirectly affected, as well as to everyone who felt like we did not handle the situation like we should have," he wrote. "HYROX made a mistake by not reacting immediately during the race. In the end, it is my job to foresee these potential incidents – and I did not."

The statement continued, "Of course, we started improving event processes, plus making rulebook changes immediately. As of this moment, there are new rules and procedures in place to prevent situations like this from happening again. We learn and try to get better every day."

What is HYROX?

According to HYROX, the race combines cardio with weight training, involving a total of eight kilometers (about five miles) of running and eight functional workout stations.

The structure of the competitions layers a one-kilometer run with one functional workout station, including SkiErg (a machine that simulates cross-country skiing), sled push, sled pull, burpee broad jumps, rowing, farmer's carry, sandbag lunges and wall balls.

Joanna Wietrzyk of Australia takes part in HYROX Beijing 2026 at the National Speed Skating Oval on Sept. 12, 2026, in Beijing, China. (Getty Images)

Launched in Germany in 2017, HYROX has grown rapidly worldwide, with recent reports putting participation at more than 1.4 million competitors globally. The organization describes the race as the world's fastest-growing fitness sport.

Physical risks

In an interview with Fox News Digital, California-based certified fitness trainer Ryan Read shared why the HYROX format can be so physically demanding.

"Your body doesn't get much time to fully recover between efforts," he said. "You may go from running directly to sled pushes, lunges or rowing. That forces your heart, lungs and muscles to work hard for a long period."

As the race progresses, maintaining proper form can become more difficult, according to Read, as the combination of intensity, duration and repetitive exercises can put a "significant amount of stress on the body."

"Muscles become fatigued, and the body uses up stored energy and fluids," he said. "During hard exercise, more blood is directed toward working muscles and away from the digestive system. That can make digestion more difficult."

"Real toughness also means knowing when your body is telling you to stop."

Running in particular creates a repetitive movement that can upset the stomach. Combining this with dehydration, heat and eating too close to competition can worsen symptoms, Read cautioned.

"This is why some athletes experience nausea, cramping, diarrhea or even vomiting during endurance events."

Participants compete in the burpee broad jumps event during the HYROX fitness race at the Bangkok International Trade and Exhibition Centre in Bangkok on March 21, 2026. (Amaury Paul/AFP)

There is a "big difference" between working through normal discomfort and ignoring a medical warning sign, according to the trainer.

Elite athletes are often conditioned to push through pain, a mindset that can intensify during competition. Adrenaline can also make it harder to recognize when the body is in distress, Read said.

Symptoms like vomiting, fainting, severe chest pain or extreme weakness "should not be treated as proof that someone is working hard enough."

"Pushing through certain symptoms can turn a manageable problem into a medical emergency," he emphasized. "Real toughness also means knowing when your body is telling you to stop."

Read suggested that fitness organizations should have "clear rules" for when an athlete needs to be evaluated or removed from a competition, and medical staff should be easily accessible throughout the event.

"Equipment that becomes contaminated with vomit, blood or other bodily fluids should be taken out of use immediately," he said. "Simply wiping it quickly and sending the next competitor through may not be enough."

Bodily fluids can contain germs that may spread through contaminated surfaces or direct contact, Read said.

"The exact risk depends on the fluid, organism and type of exposure. Competitor safety should come before keeping the event moving on schedule."
