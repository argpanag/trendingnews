'SNL' Wig Designer, Jodi Mancuso, Spills the Tea on This Year’s 'Dos

Though it may look effortless, bringing the TODAY Halloween show to the plaza each year is a well-coordinated symphony.

Of course, there are the TODAY anchors and co-hosts who dress up and perform as their respective characters. But what you might not know is that each of them has a team of operatives who help create their larger-than-life looks.

Among them is Jodi Mancuso, head hair designer for “Saturday Night Live.” Mancuso has been on the ‘SNL’ wig beat for more than 25 years and has nine Emmys to show for her incredible hair designs.

If you think that the wigs worn by the SNL cast or the TODAY hosts come pre-made in a box, think again. Each wig is custom-made by Mancuso and her team. The time it takes to build each of the wigs, and then style them, depends on the individual wig itself and how complex it is.

The average wig takes around 80 hours of work, she said. But, Mancuso explained, that she and her team can construct one in roughly half the time.

“We can do it in 40 hours for ‘SNL’ because we don’t have 80 hours,” she said, laughing.

“It’s quite intense,” she said.

It takes SNL's wig designer Jodi Mancuso and her team roughly 40 hours to create each of the TODAY hosts' Halloween wigs.  TODAY

TODAY

With that in mind, Mancuso’s crew works closely with the TODAY team to ensure everything is ready to go the morning of the Halloween show. She explained that the producers, newscaster and the costume design and makeup departments all work on their unique portions of the costumes. Then, the elements finally merge on the day of the big show.

“We have a lot of trust in each other,” said Mancuso of the process. “We’re all kind of in our own world, separated, styling one person and it all comes together that day.”

According to Mancuso, this year’s looks have been a bit easier than years past given that four of the TODAY co-hosts didn’t require wigs: Al, Willie, Laura and Sheinelle.

Of the five who did, Mancuso said that building Craig’s Snoop Dogg hair required the most time. Carson’s Ella Langley look was also a challenge, especially in getting Langley’s distinctive color just right.

Dylan’s “I Dream of Jeannie” hair was surprisingly tricky since, between the wig and the hat, the ‘do is heavier than it looks.

“It’s a lot of hair in that,” said Mancuso of the blonde, braided wig, which required the team to do some extra tweaking to make it wearable for Dylan.

Overall, Mancuso said that helping bring the TODAY Halloween extravaganza to life each year is always a good time.

“It’s fun. It’s for a good thing. They get excited,” said Mancuso of the TODAY anchors. “They get so excited about the people outside and showing everybody. So, it’s fun, it’s nice. We get joy out of it.”

Dylan Dreyer Reveals the Ab Workout That Helped Her Get Ready for her Epic 'I Dream of Jeannie'

Dylan Dreyer’s six-pack started with a piece of advice from her dad.

For TODAY’s annual Halloween extravaganza on Oct. 30, the co-anchor transformed into Jeannie from the classic sitcom “I Dream of Jeannie,” complete with a wig, a stunning wardrobe and a midriff-baring top that put her toned midsection center stage.

“I have always, except for when I was pregnant, had a six-pack,” Dylan tells TODAY.com. “And it’s literally because when I was younger, my dad always said to me, ‘Tighten your stomach.’”

Marc J. Franklin for TODAY

So she did, and she never stopped. Keeping her core tight as she went about her day became second nature, a built-in workout decades before it ever had a national audience.

“It’s not like I’m sitting there doing 1,000 sit-ups a day to get my stomach in shape,” she says.

(One caveat for anyone tempted to copy her: Physical therapists warn that constantly sucking in your stomach can lead to “hourglass syndrome,” a pattern in which the diaphragm can’t fully expand, causing shallow breathing, back pain and extra pressure on the pelvic floor.)

Still, Dylan didn’t leave the big reveal to chance.

“I have stepped up my game a little bit in the last couple weeks because I knew this costume was coming,” she says.

Her secret weapon is the Soto Method, a program created by trainer Hilary Hoffman. The name stands for “60 on, 10 on:” Each block opens with a 60-second isometric hold, followed by 10 moves meant to push the muscles to failure. Dylan’s go-to routine ends with a five-minute core finisher designed to leave the muscles burning.

Costume Designer Staci Greenbaum Takes Us Behind the Scenes of Halloween 2026

TODAY has been putting on a legendary Halloween show for decades! And in recent years, many of the costumes have been from the mind of costume designer Staci Greenbaum.

Greenbaum, who began collaborating with the TODAY team in 2023 when the theme was “Kellyoke,” says she loves being a part of TODAY’s costume extravaganza.

“Every year is something different, but this year’s been really fun,” Greenbaum told TODAY.com in a sit-down interview earlier this week.

The occasion isn’t without its unique challenges, according to the costume designer, who started with creating Savannah and Jenna’s “Friends” looks.

Given that the sitcom aired for 10 seasons, Greenbaum says it was tricky, pinning down a single iconic look for characters Rachel and Phoebe. Instead, she took inspiration from a variety of looks over the years.

Savannah Wetzel (left) and Staci Greenbaum (right) spent countless hours creating this year's Halloween costumes for TODAY.  Marc J. Franklin for TODAY

“We’re taking some liberties,” she explained of the costumes, which are more about capturing the essence of the characters, rather than duplicating specific outfits worn by cast members Jennifer Aniston and Lisa Kudrow.

Along with the “Friends” looks, Greenbaum said that other costumes this year, including Craig’s Snoop Dogg, Laura’s Simone Biles and Willie’s Johnny Carson, were more nods than they were real-life replications.

“We’re talking about costumes that span a range of different things that encapsulate NBC’s centennial. So, some of them have very direct inspiration and some of them don’t,” explained Greenbaum.

There was definitely some accurate cosplay among the costumes, too. Al’s Gumby costume, for example, was a real-life replica of Eddie Murphy’s ‘SNL’ character and Carson’s Ella Langley look was reproduced from her hit video for “Choosin’ Texas.” Dylan’s Jeannie costume was also a duplicate, mirroring Barbara Eden’s iconic maroon-and-pink genie outfit from the ‘60s show.

Of all the costumes this year, Sheinelle’s was likely the most inspired, Greenbaum explained. Rather than having Sheinelle dress up in the exact Rockettes costume, the designer said she took a cue from a joke Sheinelle made about wanting to be a tree for Halloween this year.

“I said, ‘Well, during Christmas, we have the tree lighting.’ So, her costume is more reminiscent of the Rockefeller Tree. It’s green, gold, red and silver and it has the same fringe and movement (of The Rockettes’ costume), but I’m making her what she asked for,” laughed Greenbaum.

Read on for other notable details from this year’s costumes:

  • Laura’s one-piece costume was made by the same manufacturer that creates the real Olympic leotards for Team USA.
  • Carson’s red halter top and suede chaps were custom-made by the costume team.
  • Savannah’s page outfit is a real NBC page uniform, borrowed from the program.
  • Al’s Gumby was made by the same “SNL” team that built the costume for Eddie Murphy’s return to Weekend Update in 2019.

What Is The NBC Page Program?

If you’ve ever watched sitcom “30 Rock,” then you’re probably familiar with Kenneth Parcell, the cheerful page with the can-do attitude, played by Jack McBrayer. The role is based on NBC’s real-life page program, which was launched in 1933 with the aim of fostering the careers of promising young professionals.

Indeed, many recognizable names have gotten their start in the page program, including Gregory Peck, Aubrey Plaza, Regis Philbin, Willard Scott, Ted Koppel and Michael Eisner, just to name a few!

The illustrious page program lasts anywhere from 12 to 16 months and draws thousands of applicants each year. Of them, less than 2% – or roughly 200 people a year – are accepted, making it one of the most coveted posts in media.
