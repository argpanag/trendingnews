Could history repeat itself? How past Super El Niño winters offer clues about what's ahead

TheTools · 24.10.2026 10:36 · source · permalink

For the East Coast in particular, the outcome of the historic Super El Niño will depend on the storm track, the availability of cold air, and how water temperatures influence developing systems.

Super El Niño is brewing to bring an impactful winter

Winter is near, and super El Niño is forecast to make it quite a winter to remember. FOX Weather Meteorologists Stephen Morgan, Bayne Froney, Britta Merwin and Craig Herrera break down the forecast and talk about past El Niño events similar to this one. 

Forecasts show this winter could see the strongest El Niño ever recorded, which could have significant impacts on different parts of the U.S. But will it look similar to the Super El Niños of history?

According to the FOX Forecast Center, there have been three winters since 1990, which were Super El Niño: 1991-92, 1997-98 and 2015-16.

SUPER EL NIÑO FUELED WINTER COULD UNLEASH MORE STORMS, DRIVING MORE ACTIVE PATTERN INTO THE SOUTH

While 1991-92 and 1997-98 followed very similar patterns, 2015-16 took an entirely different route, proving that just because the climate pattern is the same, doesn't mean it'll have the same effects.

Winter 1991-1992 

In 1991, El Niño kicked off with a blockbuster snowstorm, a Halloween blizzard that slammed Minnesota and Wisconsin, burying parts of the state under feet of snow.

The Regional Snowfall Index (RSI), which was launched in 1990, measured this storm as an "Extreme" storm, the highest category on the scale.

However, after that, nothing much happened.

Very few major storms followed that one, and the rest of winter saw warmer temperatures and rain.

Winter 1997-1998

A few years later, El Niño dominated again, and another major October snowstorm raged through the West.

That storm brought heavy snow from Colorado to Maine, but the rest of the major cities in the Northeast were lacking snow for the rest of the season. 

Just after the start of the New Year 1998, flooding rains and a major ice storm swept through the Midwest, Southeast and Northeast. 

According to NOAA, 16 inches of rain fell in a two-day period in Jackson County, North Carolina.

The ice storm, which impacted the Northeast and New England, left at least 44 dead in the U.S. and Canada. 

The flooding rains, which stretched from Tennessee to South Carolina, killed at least 12 people. 

Winter 2015-2016

This winter season proved to be very different from the El Niño's that preceded it, with the first major winter storm hitting in November.

December 2015 was exceptionally warm, but the Blizzard of January 2016 brought heavy snow to the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast.

Central Park received over two feet of snow from that storm alone, the FOX Forecast Center said.

Why were they different? 

All three winters featured active storm patterns in the Pacific, but whether they produced rain or snow depended on whether they had cold air available.

The FOX Forecast Center said early-season water temperatures could help explain some of the differences between these winters.

MULTIMILLION-DOLLAR CALIFORNIA OCEANFRONT HOMES BATTERED BY RELENTLESS PACIFIC WAVES AS COASTLINE CRUMBLES

In the 2015-2016 winter, the Pacific Ocean, Gulf of America and Atlantic were warmer than during the two previous Super El Niño winters.

"Warmer ocean waters can provide additional heat and moisture to developing storms, and alter the position of the jet stream influencing storm tracks," the FOX Forecast Center said. 

It's also possible the cooler coastal waters in the 1990s limited storm development along the coast.

What it means for this season

"Three winters are far too few to establish a reliable forecast, and every El Niño season develops differently," the FOX Forecast Center said. "Still, history suggests that a quiet October does not rule out a major early snowstorm, and one blockbuster storm does not guarantee a snowy winter."
