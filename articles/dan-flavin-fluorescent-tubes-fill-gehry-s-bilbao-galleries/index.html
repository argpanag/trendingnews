Dan Flavin Fluorescent Tubes Fill Gehry's Bilbao Galleries

TheTools · 18.11.2026 04:24 · source · permalink

2026-11-18

Bare fluorescent tubes appear in clusters of two or three, lean at odd angles, lock into lattices, and lie flat on the floor. In Frank Gehry‘s building, they make an unexpected pairing. A minimalist‘s stock-bought industrial parts sit beside an architect known for sweeping, sculptural forms. The American artist Dan Flavin’s exhibition Some Light, at the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao, uses dan flavin fluorescent tubes to test how much a room changes when only light fills it.

A self-taught artist who refused the label of sculpture

Flavin taught himself and disliked explaining his work. He did not call his pieces sculptures. He called them propositions or situations. His materials were standard dan flavin fluorescent tubes in lengths of two, four, six and eight feet, used in a small range of colors.

Lauren Hinkson, the Guggenheim‘s curator for collections in New York, says Flavin accepted tight limits, a discipline central to minimalist art. He worked with ten colors and the four tube lengths that manufacturers sold. He never changed the basic form of the tube.

Flavin found his medium in 1961, when he worked as a guard at the American Museum of Natural History in New York. Light became the subject of the rest of his career, and his vocabulary grew from a material most people walk past without noticing.

Light used as a shared, emotional experience rather than a private object has a long life beyond the gallery, as Thousands of lights honoring the Twin Towers shows over New York Harbor.

In his own account in Artforum, Flavin described a 1963 work. He fixed an unmodified tube to his workroom wall, and he called the result “a buoyant and relentless gaseous image.” The glow was so intense that the physical object nearly disappeared into the light it gave off.

Testing the eye across the galleries

The opening rooms hold early white-light works. Warm and cool tones play against each other, and the daylight that enters Bilbao‘s galleries shifts the effect through the day. Stand in front of a two-tube work in white and warm white, and the eye begins to adjust. The two tubes seem to merge and spill onto the floor.

The next rooms, titled Corners, Barriers and Corridors, turn from the object to the room. Flavin said that careful arrangement of lighting equipment could disrupt and reshape a room’s actual space. Colored grids and fence-like barriers build volumes of color that either dissolve or heighten the space around them.

Several works pay tribute to painters who worked with color. greens crossing greens (to Piet Mondrian who lacked green), 1966, is a standout. Its two sections, 122 centimeters and 61 centimeters high, form a post-and-lintel gateway that plays with Mondrian’s palette. An artificial barrier of blue, red and blue fluorescent light, made in 1968, was dedicated to Judd’s son, Flavin Starbuck Judd. Donald Judd kept a full-scale version in his Spring Street studio.

The white-light monuments come later in the sequence. They honor the Russian Constructivists and rise like skyscrapers or totems inside a large elliptical gallery. The “monument” for V. Tatlin XI, from 1964, stands 337.8 centimeters tall.

Keeping dan flavin fluorescent tubes working in a collection

Flavin’s certificates, drawn on graph paper, told owners how to install a work and how to replace its tubes. The documents are part of each work’s record, and they show how the pieces were meant to be built.

Most of Some Light comes from the Guggenheim’s own holdings, which include a large part of the Panza Collection. Starting in 2010, with support from the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation, the museum began a decade-long study of seven artists in the collection, Flavin among them. Hinkson says the project examined each work’s history, certificate and installations to answer questions about why floor pieces looked so different from one presentation to the next.

Conservators now use a spectrometer to record the wavelengths each lamp emits. An old lamp and a new one can be compared precisely, which helps the museum show the works as Flavin intended, according to Hinkson. The exhibition is sponsored by Iberdrola, the global electricity and clean energy company, which has been a long-term patron of the museum.

Flavin’s rooms also show how colour and light can reshape an entire space, an idea pushed further by Sabine Marcelis’s Zurich light and colour wonderland, with its towering lava lamps and luminous tunnels.

From the mailroom to the rotunda

Flavin once worked in the Guggenheim’s mailroom. He carried documents and plans between the emerging museum and Frank Lloyd Wright’s office. Hinkson notes that he carried plans between Wright and the museum’s director, James Johnson Sweeney. Years later, the same man would fill Wright’s rotunda with light.

In 1971, Flavin lit one full turn of the rotunda in a 21-artist exhibition. In 1992, he returned to fill the entire space. The source describes this only as Flavin returning to fill the entire rotunda in 1992.

At 59, four years before his death in 1996, Flavin married Tracy Harris inside the museum. The ceremony took place beneath a column of rose-pink light rising through the atrium.

Light without a god

Throughout his career, Flavin rejected religious symbolism and the spiritual meaning often attached to light. He put it plainly in 1967: “My fluorescent tubes never burn out desiring a god.” He also collected antiques and memorabilia, and he backed the Hudson River School painters at his homes in the Hudson Valley and on Long Island.

The dan flavin fluorescent tubes make no claim beyond their own presence. Some Light runs at the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao until 4 April 2027, and visitors who stay long enough will see the room change around them.
