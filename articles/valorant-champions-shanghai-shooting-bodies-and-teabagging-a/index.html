VALORANT Champions Shanghai: Shooting Bodies And Teabagging Are Banned

VALORANT Champions Shanghai is bringing a rule that’s been an unwritten norm for years into an actual written policy. Ahead of the tournament, Riot laid out its in-game conduct expectations in plain terms: for the duration of the event, shooting dead bodies or player pucks, and teabagging or other inappropriate in-game conduct, are both prohibited, with any breach subject to disciplinary action. It’s a short, blunt rule, but it’s addressing a problem that has followed VALORANT esports since its very first international events.

Champs Shanghai isn’t the first time Riot has had to step in on this exact issue. Back in 2021, ahead of a VCT EU match between G2 Esports and DfuseTeam, then G2 player Patryk “paTiTek” Fabrowski posted a message from admins telling the lobby not to teabag or shoot dead enemies, calling the behaviour undesired during the broadcast. The message didn’t sit well with everyone in the room. G2 founder Ocelote publicly hoped it was a joke and told his players to “teabag anyway” once they built a lead.

That same year, Sentinels’ Michael “dapr” Gulino said he received death threats after teabagging 100 Thieves’ Joshua “steel” Nissan in a match, an early sign of how quickly in-game gloating could spiral into something far uglier off it.

The conduct question got more serious in 2022, when Riot suspended two Game Changers community members, handing bans to two Valorant pros over an alleged teabagging incident that began as a Discord argument and spilled into targeted harassment on social media. It was a reminder that the issue was never really about the emote itself, but about what it could escalate into once it left the game client.

No stretch of VALORANT history captures the tension better than Champions 2023 in Los Angeles, where Evil Geniuses leaned all the way into the role of tournament villains. Max “Demon1” Mazanov became a lightning rod for teabagging and shooting the dead bodies of his opponents throughout the run, and when asked directly about the label, he responded that he honestly didn’t care what people said about him. That indifference didn’t sit well with everyone watching.

Fnatic’s Emir “Alfajer” Beder, also a vocal player who often stands up after great plays, called out Demon1’s conduct on stream, saying he’d been genuinely irritated by it, and promised payback if the two teams ever crossed paths. “Let’s play, I’m gonna teabag him after every time I kill him,” Alfajer said, turning what started as one player’s antics into a rivalry for the rest of the event.

If anyone needed proof this issue hasn’t gone away, Masters London provided it just months ago. In FUT Esports’ upper bracket quarterfinal against EDward Gaming, FUT took the first map 13-4 before EDG stormed back to win the series 2-1, and during the match, FUT’s Efe “s0pp” Tur shot the bodies of fallen opponents and shouted at EDG’s ZmjjKK on stage. A clip of the moment was misread by fans as a racial slur rather than the trash talk it actually was.

S0pp addressed it directly on social media afterwards, explaining that his line had simply been “kangkang, you can’t hold s***” and that he’d never intentionally disrespect any player, country, or language. It didn’t stop what came next. The backlash turned into online harassment and death threats directed at s0pp, including, disturbingly, posts mocking his late mother.

It’s the exact scenario Riot’s new rule seems designed to head off before it happens again on home soil in Shanghai.

S0pp isn’t the only case from this year. BBL Esports has built a reputation across 2026 for exactly this style of play, most visibly at the Esports World Cup, where fan reaction to their group stage win over 100 Thieves included direct callouts of their in-match habit of shooting bodies and trash-talking opponents. Whether it’s read as competitive fire or straightforward disrespect tends to depend entirely on who’s watching, which is precisely the problem a blanket rule is trying to solve.

What makes this issue so difficult for Riot to legislate cleanly is that the tolerance for this kind of conduct simply isn’t universal. In the Americas and EMEA, taunting has often been treated as part of the show. Ocelote begging his own players to teabag opponents, or caster and personality tarik openly wishing players got paid to talk trash, both reflect a regional culture where this kind of gloating reads as entertainment rather than disrespect. That same behavior lands very differently in other fanbases.

The s0pp incident is the clearest recent example: a piece of ordinary trash talk, misheard through a language and cultural gap, escalated into a firestorm specifically because it was interpreted as a targeted, personal insult rather than competitive banter. Community discussion around the incident repeatedly pointed out that fans in China, in particular, tend to treat both language slights and in-game disrespect like body-shooting and teabagging as far more personal than fans elsewhere typically do, a pattern that shows up across other esports scenes rooted in the same region too.

It’s also worth pointing out that VALORANT’s approach here is a choice, not an industry standard. Counter-Strike, the genre’s closest sibling, has never built anything close to this level of conduct policing into its official rulebook. Trash talk, body-shooting, and far rawer insults between players have been part of that scene’s identity for over two decades, treated as just another layer of competitive theatre rather than something the league needs to step in on.

VALORANT’s stricter approach perhaps isn’t really about the emotes being uniquely harmful, it’s about the audience Riot has built around the game. Perhaps Riot polices this specifically because VALORANT’s broadcast reaches a wider demographic tuning in than titles with a more hardened, no-holds-barred viewer base.

VALORANT Champions 2026 is the first since the s0pp incident put this exact issue back in the headlines, and it’s landing in a city and region where the last major flashpoint around body-shooting and provocation happened just months ago at a different event entirely, especially with a lot of backlash from the Chinese audience after FUT shot and teabagged EDG players as well as talked some smack, which was misinterpreted to be racist.

Codifying the rule in writing, rather than leaving it as an unofficial ask from admins the way it was back in 2021, gives Riot a clearer basis for disciplinary action if a repeat incident threatens to spiral the way s0pp’s did, especially with the possible cultural differences. Whether players actually change their habits under the new policy remains to be seen, but for an event hosted in the one region where this exact controversy has proven the most combustible this year, Riot clearly isn’t taking the chance.
