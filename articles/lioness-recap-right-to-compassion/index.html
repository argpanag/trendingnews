Lioness Recap: Right to Compassion

TheTools · 06.09.2026 21:04 · source · permalink

Special Ops: Lioness

Special Ops: Lioness

For its entire third season so far, Lioness has been caught in the mire of an ill-conceived bi-linear narrative structure, going back and forth in time between Joe’s abduction by Belarus contract agents and the home-turf mission that got her there. Other than accomplishing the mandate of holding the viewer’s attention week to week (if only as hostage), this late-stage How We Got Here gimmick has mostly just obscured the season’s finer moments — from above-par action scenes to those fucking amped performances of Sheridan’s broad, techno-Western-style character beats — in a sort of rhythmic discordance.

“Sugar Land” is another episode that feels like two first-halves of what would otherwise be solid chapters in a linear version of the season. What’s worse, it ends on another unearned cliffhanger that’s only sure to frustrate the viewer without offering up even a veneer of narrative justification. Thankfully, there’s enough story, thrills, and thematic meat on the bone throughout to make it the most entertaining episode of the season yet.

So much so that the first half feels like Lioness at its finest, deftly blending a little Clancy-esque espionage with an above-par TV car chase and ominous, unreliable narration/exposition brought to you by a murderer’s row of CIA-brained officials on the hill. Joe’s in a particularly good mood the morning they kick off their impromptu drain-the-swamp operation by exposing some of the high-profile politicians they’ve discovered are paid Russian intelligence assets. She finds her daughters at the sterile Camp Peary breakfast nook and tells them they’ll be going home very soon, and thanks them for being “such troopers.” Kate seems weary of the verbiage there, in the midst of confronting the stormtrooping realities of not only her mother’s job, but her worldview for the first time. She asks Joe if she ever thinks about the men who came to their house, a veiled way of asking whether she ever feels remorse for the lives she’s taken in the field. “Not for a second. They don’t deserve your fear, and they forfeited your right to compassion. The only thing they deserve is to be forgotten,” Joe responds, only confirming, maybe even inflaming Kate’s growing fear of the cold-blooded killer whose world she’ll inherit.

And boy does Joe bring the hammer down cold and hard on the hapless Senator Ryan Olson (Joe Nemmers) over breakfast at the Old Ebbitt Grill — or, at least, that’s the only real-life Washington, D.C. saloon where you can get Eggs Chesapeake, as Secretary Mullins notes. But the gang’s really all lickin’ their chops for Olson’s ass, which they waste no time chewing to ribbons as soon as he gives them an opening. The room dynamic of Mullins, Byron, and Kaitlyn all sitting around the table, silently sicking the vengeful Joe on this guy is nothing short of delicious, and the all-star performance of dressing down a corrupt senator with the strong arm of the law will prove enjoyable, if not fleeting wish fulfillment for folks on virtually any side of the aisle. Byron lays out Olson’s options (or lack thereof) in plain, punishing English: either resign in disgrace and seek no future endeavors in public or private life, or be arrested and prosecuted to the fullest extent of the law. As Mullins, Kaitlyn, and Byron leave the table, Joe stays behind to offer option 3, the final blow: “You could kill yourself. That gets my vote.”

This is effectively where Joe signs her fate away in the same stroke as this guy’s death warrant. Driven wholly by vengeance, she incepts a violent path forward for her prey and justifies it with the idea that an enemy taking themselves out of the equation is a closed loop of violence. But the violence is not contained as Senator Olson ends a simple but incredibly effective car chase by driving into an oncoming semi-truck, injuring the truck driver to the point of critical condition. Olson’s family is likely to get the news of his sudden, violent, inexplicable death from the news at the same time as Mullins and crew are getting the same feed in the debriefing room, already talking about next steps in the aftermath of this now-very public political scandal. It’s almost funny how quickly they jump to the age-old strategy of blaming the previous administration, arguing that this scandal is merely the endpoint of a corrupted national security climate they had no choice but to inherit. The political backlash (not to mention the collateral damage in lives lost and ruined) that had this group on high alert just hours ago already seems like a routine item to be checked off an office cleanup list. On a narrative level, the whole mad scene seems to mirror the ominous final moments of the previous two Lioness missions. Targets neutralized, threats eliminated, and the nagging feeling that “all we changed was oil prices,” as Cruz puts it at the end of season one. America’s illusion of security under the wing of the military-industrial complex may be reinforced, but the true scales of justice remain off balance and unaccounted for.

But there’s a second part to this mission, and it picks up right where the previous season’s finale cut to black. Joe returns home (or, in this case, the intermittent Camp Peary) to her family, bringing word that another nightmarish chapter in the McNamara family saga is coming to an end. Only she seems more excited to announce that the bad guys are “getting what they deserve” than the news that they’ll actually be able to move back into their house. And here’s where all the flash-forward business this season finally comes into thematic focus, imbuing the scene with an Old-Testament style foreboding. For the crime of vindictive, self-satisfying violence in the name of protecting her family, the narrative gods of Lioness will take it all away from Joe, from a mission accomplished to a family life secured.

Cut to six days after Joe’s abduction, and the Lioness crew is hot on the heels of her captors. Cheap cliffhanger ending aside, the rest of the episode proves an emotionally satisfying kickoff for the final stretch of the season, laying Joe’s only shot at redemption, both mortally and spiritually, at the feet of her team. It starts in Tulsa, Oklahoma, where Kyle interrupts Oleksa’s quiet life in witness protection for help in tracking down Joe’s abductors and identifying the masterminds of the operation. The way she figures it, the abductors on the ground are Belarusian contract agents, and Iran seems most likely in the position to pay them to do it. They’re also the most likely nation to have motive since Joe’s operations have mostly been in the Middle Eastern theater. Belarusian contract agents would also have a network of safe houses on U.S. soil from which to change locations thoroughly, as well as access to FBOs to get Joe on a plane and out of U.S. airspace.

With that, Kyle gives Oleksa a phone and instructs her to Signal chat all the info on Belarus safehouse locations, and the hunt is on. Mad props to Dawn Olivieri as Amber Wahlen for once again bringing a steely charm and understated snark to the thankless task of delivering the mission exposition to a wily Lioness crew as they suit up, talk shit, and pop amphetamines to stay amped for the next 72 hours. Bottom line: they’ve got a mess of potential safehouses scattered throughout a series of residential areas in Texas, and they’ve gotta hope they can track Joe and her abductors down to either one of those houses or en route to a nearby FBO before they get her in the air. And the window is so narrow that they may already be too late. Meanwhile, Kyle and his usual pair of special-forces thugs track down a suspiciously large plane (as in, big enough to fly across the Atlantic) and an equally suspicious flight manifest. The resulting shootout is a fun bit of Sheridan-esque modern-Western construction that brings an efficient quick hit of adrenaline in a third act that’s more about the emotional buildup than anything else, for the audience and Lioness recon team alike.

Cruz and Josie, both as a burgeoning couple and as separate narrative focal points of the previous seasons, have been on the back burner through most of season three, so it comes as a surprise when we hone in on Cruz’s severe longing to rescue Joe as the crew waits for the word to lift off in Josie’s helicopter. “Got to give me a chance, God,” she prays, repeatedly, like she’s the only one on the team who understands the Old Testament forces under which they carry out their special ops. Cruz’s relationship with Joe has been that of a reluctant mentee to an unreliable mentor, inherently tempestuous because Cruz maintains a healthy distaste, if only in spirit, for the lies they help construct and the ethical quandaries in which they repeatedly find themselves embroiled. But their shared history as soldiers in the field, however personally fraught, evokes in Cruz an unexpected feeling of responsibility to save the leader who gave shape to the lives of this damned band of misfits.

Unfortunately, Joe’s actual moment of rescue, whether successful or not, will have to wait until next week. In the meantime, the Iranian benefactor of her abduction just rolled in on the safehouse where she’s being kept to make some graphic threats to her life and physical well-being and even remove a pinky toe when she talks back. Between the severity of the threats in a put-upon accent and Joe’s clapback making fun of his “foreign-sounding” name (”Sounds like the word my daughter made up when she took her first shit in the toilet!”), it all feels like a way to artificially raise the stakes right before we cut to black while slipping in a little easy caricaturing of “the enemy” on racial grounds. Still, the stage is set for a thrilling rescue mission next week, with an entire U.S. intelligence apparatus waiting in the wings to obscure the consequences.
