Sam Altman says the chip supply chain's invisible hand is failing — and DRAM is why — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 29.10.2026 13:08 · source · permalink

The memory business has never looked like this — and it still isn't building fast enough

For most of its history, the memory chip industry has been a value destroyer. A DRAM producer would put $20 billion of capital expenditure into new fabs to generate $19 billion of revenue, riding boom-and-bust cycles that nearly wiped out the survivors more than once. That era, according to OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman, is over in the most dramatic way imaginable.

"Until right now, I don't think making memory has ever been a great business over a long period of time," Altman said, speaking on the Cheeky Pint podcast. "And then all of a sudden, these guys are each going to make like $100 billion of free cash flow in a year."

The obvious response to a windfall like that is to build. The obvious response is not what is happening. Memory makers remember how many times they nearly went bankrupt building too many fabs on the promise that this time was different, so they are staying conservative while prices scream. Price signals are supposed to summon supply. Altman says they aren't.

"I think the invisible hand is failing right now," he said. "I mean, the invisible hand... It has a checkered track record at best. But, I think it's not quite working here."

That is the single most important sentence in the conversation for anyone trying to work out what limits AI growth over the next few years. If the chip supply chain were responding normally to record cash flow, compute would eventually get cheap and abundant. If it is not responding, then the scarcity that Altman spends the rest of the interview working around becomes a structural feature of the industry — one that shapes where AI companies build, how they spend, and which parts of the stack earn outsized returns.

Two disclosures this fall show both halves of that argument at once. The windfall is real: SK hynix reported ₩104.6 trillion of third-quarter revenue and ₩83.3 trillion of operating profit — an 80% operating margin, or roughly $60 billion of profit in three months — and told investors it would return about ₩100 trillion to shareholders this year. Prices are still rising, though the pace has slowed sharply: TrendForce expects conventional DRAM contract prices to be up 10–15% quarter-on-quarter in the fourth quarter, after 58–63% in the second.

The response is real too, just late. On August 7, SK hynix's board approved ₩54.3 trillion, about $38 billion, of investment through 2031 in two new plants: ₩35.2 trillion for the Y2 fab in Yongin, which will make high-bandwidth memory and next-generation DRAM, and ₩19.1 trillion for the M17 NAND fab in Cheongju. The first cleanrooms are not due to open until December 2028 at M17 and June 2029 at Y2 — and a cleanroom opening is not the same as output. Micron's ¥1.5 trillion expansion of its Hiroshima fab, which broke ground in July with up to ¥536 billion of support from Japan's industry ministry, does not begin installing equipment until the second half of 2028. SK hynix says it expects demand to exceed supply through 2030 and has already signed long-term agreements covering more than half of its 2027–2028 capacity.

That is the precise sense in which the invisible hand is failing. Money is reaching the industry — it is going into fabs, and into the machine tools that fill them. It is not reaching the market in time. A supply chain with multi-year lead times, and with boards that remember the last bust, cannot respond on the schedule the AI buildout requires.

The capex reaches the toolmakers first. Tokyo Electron, Japan's largest wafer-fab equipment maker, took 31% of its fiscal 2026 equipment sales from DRAM customers, and SEMI, the industry association, expects global semiconductor equipment sales to reach a record $165.9 billion in 2026, up 23.2%, with Korea's spending driven by advanced memory including high-bandwidth memory. Those orders are the leading indicator; the wafers they will process are the lagging one.

Why your laptop is a bad place to run AI

The shortage has an uncomfortable corollary for anyone who likes the idea of open-source models running on their own machine. It sounds efficient: no cloud bill, no data leaving the house, no meters. Altman's view is the opposite.

"From a global allocation perspective, actually, it's quite terrible to have everybody have a bunch of the world's limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer," he said. "And, being used 5% of the time."

Five percent. That is his estimate of how much of the time the memory in a personal computer is actually doing work. In a world where a constrained supply of memory is the binding constraint on how much AI the economy can run, parking a slice of it on desks and letting it idle is close to the worst allocation possible. The cloud, where hardware can be packed to maximum utilization, is where scarce chips belong.

This puts Altman at odds with Apple, which has been marketing Mac mini and Mac Studio desktops on exactly the opposite pitch — buy the hardware once and run models locally "without counting tokens or worrying about rising cloud costs." It also complicates the political and consumer appeal of local inference, which trades global efficiency for individual control. Altman is not arguing that local models are useless. He is arguing that in a world where memory is the choke point, they are a luxury the aggregate system pays for.

Where is high pricing actually summoning supply, then? Not in memory so much as in the physical layer underneath data centers. Altman names turbines, batteries, concrete, steel, components for industrial robots — "not the humanoid fake demos" — and new actuator makers. The buildout's bottlenecks are less about labor and more about permitting, components, and the willingness of industries with long memories to bet on a new boom.

$70 billion per gigawatt: the number that decides how much AI the world gets

Altman's cost target is the clearest window into how OpenAI thinks about the future price of intelligence. At roughly $70 billion per gigawatt in five-year total cost of ownership, he says, "if that number were 20 instead of 70, we should be much happier," while still demanding 10x better performance per year from smarter models.

Getting from $70 billion to $20 billion requires two things: cheaper energy and compressing "all of the gross margin stacking that happens between melting sand... and getting the token back." Asked whether simply eliminating profits along the chain could close the gap, Altman is skeptical: with free materials and no profit, "maybe you could get there in the current energy envelope. But only just."

That number needs two caveats the conversation does not supply. It is a five-year total-cost-of-ownership figure, not a construction bill: the up-front capex is a fraction of it. And it is one company's planning assumption, not an industry consensus. Epoch AI's bottom-up model of a 1 GW AI data center puts up-front capex at $38 billion and operating cost at $0.9 billion a year, or $8.5 billion a year once capital is annualized across asset lifespans — about $42.5 billion over five years on its assumptions, well below Altman's $70 billion. The gap is mostly about what is assumed for servers, utilization and the cost of capital, but it changes the arithmetic of whether a gigawatt pays: at $70 billion the annualized bill is about $14 billion; at Epoch's number it is $8.5 billion.

The $70 billion figure matters because it is the denominator in every argument about whether AI's buildout is investable. Hyperscalers are on track to spend trillions on data centers through 2030 while running negative free cash flow, and a large share of that is the cost of turning electricity and silicon into tokens. If better models can deliver 10x improvement per year in converting dollars or flops into useful output, the effective price of intelligence falls even if the physical plant gets more expensive. Altman says OpenAI expects exactly that kind of annual gain.

The other lever he is pulling is vertical integration. OpenAI is building its own chip, learning to build its own data centers, and running a robotics program aimed at constructing data centers. He flags an underdiscussed idea — "self-replicating data centers," using AI to control robots that run fabs and build turbines, solar panels, and data centers — and says it is "within grasp."

Designing a chip is a great AI problem; lithography is not

One of the cleanest distinctions Altman draws separates two problems people often lump together. He is asked how much of OpenAI's custom chip comes down to a shortage of public knowledge — there are, after all, few blog posts on building a 2-nanometer chip.

His answer splits design from manufacturing. Lithography is effectively one company on Earth, and that is not something AI can dissolve. But producing the design file you send to tape-out "is well understood. That is a great problem for AI." The hard constraint is not the intelligence required to lay out a chip; it is the machinery required to print it.

He applies the same logic to procurement, which he cites as another area where OpenAI staff are willing to point AI at work they have done the old-fashioned way for a long time. This is the cultural claim underneath OpenAI's strategy: as silicon capacity stays constrained, the durable advantage goes to the company most willing to apply AI to every domain in which the industry has always done things the slow way.

AI-driven AI research works at the 5% level — the transformer-level leap is still missing

This is the thread that determines whether any of the cost math eventually breaks in the customer's favor. Altman is careful to separate what today's models can do from what they cannot.

OpenAI knows how to ask a model to "find me a 5% compute efficiency win," he says — and jokes that his host could be "an incredibly successful AI researcher now." What nobody knows how to do yet is say: "Go discover a new architecture that is as big of a single leap forward as the transformer was." He adds plainly: "I think that's where things heading."

The missing ingredient is models that are better at high-level planning and conceptual reasoning. His bet is that the same pipeline OpenAI used to make its models excel at math will work when pointed at AI research — with a critical caveat. He predicts that for a while, possibly a long while, the industry will have to agree on limits to how smart individual single models are allowed to get. Some progress will be constrained by choice rather than by capability. That is a strange thing for a company at the frontier to say, and it is the hinge on which his safety argument and his product roadmap both swing.

Many slightly superhuman AIs, not one trillion-IQ model

The concern about single models being too powerful connects directly to the idea that gives the episode its name. Altman's preference is unambiguous: "I would much rather intuitively have a trillion AIs of slightly superhuman intelligence join society than one AI of 1 trillion IQ join society."

The argument is about robustness through plurality. Human society, for all its deep flaws, is "unbelievably robust" because many people check each other through pluralism and democracy. Altman had been sad that AI seemed to be heading away from that model, and says he is now excited about agent teams as a direction — though he stresses it is something to push for, not something that arrives automatically.

He rejects the word "swarm" for groups of models — "Swarm is such a— I know, it's locusts. Yeah. Not good. Not good branding" — and settles on "agent teams," which he says likely have "very interesting scaling law properties, but also variance in alignment properties."

More vulnerabilities are coming, and patching is not the fix

The interview opens on a security episode — a PHP vulnerability that took far too long to surface — and Altman's answer explains why he thinks the industry is in for a rough stretch. The episode is PHP2Shell: two flaws in the PHP core, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, that allow unauthenticated remote code execution, with a CVSS score of 9.2. They were found with the help of AI in August 2026, disclosed on October 20 and patched the next day; the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency added both to its catalog of vulnerabilities known to be exploited on October 26. At disclosure, public reporting put the share of websites running PHP at nearly 70%. Models have been getting better at finding vulnerabilities for a while, but people had not pointed them at the whole internet. "There's a lot of surface area and a lot of code on the internet," he said. As people start scanning it, "we are in a world of many more serious vulnerabilities than we expect" — even relative to people who already expected a lot.

The durable response, he agrees, is not patching code but re-architecting systems, and he argues that is now feasible because "we have this amazing new tool to do it with." A new generation of startups is talking about building a new kind of internet and application architecture for a world where models keep getting smarter — rethinking "what it means to be a router, or an operating system, or an application." Two years ago that would have seemed insane: "There's no way. There's too much surface. You can't write all that. And now maybe soon you can."

He declines to declare victory on defense. "I don't want to declare victory at all on the PHP thing, because I still don't think we know. And I assume some bad things are going to happen." The first day of the episode went better than his worst fears, with companies using models to build patches and cyber defenses and some taking applications offline briefly to scan and redeploy. But prevented exploits do not get headlines, and cyber experts have told him they are no longer confident the right assumptions are baked into threat models — because if AI discovers deep novel hardware bugs requiring physics nobody understood, an assumption may simply fail.

The gap between the best AI users and everyone else keeps widening

One of the most underappreciated dynamics in the conversation is internal to the industry. Advanced math and advanced hacking both use agent teams, while ordinary corporate work does not — despite the capability being months old. The difference is "how much compute can we productively throw at a single problem." A few years ago, spending more than a dollar of compute on a problem did not help at all. Chain-of-thought and test-time compute changed that, and only then did agent teams become useful: "you can't have a useful swarm with a bunch of GPT-3s."

The consequence is that the delta between what most people use AI for and what the most productive users do "has just continued to spread, and spread, and spread." Inside OpenAI, capital allocation is largely compute allocation. Researchers get wide latitude with a skeptical default rather than hard caps, and "we try never to reward people for using a lot of compute" — no token leaderboards, and ranking high on one is "actually slightly bad" at promotion time. That changes as costs scale: a researcher spending $10 million on interesting math is fine, $100 million is "a little pricey, but cool math," and at $1 billion "I really need you to cool the jets with the math."

He defends letting go during a new paradigm. When OpenAI ran the Navier-Stokes proof, nobody understood the scaling laws of agent teams — how many copies, how long the rollouts, which model. "We did let it get out of control for one week on the math. Intentionally so." He estimates the work cost on the order of $10 million per proof, though total math spending ran into the hundreds of millions and produced more than the 700 published proofs. He predicts the same proof could be redone at 1/100th the cost a year later. OpenAI published the proof on September 8, 2026 together with a formalization in Lean, the proof assistant that checks each step mechanically — which is what made the claim assessable rather than merely asserted.

That number — $10 million for a fundamental proof, falling 100x in a year — is the most concrete illustration of what "cheap compute" would actually buy. It also sets up the uncomfortable question Altman does not fully resolve: if OpenAI publishes the results, what happens to the mathematicians whose careers were built on discovering them?

He takes the tension seriously without conceding the substance. "Humanity deserves fundamental knowledge. And we should not be sitting on a major fundamental science discovery." But if OpenAI does this for math and then other fields, "we more than owe it to the people that work in those fields" to find a solution space where the world benefits and those communities get the best version of their future. He describes the internal moment when a model in a channel named "Model Name-Hold My Beer" was thrown at researchers' hardest problems and "just knocking down one after the other." He stayed up most of two nights out of excitement — and felt "much more sadness and melancholy, and a feeling of something ending than I was expecting." He compares it to his father watching horses still used productively in Irish industry in the 1950s, at the tail end of a technology humans had used for thousands of years.

Personal agents are a few ideas away, and web forms are the tell

Altman concedes that no one has built the personal agent people cannot imagine living without, the way they talk about coding agents or ChatGPT. The ingredients are "intelligence, context, tools." The winning product will look a lot like current Dots, Muse, or Grokbot, "but it'll just work. It will just work. It will be reliable." He calls it "a small number of ideas away from a super mainstream thing" and says the Macintosh moment for personal agents has not happened.

The commercial side of this is where the argument gets interesting for investors. Companies resist agentic access, but cannot fully control it, because an agent can drive a computer directly. Altman's instinct is that the world converges on "you're allowed to tell an AI to use your computer on your behalf, including software," though he holds that with low confidence.

His host's sharper observation is about commerce: every company wants an AI tab or chat widget, and consumers do not want it. They want to tell their primary chat app to "go find my recent order on this business, and refund it." Businesses resist because they fear losing control and their moat, but the moat came from brand and product selection, not from HTML. Altman agrees directionally and personalizes it: given a choice to buy through ChatGPT from Amazon versus an unknown vendor, "10 out of 10 times I click Amazon. Pay a higher price" — and that has nothing to do with clicking around amazon.com. He thinks Amazon is well placed if commerce becomes agentic because of its fulfillment network, and concedes OpenAI has not done enough to offer partners a platform inside ChatGPT.

The host's broader thesis is worth repeating: any web form a human fills out is "below your pay grade," because you are almost never inventing that information for the first time. Altman agrees: "I would be so happy to never do fake work at a computer again." He was an early, clumsy adopter of autofill plugins for exactly this reason.

Benchmarks are a poor proxy, tokens are a fake unit, and pricing is a mess

Altman says OpenAI tries not to benchmaxx, and believes it is better at that than in its history, though "I keep thinking that benchmaxxing is over as a phenomenon, and people keep doing it." Two things really matter, he says: scientific discovery, and being enjoyable to work with — "I don't think we have great benchmarks for being enjoyable to work with." On model personality, he says the industry passed through a glazing era and then a stroppy, ornery anti-glazing era, and that current models are "much better than we've had in a long time." He cites Emmett Shear's description of the ideal as "a really thoughtful and challenging, but still respectful, senior coworker" and says he has not heard a better encapsulation.

On pricing, he admits OpenAI almost made "a very bad call" to stop pricing in tokens and instead quote a price per prompt like AWS. Customer response was strongly negative: people want to know what they are paying for, and when asked whether they would rather pay more for the same output with inflated token counts, they preferred the inflated tokens "because I know there's a real thing there." He dislikes the analogy to chip nanometer labels, which he calls "very silly" and unrelated to actual nanometers: "I would rather not make up a fake unit." Tokens, he concedes, are "real in some sense, but they're not a good unit" from a business-value standpoint. He expects a floor of information-theoretically useful tokens on both sides, and calls the current need to weigh 10 cents per million tokens from a small model against $10 per token from a big one, plus chain-of-thought length, "cognitive overhead that I don't think actually adds any value."

On enterprise budgets, his host notes tokens are roughly 1% of Stripe's opex and expects that to rise to 5%, 10%, 20%, at which point allocation becomes a real question. Altman's steady-state guess is tens of percentage points of opex for many companies, but today "there are more companies with the problem of not using enough AI rather than using too much AI." He invokes a Paul Graham-style observation: companies let people save $17 on snacks while holding meetings that cost enormous sums and add no value, and AI spend is not where he would focus optimization first.

On his colleague Greg Brockman, Altman is unreserved: "one of a kind in the best sense. He's singular on the Greg Brockman scale" — a combination of intensity, monster productivity, extreme dedication, and focus on the mission.

The through-line across all of it is Altman's preference for distributing capability over concentrating it — many agents over one, many companies over one planner, many scientists sharing credit over one lab sitting on results. The unresolved tension is that the same distribution widens the productivity gap he describes, and the supply chain he wants to respond to high prices is, by his own account, not responding. For investors, three things are worth watching: whether memory capex finally breaks its historical caution, whether OpenAI ships a personal agent that "just works," and whether the field actually agrees to limits on single-model intelligence — the one lever Altman says would slow progress by choice rather than by capability. The first is the one that shows up in earnings first.
