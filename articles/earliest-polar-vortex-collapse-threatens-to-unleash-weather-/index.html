Earliest polar vortex collapse threatens to unleash weather chaos

TheTools · 07.11.2026 09:52 · source · permalink

Winds circulating above North Pole are splintering into two, pushing Arctic air towards Europe

Winds swirl above the northernmost tip of our planet.

As in every year before, the westerlies whip biting air around the North Pole, shielding the northern hemisphere from its Arctic extremes – but now the vortex is on the brink of collapse.

Winds are shifting, starting to spin in the other direction, threatening to upturn the weather system and unleash chaos thousands of miles to the south.

The “unprecedented” breakdown could plunge Britain into a cold spell, blanketing the country with snow and exhausting Europe’s gas supplies. 

A record El Niño has added to uncertainty, with Britons being warned to start preparing emergency winter boxes.

“As a weather fan, this is a big event because it’s so unprecedented,” Judah Cohen, a climatologist at Massachusetts Institute of Technology, told Scientific American magazine.

“It’s a lot of excitement. Everybody’s hair is on fire in the climate or meteorologist community.”

Jim Dale, a meteorologist and the founder of British Weather Services, told The Telegraph: “It’s not just a dangerous time politically and economically, but also meteorologically.

“People need to be in a position to withstand any situation so that they can come out of an extreme weather event with a smile not a grimace.

“Most people think it is never going to happen to them, but we need to be prepared for whatever Mother Nature throws at us.”

The polar vortex typically begins forming in the late summer and reaches peak intensity during the coldest months of December and January.

This collapse would be the earliest breakdown ever recorded. The previous record was set on Nov 28, 1969.

Jeff Knight, the lead climate scientist from the Met Office, told the BBC: “It would appear to be the earliest on record looking back over the 75 years we’ve been observing, with most experts agreeing this is a very unusual event.”

What is the polar vortex?

A polar vortex develops every year when temperatures fall in the Arctic in autumn.

However, changes in its behaviour can affect the weather in the northern hemisphere, sending colder air and snow and changes to jet streams.

The vortex is a ring of westerly winds, around 10 to 30 miles above the Earth’s surface, encircling the North Pole. It locks in cold air and encourages a strong jet stream, sending warm air over Europe. 

Atmospheric disturbances can weaken the vortex, eventually leading to its collapse.

Forecasters have recorded a significant wind weakening in the current vortex, with some predicting a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) event by mid-November, where the westerly winds that create the vortex quickly reverse into easterly winds.

“The big question is where it goes – the current favourite is Norway, Sweden, Denmark and Germany, but we cannot say for sure,” said Mr Dale. “It’s currently just guesswork where the kink in the polar vortex is going to land.”

Andrew Charlton-Perez, a professor of meteorology at the University of Reading, told The Telegraph: “The atmosphere is a chaotic system, so we don’t know exactly what will happen.

“When we had the Beast from the East in 2018, the cold air from the continent blew over and got stuck over the UK. However, the air over Europe isn’t that cold yet – so it won’t be as bad as that.”

Mr Charlton-Perez said that if the collapse was “reasonably large”, it would have an influence on the weather for 30 to 40 days. 

It could be potentially “debilitating, dangerous and hazardous”, Mr Dale said.

He praised the UK Government for urging people to stockpile, warning that extreme weather could cause electricity, water or gas to become inaccessible.

El Niño influence

The reason why this event is happening so early is not clear, but it could be caused by the current record-breaking El Niño.

Aidan McGivern, a Met Office meteorologist, said: “El Niño is basically enhancing the waves that go on in the troposphere, the wriggly jet stream.

“Those waves can then propagate upwards into the stratosphere, knocking, bumping and disturbing that circle of winds around the polar stratosphere, causing it to be displaced, pushed off the pole, deformed or even split.

“We know that El Niños increase the chances, through this chain of events, of SSWs happening, and we know SSWs increase the chance of colder weather for the UK. So we’ve got this chain of events that we can start to flag well in advance.”

Long-term autumn and winter forecasts focused on the influence of El Niño, suggesting a stormier start to winter followed by a colder and drier January.

However, because of the unprecedented timing of the SSW event, it is difficult to predict when the cold spell will hit or whether Britain will see November snowfall.

This year’s El Niño is predicted to be the strongest on record, with heavy flooding, severe droughts and record temperatures forecast.

Dame Angela Eagle, the Environment Secretary, said in September that the El Niño would lead to wetter winters and more extreme storms in the UK, which experienced one of the driest summers on record this year.

However, with it combined with a polar vortex collapse, many are stumped as to what will happen.

The lack of rain that a SSW event brings is particularly concerning as much of Europe is still battling with the effects of the summer drought. Countries were relying on a wet start to winter to refill reservoirs and provide water supplies.

Mr Charlton-Perez said that the UK needed 120 per cent of average rainfall this winter to be out of drought by next summer.

“Water resources are really stretched and a lot of planning was relying on having an above average winter to refill reservoirs and provide much-needed water supplies,” he said.

Energy security

The change in weather could have a significant effect on energy security, with heating demand soaring. It could wipe out any natural gas storage gains made during October’s mild weather.

The EU is heading into winter with gas storage about 72.4 per cent full – the lowest for this time of year since records began in 2011.

A cold snap like the one in January would drain gas storage reserves faster, the Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis warned.

It said an increased reliance on gas storage had become a major financial liability for Europe because of the US-Iran war pushing up prices.

There have already been warnings that Europe should brace for lower temperatures. A colder-than-usual patch of water just south of Greenland, nicknamed the “cold blob”, is worrying scientists.

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, a vast system of swirling currents that carries warm waters north, is starting to weaken, experts fear.

A severe slowdown or collapse would disrupt Europe’s agriculture, infrastructure, welfare and economy. The continent could face glacial winters and dry summers, surging sea levels and ferocious windstorms.

Britain is spending millions on an early-warning system, while the EU has added it to a list of national security concerns.

Even if a full Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation collapse unfolded across many decades, probably beyond the end of this century, parts of the system could start failing much sooner.

Consequences would be felt well before the currents ground to a halt.
