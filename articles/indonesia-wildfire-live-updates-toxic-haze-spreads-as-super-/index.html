  2. Indonesia to pursue legal action against wildfire offenderspublished at 12:40 BST 3 September
    12:40 BST 3 September

    Astudestra Ajengrastri
    BBC News Indonesia

    Alongside firefighting efforts, Indonesian authorities are intensifying legal action against individuals and companies linked to forest and land fires, according to the director general of Forestry Law Enforcement at Indonesia's Ministry of Forestry.

    Officials say penalties can range from administrative sanctions, such as mandatory ecosystem restoration, permit suspensions and permit revocations, to civil lawsuits and criminal prosecutions, depending on the severity of the violations.

    Since April, authorities have issued early-warning letters to 20 legal entities whose concession areas showed wildfire hotspots.

    The government has also conducted inspections of 32 forestry permit holders as part of prevention efforts.

    They have said administrative sanctions remain the primary enforcement tool, but warned that repeat fires in concession areas could trigger a combination of administrative, civil and criminal measures.

  5. Wildfires drive sharp rise in Indonesia's carbon emissionspublished at 12:29 BST 3 September
    12:29 BST 3 September

    By Barbara Metzler

    BBC Verify has been analysing satellite data from the satellite-based Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) which shows unusually high carbon emissions from wildfires in Indonesia this year.

    So far in 2026, fires have produced an estimated 58 million tonnes of carbon, which is almost double the annual double the average of 32 million tonnes seen by this point in the year between 2003 and 2025.

    While agricultural burning is common in Indonesia, CAMS data shows emissions from fires this year are significantly above average.

    Over the past week, the highest emissions were recorded across Central, West and South Kalimantan in Borneo, as well as South Sumatra.

    Smoke from the fires can contain fine particles and other pollutants, which can worsen air quality across affected areas.

    Data from yesterday shows high carbon emissions across parts of Kalimantan in Borneo and South Sumatra, while the highest levels of smoke or ash pollution are concentrated over Kalimantan and extend across the surrounding region.

  6. Indonesia tests underground cooling system to fight peat firespublished at 12:23 BST 3 September
    12:23 BST 3 September

    Astudestra Ajengrastri
    BBC News Indonesia

    Authorities in South Kalimantan are testing a new firefighting technique designed to tackle one of the biggest challenges of peatland fires: heat that continues smoldering underground long after surface flames appear to be extinguished.

    The method, known as undercooling, uses pipes driven up to two meters into peat soil to inject water and a specially formulated firefighting liquid directly into underground hotspots.

    Thermal drones are then used to detect hidden heat and monitor results.

    "We don't just need to know if the fire on the surface is out. We also need to know whether heat remains underground,” police spokesperson Trunoyudo Wisnu Andiko told reporters. “The undercooling system allows us to cool the source of the heat from below.”

    Police said a recent trial in Pengayuan, near Banjarbaru, reduced underground temperatures from around 120°C to 30°C, with hotspots no longer visible in follow-up thermal imaging.

  10. How the Indonesian fires are linked to the Iran warpublished at 11:50 BST 3 September
    11:50 BST 3 September

    Tessa Wong
    Asia Digital Reporter, in Singapore

    While the exceptionally dry conditions caused by the "super El Niño" are expected to exacerbate the fires in Indonesia, there are also deeper, more structural reasons why it may be particularly bad this year.

    The think tank Singapore Institute for International Affairs (SIIA) has been tracking and analysing the haze situation for years. In its report released in June, external, it pointed out that recent events such as the closure of the Strait of Hormuz have disrupted global energy supplies.

    This has led to a higher demand for biofuels, particularly from Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand within South East Asia.

    All this amounts to more pressure on plantation owners, especially small and medium-sized companies, to save costs and expand plantations - by setting fire to their land and waste.

    "Fertiliser and energy price spikes caused by the Strait of Hormuz closure are likely to persist for months even after seaborne trade resumes, adding pressure on agricultural growers and increasing the risk that some may use fire for land clearing or waste disposal as a cost-saving measure," the report noted.

    "Growers will need to meet demands for both food and fuel, increasing the risk that some might resort to expansion using unsustainable methods."

  15. What to know about the wildfirespublished at 11:00 BST 3 September
    11:00 BST 3 September

    Liza Tambunan
    Jakarta Correspondent

    Where exactly are the fires?

    In Sumatra and Kalimantan, with the majority of them spread across West and Central Kalimantan provinces.

    How long have they been burning for?

    They have been burning for weeks, with authorities recording a massive surge of hotspot and active fires in August.

    How much land has been destroyed by the fires?

    According to data from the Forestry Ministry, 107,465 hectares burned between January and July this year. This is a 110% increase from the same period in 2023, when El Nino last hit Indonesia.

    Separately, the National Disaster Mitigation Agency (BNPB) estimates that the fires have burned 73,310 hectares of land.

    Do we know how many fires are burning in the region?

    The BNPB has designated six priority provinces and noted that there are 182 active fires across the two islands as of today, with over 145 of those fire spots in Kalimantan.

    How many fire fighters have been deployed?

    The BNPB says more than 39,000 personnel have been deployed to take on the land and forest fires.

  17. What are peatlands?published at 10:17 BST 3 September
    10:17 BST 3 September

    Gavin Butler
    Reporting from Singapore

    As we have reported, there are fears the fires in Indonesia could activate millennia-old "carbon bombs", as burning peatlands threaten to release huge amounts of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

    Peatlands are waterlogged ecosystems which accumulate large amounts of semi-decomposed vegetation. They have a high carbon content, and can support forest and vegetation growth.

    But if they become dry, as we have seen in these recent fires, they can also become highly flammable.

    The area that's burning in Indonesia is a major peatland. It is considered globally important due to the amount of carbon that it stores and could potentially release - either as carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, or carbon monoxide, a toxic gas to humans.

    Susan Page, a professor of geology and environment at the University of Leicester, told the BBC that as soon as peatlands are drained, "we convert long-term carbon sinks into a short-term carbon source".

    "That means that they then start to emit carbon into the atmosphere, carbon that maybe was accumulating thousands of years ago."

  19. Smoke, heat and death: Apocalyptic scenes from the fire frontpublished at 09:57 BST 3 September
    09:57 BST 3 September

    Ahmad Dhani
    Reporting from Banjarbaru, South Kalimantan

    When I first came to the fire front this morning the air was thick. I saw someone on a motorcycle disappearing into the haze.

    Smoke continued to pour out of the ground and trees. I saw the fire team find the burned carcass of a python.

    The smoke was a lot. I tried to use an extra mask and layer of tissue, but still felt it penetrating my nose and throat. It didn't take long for my tissues and mask to get wet with sweat, so I just took it off.

    The fire team continued to water the smoky soil, because the roots below were still burning and difficult to extinguish. They had to continue to water the soil "until it becomes like porridge", they said.

    After watering, one team took turns with the other, then went out in search of fresher air where they could rest.

    The sound of helicopters flying low overhead was so loud – dozens of times, back and forth, from the water source to douse the fire.

What are peatlands?published at 10:17 BST 3 September
10:17 BST 3 September

Gavin Butler
Reporting from Singapore

Smoke rises from a peatland area in Indonesia's West Kalimantan province

As we have reported, there are fears the fires in Indonesia could activate millennia-old "carbon bombs", as burning peatlands threaten to release huge amounts of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.

Peatlands are waterlogged ecosystems which accumulate large amounts of semi-decomposed vegetation. They have a high carbon content, and can support forest and vegetation growth.

But if they become dry, as we have seen in these recent fires, they can also become highly flammable.

The area that's burning in Indonesia is a major peatland. It is considered globally important due to the amount of carbon that it stores and could potentially release - either as carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, or carbon monoxide, a toxic gas to humans.

Susan Page, a professor of geology and environment at the University of Leicester, told the BBC that as soon as peatlands are drained, "we convert long-term carbon sinks into a short-term carbon source".

"That means that they then start to emit carbon into the atmosphere, carbon that maybe was accumulating thousands of years ago."
