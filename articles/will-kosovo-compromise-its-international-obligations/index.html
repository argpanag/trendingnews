Will Kosovo Compromise its International Obligations?

TheTools · 17.11.2026 15:58 · source · permalink

The Specialist Chambers and the New Amending Law

On 16 September 2026, Trial Panel II of the Kosovo Specialist Chambers (KSC/Specialist Chambers) rendered its judgment in the Hashim Thaҫi et al. case. This is by far the largest core crimes case before the KSC involving former senior leaders of the Kosovo Liberation Army and subsequent senior office holders in Kosovo, topped by Mr Thaçi, the former President of Kosovo.

The Trial Panel acquitted the four accused on all six counts concerning crimes against humanity. It also acquitted the four accused of several incidents of war crimes.  Yet, the Trial Panel convicted each of the defendants of four counts of war crimes. The Trial Panel sentenced Messrs Thaҫi and Krasniqi, each to a single sentence of 25 years of imprisonment, Mr Veseli to a single sentence of 18 years and Mr Selimi to a single sentence of 13 years, with credit for the time served for each of them.

The judgment triggered strong negative reactions in Kosovo, given the status of the defendants in the fight for the independence of Kosovo. A few days after the pronouncement of the judgment, the Democratic Party of Kosovo (“PDK”) – one of the country’s main political parties, formerly headed by Messrs Thaҫi and Veseli –  introduced an amendment (Amending Law)1) to the law establishing the KSC and Specialist Prosecutor’s Office, namely the Law on Specialist Chambers and Specialist Prosecutor’s Office  (see reports on the initiative here and here).2) The Amending Law is principally aimed at substantially restricting the subject-matter and temporal jurisdiction of the KSC. It also removes important existing competences of the Supreme Court Panel and curtails the constitutional review powers of the Specialist Chamber of the Constitutional Court. The Assembly of Kosovo adopted the Amending Law on 6 October 2026 and subsequently submitted it to the President of Kosovo for signature and promulgation.3) Yet, it was returned on 22 October 2026 to the Assembly legislative committee for reconsideration.4) The Amending Law may be readopted provided that it secures “a majority vote of all its deputies” (Art. 80 (4) CoK).

This blog post argues that the Amending Law, if promulgated, would violate Kosovo’s international obligations vis-à-vis the European Union (EU), since Kosovo carried out the amendments unilaterally and without prior consultations with the EU. It further observes that, from a domestic perspective, the Amending Law raises concerns about its constitutionality. It concludes that although Kosovo is entitled to amend its domestic legislation, it must exercise that authority in conformity with its Constitution and without compromising its international obligations.

The Amending Law and Kosovo’s International Obligations

The question remains whether the Amending Law, which is currently under reconsideration by the Assembly of Kosovo, would, if adopted and promulgated, constitute a valid unilateral act consistent with Kosovo’s international obligations. The answer is a clear-cut “No”.

The starting point is the 2014 Exchange of Letters signed on 14 April 2014 between the then President of Kosovo and the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy.

It is clear from the Exchange of Letters that the parties agreed on a number of fundamental points integral to the effective operation of the envisaged specialist chambers and specialist prosecutor’s office. These may be summarised as follows: 1. Proceedings related to the SITF investigations should be conducted before new dedicated and separate judicial chambers; 2. Filings and sensitive records to be maintained outside Kosovo; 3. A constitutional court to be part of the structure of the newly established specialist chambers to deal exclusively with proceedings arising from the SITF investigations; 4. The statute of the specialist chambers should not include provisions granting pardons or serving a convicted person’s sentence in Kosovo; 5. Kosovo will delegate all necessary powers and mandates to operate the specialist chambers and specialist prosecutor’s office; and 6. There must be consultations in the process of “amending laws having an impact” on the effective functioning and operations of the specialist chamber’s judicial proceedings.

The Amending Law, which includes 27 articles, introduced several provisions that are inconsistent with the terms of the 2014 Exchange of Letters, namely: service of sentences of convicted persons in Kosovo; eligibility of convicted persons for pardons; transfer of cases and materials in case of termination of proceedings due to lack of jurisdiction; delivery of materials connected to cases transferred to Kosovo; delivery of materials necessary for the continuation of proceedings to judicial and prosecutorial authorities of Kosovo; restricting the Specialist Chambers of the Constitutional Court (SCCC) from reviewing the constitutionality of any law, including Law No. 05/L-053 and any law amending, supplementing or repealing it; and introducing a new provision, which deprives the KSC Supreme Court from its jurisdiction to adjudicate third instance appeals, requests for protection of legality and any other legal remedy under Law No. 05/L-053, and provides such competence to the Supreme Court of Kosovo.

By adopting the Amending Law in the Assembly, Kosovo not only introduced provisions that are in breach of the terms of the 2014 Exchange of Letters, but also did so unilaterally and without consultations with the EU, as this agreement dictates, and as Article 162(12) and (13) of the CoK requires. Although Article 162(13) and (14) of the CoK envisions the requirement of consultations in the context of ending the mandate of the KSC and SPO, the SCCC clarified that such a requirement goes beyond “completion of the [KSC] mandate”. In its 26 November 2020 Judgment, the SCCC stated:

“[I]t should not escape the Chambers’s attention that the 2014 exchange of letters provides for a requirement on Kosovo to conduct consultations with the respective EU authorities in the process of amending Kosovo laws having an impact on judicial proceedings arising out of the SITF investigations. […] [Article 162(13) and (14) of CoK] refer not only to ‘completion of mandate’ but also, more generally, specify the mandate by reference to Law No. 04/L-274. Therefore, the obligation to conduct consultations as provided for in the 2014 exchange of letters is also a constitutional requirement relevant in the legislative process of Kosovo. […] It is true that the requirement to conduct consultations in the process of amending the respective domestic laws of Kosovo may be seen as restricting in some ways the exercise of the legislative powers by the national authorities. Yet, such restriction is consistent with the delegation of specific sovereign powers for the operation of the Specialist chambers and the SPO under the 2014 exchange of letters. Furthermore, with regard to the Specialist Chambers and the SPO, the requirement of consultations safeguards their independent, effective and secure operation.”5) (26 November 2020 SCCC Judgment, paras 71, 73-74).

The 2014 Exchange of Letters is an international agreement that became an integral part of the Kosovo legal system. As such, it has “superiority over the laws of the Republic of Kosovo” (Article 19 CoK), including the Amending Law.

Thus, from an international law perspective, the adoption by Kosovo of the Amending Law, insofar as it introduces provisions incompatible with the obligations undertaken in the 2014 Exchange of Letters, has the effect of unilaterally modifying the substance of the obligations arising from an international agreement, without the consent of the other party. Such conduct would be incompatible with the customary principle of pacta sunt servanda, which requires international obligations to be performed in good faith.

In the Nuclear Tests cases, the ICJ affirmed that good faith is

“[o]ne of the basic principles governing the creation and performance of legal obligations, whatever their source […]. Just as the very rule of pacta sunt servanda in the law of treaties is based on good faith, so also is the binding character of an international obligation assumed by unilateral declaration. Thus, interested States […] are entitled to require that the obligation thus created be respected” (Nuclear Tests (Australia v. France), Judgment, ICJ Reports 1974, p. 268, para. 46; Nuclear Tests (New Zealand v. France), Judgment, ICJ Reports 1974, p. 473, para. 49).

In Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros, the ICJ ruled against a State acting in a unilateral fashion in the context of performance of treaties, stating that:

“The Court would set a precedent with disturbing implications for treaty relations and the integrity of the rule pacta sunt servanda if it were to conclude that a treaty in force between States, […] might be unilaterally set aside […]. It would be otherwise, of course, if the parties decided to terminate the Treaty by mutual consent”.

It would be otherwise, of course, if the parties decided to terminate the Treaty by mutual consent”.

Although the ICJ’s pronouncement concerns termination of a treaty rather than its amendment, the underlying logic is relevant: one party cannot unilaterally alter the legal relationship created by an international agreement. In this context, it has been reported that politicians from Kosovo referred to the President of the European Commission, Ursula von der Leyen, stating during her recent visit to Kosovo before adopting the Amending Law, “that it is Kosovo’s right to amend the law”, yet “[the KSC’s] mandate must be fully respected” (Koha, 7 October 2026; Argumentum, 1 October 2026).  This was followed by statements from the EU External Action spokesperson who said “[t]he Law adopted by the Kosovo Assembly amending and supplementing the Law on the Specialist Chambers goes against Kosovo’s international obligations towards the EU” (EEAS, 8 October 2026). The EU Commissioner for Enlargement, Marta Kos, also stated that the Amending Law “violate[s] Kosovo’s fundamental obligations towards the European Union” (Marta Kos, 15 October 2026).

This issue raises the question of the legal effect of such statements on the EU. Even assuming arguendo that the statement attributed to the President of the European Commission could be interpreted as acknowledging Kosovo’s competence to amend its domestic laws, particularly Law No. 05/L-053, this does not expressly authorize Kosovo to depart from the obligations arising from the 2014 Exchange of Letters. This conclusion is reinforced by the acknowledgment voiced at the same time that the KSC’s “mandate must be fully respected”. Moreover, her response about “Kosovo’s right to amend the law” was not part of her official statement, but only a response to a question posed by the media. Thus, the adoption of the Amending Law can hardly be reconciled with the requirement to respect the KSC’s mandate and judicial independence. One may further question whether such a statement may be considered an expression of consent on the part of the EU to modify the terms of the 2014 Exchange of Letters through the adoption of a law the provisions of which are inconsistent with the 2014 Exchange of Letters. While this reading attributes undue weight to a statement that was merely intended to address a question concerning domestic legislative competence as opposed to modification of an international agreement, the hypothesis remains valid.

In this regard, one may question whether the President of the European Commission was legally empowered to bind the EU or to express consent to a modification of the terms of an international agreement. Under the jurisprudence of the CJEU, EU institutions may act externally only within the powers conferred upon them and must respect the institutional allocation of competences6). In French Republic v. Commission, the CJEU stated that the Commission has only the powers which have been conferred upon it (para. 36).

Furthermore, in its 26 November 2020 Judgment, the SCCC quoted the relevant part of the 2014 Exchange of Letters which contemplate that consultations concerning the process of amending laws having an impact on the “judicial proceedings arising from SITF investigation should be undertaken with ‘EULEX Kosovo Head of Mission and/or EUSR” (26 November 2020 SCCC Judgment, para. 13). Whether the authority or functions entrusted to those latter representatives extended to consenting, on behalf of the EU, to a modification of the international agreement itself is a separate question. Suffice it to observe that EULEX Kosovo is a mission established within the framework of the Common Foreign and Security Policy (“CFSP”) and the Common Security and Defence Policy (“CSDP”). Pursuant to Article 17(1) of the Treaty on European Union (“TEU”), the Commission’s competence to ensure the external representation of the Union expressly excludes matters falling within the CFSP. By contrast, Article 27(2) TEU entrusts the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy with representing the Union in matters relating to the CFSP. The CJEU has affirmed this distinction.7) Accordingly, the President of the European Commission cannot, merely by virtue of her office, be presumed to possess the full powers or authority to express the EU’s consent to a modification of the obligations arising from an international agreement falling within the CFSP. Such authority can be established by reference to the relevant provisions of the EU Treaties and the applicable procedures governing the modification of international agreements.8)

Potential Conflict with the Constitution of Kosovo

While the Amending Law is questionable from an international law perspective, it is also problematic from a domestic law standpoint. The Chapeau of Article 162 of the CoK reads: “[N]ot withstanding any provision in this Constitution”. This formulation stresses the lex specialis nature of this provision vis-à-vis any matter related to the regulation of the Specialist Chambers and SPO. Article 162(1) of the CoK, second sentence clarifies that “[t]he organisation, functioning, and jurisdiction of the Specialist Chambers and Specialist Prosecutor’s Office shall be regulated by this Article and by a specific law”. The “specific law” is currently Law No. 05/L-053. If the Amending Law came into force, theoretically the operation of the KSC should be governed also by that Amending Law, but insofar as the latter does not conflict with Article 162 of the CoK. In other words, the Amending Law should be subject to the regulation of Article 162 and in case of conflict, Article 162 should prevail (see Article 16(1) CoK: “[t]he Constitution of Kosovo is the highest legal act of the Republic of Kosovo. Laws and other legal acts shall be in accordance with this Constitution”).

In this respect, Article 162 includes 14 paragraphs, of which notably paragraphs 3, 4, 8, 11, 13 and 14 regulate core competencies of the Specialist Chambers. For example, paragraph 4 makes clear that the Specialist Chambers and SPO “shall have full legal and juridical personality and shall have all the necessary powers and mandate for their operation […] [including] detention and the service of sentence outside the territory of Kosovo for anyone convicted […]”. Paragraph 3 provides for a SCCC, which “shall exclusively decide any constitutional referrals under Article 113 of the CoK relating to the Specialist Chambers and Specialist Prosecutor’s Office in accordance with a specific law”. This provision should be read in conjunction with Article 113 of the CoK and Article 49 of Law No. 05/L-053. In particular, Article 49(2) stipulates that the SCCC “shall have jurisdiction over any referral to the Constitutional Court made by persons authorised to make referrals under Article 113 of the Constitution which relates to or directly impacts the work, decisions, orders or judgments of the Specialist Chambers or the work of the Specialist Prosecutor’s Office”. Paragraph 8, like paragraph 4, also refers to convicted persons serving their sentence outside Kosovo. Paragraph 11 provides exclusive competence to a separate Ombudsperson for the Specialist Chambers and SPO who may make referrals under Article 113(2) of the CoK to the SCCC and Article 49(5) of Law No. 05/L-053. The latter provision empowers the Specialist Chamber’s Ombudsperson, inter alia, to submit a referral to the SCCC on the question of compatibility of laws with the CoK. This may include the Amending Law. Thus, comparing the above provisions of Article 162 with the equivalent from the Amending Law (see, section 2 above), it becomes clear that the Amending Law is incompatible with Article 162 of the CoK. It follows that the Amending Law may cast doubt about its constitutionality.

Conclusion

The latest attack against the Specialist Chambers is hardly surprising, as it appears to constitute a direct response to the Trial Panel’s Judgment of 16 September 2026. Against the backdrop of a rapidly changing geopolitical landscape, in which international justice and the rule of law have increasingly become entangled in the political agendas of the great powers, the current challenge facing the KSC may be viewed as merely one small scene in a much larger unfolding saga.

It is not disputed that Kosovo may exercise its sovereign powers and regulate its domestic affairs through legislation, including amendments to its existing laws. Nevertheless, the exercise of these powers must remain consistent with Kosovo’s international obligations and the requirements of its own Constitution. Kosovo’s sovereign right to legislate cannot serve as a justification for circumventing its international obligations or for unilaterally altering the legal relationship established by the 2014 Exchange of Letters. Moreover, Kosovo’s executive mandates, powers and authorities that have been delegated to operate the Specialist Chambers and SPO by virtue of the 2014 Exchange of Letters, and Article 162(4) of the CoK in conjunction with Article 5 of Law No. 05/L-053, cannot be overridden by the mere adoption of the Amending Law without first amending the CoK. As argued above, by adopting the Amending Law, Kosovo appears to have disregarded these requirements. By doing so, it has enacted legislation that raises serious concerns as to its constitutionality.
