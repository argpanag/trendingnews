Lululemon founder’s remarks about women’s reproductive choices spark outrage at Calgary event

TheTools · 25.11.2026 00:48 · source · permalink

Lululemon founder Chip Wilson, shown in Vancouver in 2022, has not been in a leadership position at the retailer for more than a decade, but remains one of its largest shareholders.DARRYL DYCK/The Canadian Press

Lululemon Athletica Inc. LULU-Q founder Chip Wilson told a leadership conference on Monday that women in Canada are often not agreeing to have as many children as men want, and made other comments about women’s reproductive choices that led some attendees to walk out, according to two sources in attendance.

Mr. Wilson also said that some men may need to import brides from Thailand to have multiple children, according to one of the sources.

The Globe and Mail agreed not to name the sources because they are not authorized to speak publicly about the event in Calgary. But the organizer also acknowledged on Tuesday that Mr. Wilson’s comments had raised concerns.

The event at the BMO Centre was organized by Axis Connects, a non-profit organization that “exists to advance women in leadership and to create space for candid conversations about the barriers women face both in business and society,” Axis Connects’ chief executive Nuvyn Peters wrote in an e-mailed statement to The Globe.

“We recognize that several comments made by Chip Wilson at the Leadership Forum were offensive and inconsistent with the values and mission of Axis Connects,” she wrote.

    There were 720 people registered to attend Monday’s “leadership forum,” the majority of whom were women.

    During the onstage question-and-answer session, moderated by Ms. Peters, Mr. Wilson said birth control had been crucial to women’s professional development. That is because business leaders such as himself became more willing to hire and invest in women in their early 20s without being concerned they would go on maternity leave right away – and would instead delay having families, according to the sources.

    The comments elicited audible gasps from the assembled crowd, the sources said.

    “We understand why those remarks caused concern and disappointment among members of our community,” Ms. Peters of Axis Connects wrote. “His comments also underscore an uncomfortable reality: attitudes and assumptions that hold women back in the workplace and in leadership have not disappeared. Challenging those attitudes is central to the work and ethos of Axis Connects.”

    Mr. Wilson has not been in a leadership position at Lululemon for more than a decade, but remains one of the company’s largest shareholders.

    Pedestrians pass a Lululemon store in Toronto. Mr. Wilson stepped down as chair of the board in 2013 and launched a proxy battle for control of the retailer last year.Sammy Kogan/The Globe and Mail

    He has made much of his fortune from women whose independence made them attractive customers: In the early days of Lululemon – the athleisure giant he founded in 1998 – he described its archetypal customer as the “super girl,” a 32-year-old professional without children, and with enough disposable income to drop $100 or more on a pair of yoga pants.

    In his memoir, Little Black Stretchy Pants, Mr. Wilson wrote about the women who also profited under his leadership. “When Lululemon was much, much smaller, I’d told a group of our people that if we ever went public, I would give them 10 percent of my shares. I did this, which created thirty or so multimillionaires—most of them women (who have since gone on to build their own businesses),” he wrote.

    During Monday’s session, Mr. Wilson remarked that some of North America’s most successful women’s apparel brands are run by men, and said men have a distinct idea of what looks good on women’s bodies, according to the sources, while women also have a distinct idea of what looks good on men.

    The Globe contacted a spokesperson for Mr. Wilson to ask if he would comment on his remarks and received an e-mailed reply saying: “This is an effort to discount Chip Wilson’s record of mentoring women who have become executives and entrepreneurs in their own right to drive revenue with a clickbait headline.”

    Mr. Wilson stepped down as chair of Lululemon’s board in 2013, after explaining during a televised interview that customer complaints about see-through fabric in some yoga pants were not the fault of quality control, but rather the issue was that “some women’s bodies just actually don’t work for” the clothes. When Ms. Peters asked Mr. Wilson on Monday whether he regretted those comments in retrospect, he replied that they were taken out of context, the sources said.

    Last year, Mr. Wilson launched a proxy battle for control of the retailer, criticizing its “loss of cool” and declining appeal to customers. That conflict was resolved after Lululemon agreed to appoint two nominees he had put forward to the board of directors.

    Copies of Mr. Wilson’s book, Lululemon and the Future of Technical Apparel, were included in gift bags handed out at the event on Monday, but many attendees took the books out of the bags and left them on the tables in the room, the sources said. One source said they saw organizers clearing away stacks of the books.

    The conference advertised sessions with “some of Canada’s most respected executives and thought leaders,” including a keynote address from musician and author Jann Arden, and sessions featuring former Alberta premier Jason Kenney and five-time Olympic medalist Tessa Virtue.

    Attendees at the event were invited to submit questions during sessions via a QR code, which also allowed people to see others’ suggested questions. During Mr. Wilson’s session, people submitted questions asking him to clarify whether he thought female employees lost their value to companies after having children, and how parenting his own children had affected his ability to do his job, one source recalled. The session was supposed to last for one hour, but Ms. Peters cut it short.

    “We remain committed to advancing women in leadership and to building a business community in which women have equitable opportunities to lead and succeed,” Ms. Peters wrote.

    With a report from Carrie Tait
