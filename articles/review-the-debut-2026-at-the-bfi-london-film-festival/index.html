‘The Debut’ (2026) review – Jesse Eisenberg’s cringeworthy community theatre comedy

Was this really supposed to be a “love letter to community theatre” from actor-director Jesse Eisenberg, as described in the billing for its London Film Festival screenings? Because The Debut, starring Julianne Moore as a woman cast in a local production of a musical who slowly loses herself in the role, largely paints the theatre world as a bright, over-complimentary utopia devoid of the same reality for which Moore’s character ends up desperately looking.

The fictional musical is called Nosy Neighbours – about the occupants of an apartment building – and Mona (Moore) ends up being cast as the minor character Miss Danielle, its superintendent, after the initial Serena Cherry dropped out. Key scenes and lines for the ‘super’ include telling people their ‘rent’s due’, fixing a tenant’s toilet and proclaiming “let wedding bells chime” when she is asked to sign a marriage contract between The Girl in 4 F (Halle Bailey) and The Boy in 26D (Colton Ryan). Eisenberg penned the numbers of the musical himself, and they’re impressive and convincing, with a satisfying rhyme scheme and melody. The musical’s script falters, however, in having Miss Danielle end every sentence in one scene with the other character’s name, as if we’d forgotten it already. A trope in some musicals, sure, but one from which I’d argue many are moving away.

To begin with, Moore’s Mona shrinks in everyday life, letting people barge in front of her at the supermarket, over-apologising, and getting lost in wide shots which show the expanse of the stage. This begins to change upon learning from director Jerry (a sharp, complex performance from Paul Giamatti) that she has the part of Miss Danielle, and she excitedly tells her husband Howard (Patrick Fabian, who spends most of the time sleeping, sitting on the sofa or defecating) “I’m Miss Danielle” as a warm heavenly glow lights up her face. She buys bananas for everyone in rehearsals, meets cast assistant Piotr who offers to do practically anything for her during the process, and bonds with female lead Stef (Bailey) who excitedly tells her, “We’re going to have so much fun together”. Hell, at one point, Mona hears a voice in the clouds above.

It all feels like a dream, not least because of the superficial writing from Eisenberg, but we never arrive at a point of true realism in the film, where the almost ‘rose-tinted glasses’ view of theatre is replaced with something a little more raw. There’s no proper indication in the direction or writing as to whether this is genuinely just an overly positive community theatre group, or that we’re watching a warped or imagined world and interactions from Mona’s perspective. The pretence never really goes away, and the humour suffers as a result, making it awkward and cringeworthy as opposed to silly or wacky because we don’t really know the context underpinning it.

We then watch Jerry coach Mona from one extreme to the other, as she goes from shy to shouty (thanks to a brilliant scene where the director has the actor try to convince him to give her an envelope containing his ‘rent payment’, where we watch that transformation in real-time) to full-blown method. The unlit cigarette placed in her mouth, initially symbolising a lack of commitment, becomes a source of confidence for Mona as she becomes Miss Danielle. Moore navigates the transformation seamlessly, from nervous to a kind of lying-to-oneself level of confidence, her growing erratic nature as Mona is a thought-provoking watch, in spite of the aforementioned narrative flaws.

Eventually, she rents a room in a hotel to get closer to the role, as she moves away from the ‘Mona mask’ she’s been wearing all her life – an idea which is probably the closest we get to a realisation or ‘penny drop’ from the protagonist that she is disconnected from her ‘truth’ and the truth. We learn Jerry has his own mask too, and that comes off in a fiery scene where his patience runs out with Mona, and a tender moment when he misses his time as an actor.

Some of the most enlightening details in The Debut come in Drew Daniels’ work as director of photography. The glances in the mirror provide a reference to the self and Mona’s focus, and the lighting showing the Brook Arts Center shining bright against the gloom of the surrounding streets is not only gorgeous to look at, but highlights the pull of the stage and Miss Danielle for Mona compared to her otherwise unremarkable life with Howard, the kids and her unsupportive friends. One scene in which Mona, more like Miss Danielle, stands underneath the comedy mask of the familiar comedy and tragedy mask pairing, both references the aforementioned ‘Mona mask’ idea, but also reinforces the duality of the protagonist’s life. When the two masks are flipped at the end of the film – so ‘comedy’ now on the other side – it’s a subtle nod to the change Mona has undergone.

All of it is reminiscent of Opening Night – that is, the disastrous stage adaptation performed in early 2024, not the original 1977 film, as I haven’t seen it. In that case, the death of a fan contributes to an actor having an existential crisis while performing The Second Woman. By the end of the musical, when Sheridan Smith’s Myrtle takes to the stage on opening night, she’s blowing raspberries as her co-star and going completely off-script, and the same happens here as Mona begins fact-checking the script and insisting on reality.

What may seem, at first, to be a powerful piece on the messy process of honing an actor’s performance – and that really would be a nice message to send about the beauty of community theatre – becomes a weird psychodrama and exploration of the ego. As Mona becomes more and more unrecognisable as she assumes the role of Miss Danielle, so too does the picture The Debut paints of community theatre.
