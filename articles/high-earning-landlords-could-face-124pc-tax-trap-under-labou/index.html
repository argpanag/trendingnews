High-earning landlords could face 124pc tax trap under Labour

TheTools · 13.11.2026 14:13 · source · permalink

High-earning landlords face an effective tax rate of up to 124pc under a Labour squeeze on rental income.

Buy-to-let owners on a salary of £100,000 or more face paying £1.24 to HMRC for every £1 of profit following the Chancellor’s decision to raise income tax on rental income by four percentage points from April next year.

A landlord with a single rental property risks a shortfall of more than £1,000 each year because of a quirk in the tax system that penalises higher earners for every pound they earn over £100,000, combined with the removal of mortgage interest relief.

Analysis by the Intermediary Mortgage Lenders Association (IMLA), a trade body, looked at the impact of rising tax rates on the average high-earner renting out a typical rental property valued at £250,000.

By taking the average rental yield of 5.8pc, as estimated by property portal Zoopla, and deducting 25pc in annual operating costs, in line with the industry average, it would leave landlords with a net rental income before mortgage interest of £10,875.

After deducting mortgage interest charged at the Bank of England’s current rate of 4.34pc on a standard £150,000 loan, it would leave landlords with a profit of £4,365.

The new higher rate of income tax for rental properties, charged at 44pc from April next year – which is levied on pre-mortgage rental income rather than profit – would trigger a total tax bill of £3,223 once tax credits are factored in.

However, because the personal allowance is withdrawn at a rate of £1 for every £2 earned over £100,000, the additional rental income of £10,875 would be added to their total taxable salary for the year, leading to an extra £2,175 lost to tax that would otherwise have been exempt.

This means higher-earning landlords would face a total tax hit of £5,398, against a profit of just £4,365 from their rental – equivalent to an effective rate of 124pc.

Only when the landlord’s salary reached £125,140 – the point at which personal allowance is fully withdrawn – would they start to see their effective tax rate reduce to around 86pc.

Kate Davies, executive director at the IMLA, warned further tax rises from next year threatened to leave many landlords with no choice but to leave the sector.

She said: “If a landlord holding a property in their personal name is having to dip into their salary to keep a property going, the higher tax bill may bring forward a decision to sell.”

Between November 2022 and August 2026, the number of mortgaged buy-to-let properties fell by 157,000, according to the trade body, as landlords were forced to contend with rising costs and additional red tape under Labour’s Renters’ Rights Act.

Ms Davies added that while holding the property in a corporate structure could be more tax-efficient, existing buy-to-let owners faced stamp duty and potential capital gains tax bills for transferring into a company.

She said the changes in April were likely to drive a shift in the rental market away from smaller landlords whose returns were being “squeezed” and towards larger, more corporate outfits.

Rachel Reeves, the former chancellor, introduced a higher income tax rate for rental income in her November 2025 Budget, adding two percentage points to rates for the 2027-28 tax year.

She justified these rises by highlighting the higher taxes on earnings faced by workers when National Insurance was accounted for.

Mr Healey, her successor, increased these rates in his maiden Budget by a further two percentage points, meaning basic-rate, higher-rate and additional-rate landlords face a jump to 24pc, 44pc and 49pc respectively.

Yet even before the rate rises, IMLA analysis showed high earners were still facing losses on a typical rental property of £1.20 in every £1 earned because of the cliff-edge effect of personal allowance rules and the loss of mortgage interest relief.

Full deduction of mortgage interest from rental income was replaced by a flat 20pc tax credit in April 2020.

Charlene Young, at wealth adviser AJ Bell, said landlords whose earnings before rental income already reached £100,000 or more could reduce their tax bills by using pension contributions or Gift Aid donations.

She added: “These can effectively move the threshold at which the personal allowance is lost and potentially save a significant amount of tax.”

A Treasury spokesman said: “We are raising taxes on banks and landlords to help give breathing space to people, including £100 cost of living payments for 20 million families.

“The tax someone pays will depend on their individual circumstances, including their income and the allowances and reliefs they can claim. The vast majority of taxpayers will be unaffected by these changes, with 95pc paying no more.”
