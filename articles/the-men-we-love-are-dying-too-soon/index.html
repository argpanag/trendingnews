In 1999, my old­er broth­er, his­to­ri­an Dr Jerome Teelucks­ingh, chose our fa­ther’s birth­day as In­ter­na­tion­al Men’s Day in Trinidad and To­ba­go. No­vem­ber 19 be­came an in­vi­ta­tion to ho­n­our the con­tri­bu­tions of men, im­prove gen­der re­la­tions, pro­mote pos­i­tive role mod­els and con­front the prob­lems we of­ten en­dure in si­lence.

The 2026 theme, “The Fu­ture Is Every­one”, chal­lenges the fool­ish idea that at­ten­tion to men’s health must com­pete with women’s health. A health­i­er man is more like­ly to be present as a fa­ther, part­ner, broth­er, son, col­league, neigh­bour and friend. Car­ing about men strength­ens fam­i­lies.

Men are of­ten taught that mas­culin­i­ty means en­durance with­out com­plaint and that “big boys don’t cry”. A young man learns that fear should be dis­guised as anger and un­cer­tain­ty cov­ered with jokes. A hus­band may be­lieve his val­ue lies chiefly in pro­vid­ing, even when the ef­fort is break­ing him.

A day’s clin­ic ap­point­ment may mean lost wages. Some men have had dis­mis­sive en­coun­ters with health­care, while oth­ers fear bad news, in­va­sive ex­am­i­na­tions or ap­pear­ing weak. Gay and bi­sex­u­al men, men with dis­abil­i­ties, mi­grants and those liv­ing in pover­ty may face ad­di­tion­al bar­ri­ers.

High blood pres­sure is a mas­ter of dis­guise, while dam­age ac­cu­mu­lates in the heart, brain, blood ves­sels, kid­neys and eyes. Fea­tures of di­a­betes in­clude thirst, fre­quent uri­na­tion, weight loss, re­cur­rent in­fec­tions and blurred vi­sion, but the con­di­tion may al­so re­main asymp­to­matic for years. High cho­les­terol is usu­al­ly silent.

Every adult man should know his blood pres­sure, blood glu­cose or HbA1c when in­di­cat­ed, cho­les­terol pro­file, weight and waist cir­cum­fer­ence. Screen­ing and mon­i­tor­ing should be tai­lored to age, fam­i­ly his­to­ry and in­di­vid­ual risk.

Fat around the ab­domen is meta­bol­i­cal­ly ac­tive and is as­so­ci­at­ed with di­a­betes, can­cer, fat­ty liv­er dis­ease, sleep ap­noea and car­dio­vas­cu­lar risk. Sham­ing has no place in clin­i­cal care. We should ask about food, sleep, al­co­hol, phys­i­cal ac­tiv­i­ty, med­ica­tion, stress and what change is gen­uine­ly pos­si­ble in that man’s life.

Men’s health is al­so men­tal health.

De­pres­sion in men may ap­pear as ir­ri­tabil­i­ty, reck­less dri­ving, drink­ing, gam­bling, fight­ing, with­draw­ing from friends, work­ing every wak­ing hour or los­ing in­ter­est in sex. Some men de­scribe headaches, back pain, poor sleep or ex­haus­tion rather than sad­ness, hope­less­ness or help­less­ness.

Caribbean men are dis­pro­por­tion­ate­ly rep­re­sent­ed among vic­tims and per­pe­tra­tors of homi­cide, road deaths and se­ri­ous in­juries. Pre­ven­tion re­quires safer com­mu­ni­ties, con­flict-res­o­lu­tion skills, ed­u­ca­tion, em­ploy­ment, re­spon­si­ble polic­ing, men­tal-health sup­port and cred­i­ble male men­tors.

Prostate can­cer is im­por­tant in Caribbean pop­u­la­tions. A PSA blood test is not a per­fect can­cer mark­er, and a raised re­sult can have sev­er­al caus­es. Men should have an in­formed dis­cus­sion with a clin­i­cian about their in­di­vid­ual risk, the pos­si­ble ben­e­fits and harms of test­ing, and what hap­pens af­ter an ab­nor­mal re­sult. Dif­fi­cul­ty pass­ing urine, a weak stream and get­ting up re­peat­ed­ly at night com­mon­ly re­sult from be­nign prostate en­large­ment. Blood in the urine, un­ex­plained weight loss or per­sis­tent bone pain should nev­er be ig­nored.

Erec­tile dys­func­tion can be an ear­ly warn­ing sign of vas­cu­lar dis­ease, di­a­betes, med­ica­tion ef­fects, hor­mon­al prob­lems such as low testos­terone, anx­i­ety or re­la­tion­ship strain.

Tes­tic­u­lar can­cer is un­com­mon but of­ten af­fects younger men. A new pain­less lump, swelling, heav­i­ness or change in a tes­ti­cle war­rants prompt med­ical re­view.

A per­sis­tent cough with bloody phlegm, un­ex­plained weight loss, chang­ing bow­el habits, blood in the stool, un­usu­al bleed­ing, a chang­ing mole or a skin lump that per­sists al­so re­quire at­ten­tion. Men should dis­cuss col­orec­tal can­cer screen­ing, vac­ci­na­tions, den­tal and eye care, and sex­u­al health. HIV and oth­er sex­u­al­ly trans­mit­ted in­fec­tions do not re­spect rep­u­ta­tion, mar­riage, oc­cu­pa­tion or church mem­ber­ship.

Sleep is an­oth­er ne­glect­ed vi­tal sign. Loud snor­ing, chok­ing dur­ing sleep, morn­ing headaches and day­time drowsi­ness may in­di­cate ob­struc­tive sleep ap­noea. Un­treat­ed, it is as­so­ci­at­ed with road ac­ci­dents, high blood pres­sure and car­dio­vas­cu­lar prob­lems.

We must bring screen­ing to work­places and com­mu­ni­ties and of­fer ap­point­ments out­side con­ven­tion­al work­ing hours. Health pro­fes­sion­als should be trained to recog­nise how dis­tress presents in men. Fa­thers, teach­ers and coach­es should show that ask­ing for help is not a sur­ren­der of man­hood.

In­ter­na­tion­al Men’s Day should cel­e­brate the step­fa­ther who stays, the teacher who no­tices a trou­bled boy, the man who apol­o­gis­es, the friend who takes away the car keys and the old­er man who tells a younger one that ten­der­ness can live in the male body.

My fa­ther could not have known that his birth­day would trav­el far be­yond our fam­i­ly. Jerome’s choice gave the world a date on which to ex­am­ine how men live, love, suf­fer and con­tribute.

The finest trib­ute is help­ing men re­main alive and well enough to in­hab­it the fu­ture we keep ask­ing them to build.

The fu­ture is every­one, and men must live to see it.
