'Everybody's shell-shocked': Republican operatives face 2028 prospects after bruising midterm defeats

TheTools · 19.11.2026 11:35 · source · permalink

Some argued JD Vance may no longer be the presumptive 2028 nominee.

On Election Day, many Republicans were bracing for a tough outcome, but not what has been described as a blue tsunami.

"I thought that we were going to keep the Senate," former Republican National Committee Chair Ronna McDaniel told ABC News. "I didn't think that Democrats could make inroads in states like South Carolina and Kansas and even Texas."

Democrats did more than make inroads. They flipped eight GOP-held Senate seats and as of Monday, Democrats hold 256 House seats to the GOP's 170. There are still some outstanding races as of Monday: nine House races and one Senate race.

Over a dozen Republican donors and operatives told ABC News that the Republican Party is still in shock after the sweeping midterm losses. They pointed to a message that focused on culture wars and failed to reach voters squeezed by costs tied to the war in Iran and a Latino coalition that slipped away. Some argued Vice President JD Vance may no longer be the presumptive 2028 nominee. Vance, who has not formally announced he is launching a presidential bid for 2028, has acknowledged "there's a chance" he won't run in 2028.

The president’s record-low approval ratings were also difficult to ignore, some said.

"The losses are all about Trump," said one Republican consultant who worked on races out West and spoke to ABC News on the condition of anonymity. "This was a total rebuke of Donald Trump, both him personally and his politics."

RNC National Press Secretary Natalie Baldassarre said in a statement that Republicans will "continue to put America first,” highlighting that the RNC raised $130 million, sending more than $60 million directly to Republican campaigns.

When reached for comment, the White House emphasized Trump’s signature tax legislation and said that Trump will continue to work for the American people.

"Under President Trump’s leadership, this administration has signed the largest working-class tax cuts in history, secured the border, cut the cost of prescription drugs, added more than one million private-sector jobs, and locked in nearly 20 trade deals. President Trump will never stop delivering results for the American people and he will undoubtedly go down as the most successful and consequential president in American history," White House spokesman Davis Ingle told ABC News in a statement.

Vance's office, when asked for comment, deferred to the White House.

A messaging problem

The donors and operatives who spoke to ABC News agreed Republicans lacked a clear message on affordability -- a key issue for voters.

"We need to talk about pocketbook issues. We didn’t do that,” said Sarah Chamberlain, president and CEO of the Republican Main Street Partnership, a group that fundraises for moderate Republicans.

T.W. Arrighi, a Republican strategist who worked on Republican Darline Graham’s Senate race, said Democrats were talking about affordability "in a more tangible way than we were … We have conservative solutions to these issues, we just didn’t deliver."

A GOP strategist involved in many 2026 races said the party's turnout operation will likely take much of the blame. ABC News' analysis of early voting in Texas and Georgia a week before Election Day showed greater Democratic enthusiasm.

"We just weren't hitting the numbers we needed to hit of just sheer Republicans voting. A lot of them stayed home, and we knew that was happening," the strategist said.

The Republican consultant who worked on races out West added that Republicans had "no choice" but to run on culture war issues because of the high costs brought on by the Iran war.

"You can't tell people to ignore your lying eyes,” the source said. "[Republicans] had no choice but to go down the culture-war road because we couldn't talk about the economy."

Trump said in a phone interview with Fox News on election night that he was "a little bit surprised" by the midterm results and added that part of the reason was the Iran war, directly linking it to gas prices.

In a social media post after the election, Trump wrote that Republicans would have been more successful had he been on the ballot.

Reversal among Latino voters

According to the ABC News Voter Poll, in 2026, Latino voters nationally supported Democratic House candidates by a 30-point margin. In 2024, it was a 13-point margin.

Trump acknowledged his mass-deportation policies may have been "too tough" in an interview with CBS News' "60 Minutes" after the midterms.

Mike Madrid, a GOP consultant who studies Latino voting behavior, said "there is no question that the ICE stuff and deportations played a role."

"There’s also no question that the economy was the No. 1 factor. But in some districts, like south Texas, they're one and the same. Immigration is the economy there," Madrid said.

Democrats swept every congressional district along the southern border, flipping several Republican-held seats in Texas.

Billionaire donor John Catsimatidis told ABC News that the losses came down to the war in Iran and "ICE being too tough on waiters and busboys."

Immigration enforcement under the Trump administration has reached local businesses and restaurants. ABC News reported last May that in D.C., federal law enforcement officials visited dozens of restaurants, carry-out spots and bars across several neighborhoods.

A fight over who leads the RNC

RNC Co-Chair KC Crosbie announced on Nov. 5 that she's challenging RNC Chair Joe Gruters, saying the party should "be willing to confront where we fell short."

A source close to Gruters confirmed to ABC News that he is running for reelection. 

While Trump has since reaffirmed his support for Gruters, some Republicans questioned Gruters' competence.

“Joe Gruters failed the Republican Party, and I find it puzzling that he believes he deserves to be reelected,” said an RNC member.

Florida GOP Chairman Evan Power said in a statement, however, that "Gruters was a key partner in our fight to keep the Free State of Florida red, and he’s the leader I trust to keep our party on offense and ready to win in 2028."

McDaniel said "the committee should support whoever the president wants to be the RNC chair."

She added that donors who may be discouraged from giving in the future need an autopsy from the party over what went wrong.

"People are still wondering what happened, and the sooner that a plan is put forward, and they get in the field and they get data … That's going to be what’s going to bring donors back. But right now, I think everybody's shell-shocked."

The road to 2028

While Vance hasn't ruled out a run in 2028, some donors told ABC News that his proximity to Trump may be a liability.

But a Vance ally who spoke to ABC News cautioned against writing off Vance, comparing it to the 2016 and 2024 election cycles, when different parts of the Republican Party wrote Trump off.

And although Vance is still undecided about a 2028 presidential run, many of Vance's allies expect that he will run for president in 2028, sources confirmed to ABC News Chief Washington Correspondent Jonathan Karl.

Vance's allies say he will decide whether to pursue a White House bid on his own timeline. 

But some Republicans see an opening for Secretary of State Marco Rubio to run for president in 2028 following the midterm elections.

Rubio has said he has no plans to run for president in 2028, adding that he would support Vance if he mounted a presidential run.

Catsimatidis said his family has been supporters of Vance, but he has known Rubio for 15 years and considers the nomination "still open."

A Texas-based GOP donor said the next nominee must appeal to "most all Republicans, a majority of independents and a reasonable group of conservative Democrats."

"We're in like stage two with a five-stage model of grief or something. We're not at the writing-checks-for-new-people yet stage," said one donor. "America doesn't want more MAGA."
