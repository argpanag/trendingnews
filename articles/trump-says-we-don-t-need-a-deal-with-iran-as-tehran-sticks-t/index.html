Trump says "we don't need a deal with Iran," as Tehran sticks to its demands

Follow updates on the war in the Middle East for Friday, Nov. 13, here. See earlier developments below.

What to know about the Iran war:

  • "We don't need a deal with Iran," President Trump declared Thursday in a social media post, hours after he again declared victory in the war during a Veterans Day address, but acknowledged the conflict wasn't over. "Just a question of when we want to end it," he said.
  • A senior Iranian security official insisted Thursday that Tehran would "decide on the manner and agenda of any negotiations" with the U.S. only after its "seven conditions are fulfilled and accepted." Secretary of State Marco Rubio has called those conditions "unacceptable."

Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it destroyed U.S. radar system in Bahrain

Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it destroyed a U.S. early warning radar in Bahrain, the first such claim in months.

It couldn't be independently verified. 

A report by Iran's official IRNA news agency on Friday quoted a statement by the Guard as saying the attack on the U.S. radar system followed a U.S. strike on a telecommunications pole in the southern Iranian port of Sirik.

Bahrain has been one of the strongest critics of Iran and is home to the U.S. Navy's 5th Fleet.

The statement said the attack on the radar was a serious warning to the U.S. to cease its strikes. It was the first report by Iran of targeting a facility in Bahrain since September.

During an air war that began in February, Iran targeted U.S. bases in neighboring Arab nations including Bahrain in retaliation for U.S. strikes. Since then, Iran routinely targets oil tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, the critical waterway for international oil shipments.

Iran continues to warn ships not to pass through the strategic shipping lane without its approval. 

Iran says another ship hit a mine in Strait of Hormuz

Iran's Revolutionary Guard said Friday that there was an explosion on a ship that struck a mine in the Strait of Hormuz, where Tehran has sought to restrict navigation.

Since the outbreak of the Middle East war in late February, Tehran has insisted that vessels obtain its authorization to traverse the strait and has attacked vessels that don't comply.

In response, the United States imposed a naval blockade on Iranian ports and began providing military support to tankers using the vital trade route.

"A few hours ago, a ship carrying liquefied natural gas east of the Strait of Hormuz tried to cross an unauthorized passageway south of the strait, but was hit by a naval mine and exploded violently," the IRGC said on Friday, without giving the name or origin of the vessel.

In recent weeks, Iran's armed forces have repeatedly reported fires or explosions on ships in the strait, often saying they'd hit mines.

The U.S. military, however, claims to have cleared the waterway's routes of mines.

U.N. demands halt to Houthi attacks in Yemen and against Saudi Arabia

A Security Council resolution adopted unanimously by the United Nation's most powerful body late Thursday strongly condemned the Houthis military escalation and emphasized that "there is no military solution to the current conflict, and that the only viable path forward is dialogue and reconciliation among the multiple and varied parties."

The U.K.-drafted resolution extended sanctions including travel bans and the freezing of assets of Houthis on a U.N. blacklist until Nov. 14, 2027. An arms embargo against the Houthis remains in effect.

The 15-member council expressed "grave concern" at the continuing Houthi generation of funds from international sources and the rebel group's control over key economic sectors including energy and telecommunications which finance their acquisition of arms including drones.

U.K. Ambassador to the U.N. Sarah MacIntosh said after the vote that the resolution's adoption "demonstrates the council's determination to take decisive action against reckless attacks by the Houthis" in Yemen and against Saudi Arabia.

2 Iranians guilty in London of spying on journalists for Tehran

Two Iranian men who spied on journalists working for a Persian-language broadcaster in London were found guilty Thursday of spying for Iran.

Mostafa Sepahvand, 41, and Farhad Javadi Manesh, 46, were convicted of engaging in conduct likely to assist the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. The IRGC was outlawed by the U.K. in July under new powers to counteract state threats, with penalties of up to 14 years in prison for anyone convicted of supporting or assisting the organization.

A third defendant, Shapoor Qalehali Khani Noori, 57, was acquitted on all charges.

"This was a calculated operation to spy on those critical of the Iranian regime," said Frank Ferguson, chief prosecutor for the Crown Prosecution Service's special crime and counter-terrorism division.

He said the pair were promised rewards in exchange for sending intelligence back to Iran, while Sepahvand was willing to go beyond surveillance to commit serious violence against their targets.

These included Iran International journalists Mojtaba Pourmohsen and Kambiz Hosseini, and Jonathan Turner, a founding member of UK Lawyers for Israel. Iran International is banned by the government in Tehran.

London's Metropolitan Police said this was the fourth case in recent years involving a direct threat to Iran International journalists and that it is "working hard to keep them safe."

"The threat from hostile states to the U.K. is increasing and Iran continues to adapt its approach to using 'proxies'," said Vicki Evans, a deputy assistant commissioner at the London police.

"We have seen examples of people who fly into the country to carry out criminal activity and then leave shortly afterwards, to using British nationals or, in this case, Iranian nationals with settled immigration status," she added.

The Iranian Embassy in the U.K. has said that it "categorically rejects" allegations of involvement in "violent activities or incidents in the United Kingdom."

The pair will be sentenced at London's Central Criminal Court, better known as the Old Bailey, on Dec. 17.

Iran's powerful Assembly of Experts urges regime to abandon nuclear non-proliferation treaty

Iran's powerful Assembly of Experts has called on the Islamic Republic leadership to consider pulling Iran out of the international Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT), citing perceived needs to revise "the country's defense doctrine" and establish "the necessary deterrence" given threats from adversaries.

The consideration has "become an unavoidable necessity and a public and national demand," the assembly has determined, according to the semi-official Tasnim news agency and Iran's state-run Press TV network, citing a statement by the body. 

"It is hoped that the esteemed officials, drawing on the wise guidance of the dear Leader, will heed this demand. Should the failed enemies seek to try their luck again and launch another attack, they will certainly face an immediate and effective response, without geographical limits, from our heroic forces," the report cited the assembly as saying.

Iran, like the United States, has long been a party to the treaty, aimed at limiting the number of nuclear weapons around the world and work on developing new ones. Iran ratified the treaty prior to the 1978 Islamic Revolution that brought the current regime to power. 

The Iranian regime has long rejected any desire to develop nuclear weapons, rejecting accusations by the Trump administration and Israel that it was secretly working toward the goal, and often citing its NPT commitment.

While the supreme leader has the final say on all matters in Iran, the Assembly of Experts, elected by the public, wields significant influence both as the body responsible for choosing the leader, and for guiding policy decisions.

U.S. pulls boys' Under-17 World Cup team out of championship in Qatar over security concerns

The United States soccer body pulled its team from the boys' Under-17 World Cup in Qatar starting next week for security reasons, FIFA confirmed Thursday. Qatar and the Gulf region have been on high security alert since the United States military attacks on Iran started in February, though an F1 race is scheduled in Doha this month.

The 48-nation FIFA tournament kicks off in Doha on Wednesday and the U.S. team's scheduled first game was Nov. 21 against Montenegro. Its other group opponents were Chile and Algeria.

"FIFA understands that U.S. Soccer's decision was made in light of the existing U.S. Level 3 travel advisory," FIFA said in a statement, noting other sports events staged safely in Qatar this year. They included a track and field Diamond League meeting, a MotoGP race last week and qualifying games for the 2027 FIBA World Cup in men's basketball.

FIFA said "security assessments remain unchanged, and no new developments affecting the security situation around the tournament have occurred."

FIFA tournament regulations state the U.S. Soccer Federation should be fined "at least" 20,000 Swiss francs ($24,000) by the FIFA disciplinary committee for withdrawing fewer than 30 days before the opening game.

The USSF informed players Wednesday of the decision, hours after FIFA published the American squad among official player lists for the tournament. The 21 players picked by coach Alex Aldaz last week were drawn from clubs in the U.S., England, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Spain.  

"The safety and security of all participants and fans remains paramount," FIFA said. "As with all FIFA tournaments and events, we are in close contact with the relevant authorities and are looking forward to the tournament kickoff in Qatar." 

Senior official says Iran will only decide "manner and agenda of any negotiations" after its 7 conditions met

The head of Iran's feared paramilitary police force the Basij said Thursday that Iran would "decide on the manner and agenda of any negotiations only after its seven conditions are fulfilled and accepted by the other side," according to Iranian state news agency IRNA.

It was a reiteration of Iran's refusal to budge on its core demands for a preliminary peace deal with the U.S. — and a seemingly deliberate response to President Trump's assertion on Wednesday that the costly conflict had already been a victory for his administration, and it was "just a question of when we want to end it." 

Basij chief Hossein Taeb said it made no difference to Iran how long the process of ending the war might take, and that "the main issue is the fulfillment of the conditions set by the Islamic Republic of Iran. After that, Iran will decide on the manner and subjects of negotiations."

As laid out by Vahid Ahmadi, a member of the Iranian Parliament's National Security and Foreign Policy Commission at the end of September, the conditions that must be met before "substantive negotiations" on matters such as Iran's nuclear program can begin are:

  • An ending to hostilities on all fronts, including Lebanon and Gaza
  • The easing of what Iran considers "unjust and illegal sanctions"
  • The lifting of the U.S. naval blockade of Iran's ports and vessels
  • An accepted system for Iran and Oman to control Strait of Hormuz traffic
  • A plan to compensate Iran for damage inflicted during the war
  • The release of frozen Iranian financial assets held abroad
  • An end to all threats against Iran

"These issues only make sense when considered together," Vahid said at the time, adding that some of the conditions could potentially be met during an initial ceasefire, rather than as a prerequisite for one.

"You cannot continue threats and war on the one hand, maintain maritime restrictions, keep Iran's assets frozen and refuse to compensate for the damage inflicted, while expecting Iran to enter serious negotiations on the other," he said, according to the Iranian news outlet IRAF.

Speaking Sunday, U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called Iran's demands "unacceptable" and made it clear there was little expectation of any near-term diplomatic breakthrough.

Hours later, Iran offered to shorten the proposed implementation period for a ceasefire deal, during which some of its seven conditions could be fully met, from seven days to two, calling it a "sign of goodwill." The regime has not, however, shown any willingness to change its core demands.

U.K. maritime risk firm confirms strike caused fire on commercial ship near Strait of Hormuz

British maritime risk company Ambrey said Thursday that a commercial ship was hit the previous day by an unknown projectile just west of the entrance to the Strait of Hormuz, causing a fire in the engine room but no known injuries or pollution. Ambrey said the vessel was struck 14 nautical miles off the coast of Ras al-Khaimah, in the United Arab Emirates.

It is the latest in a series of unceasing attacks on ships in and near the vital shipping lanes of the strait that have kept logistics firms and insurers wary, and global oil prices more than 40% higher than they were before the U.S. launched its war against Iran in conjunction with Israel more than eight months ago.

Despite repeated assertions by the Trump administration and U.S. military since the summer that a southern route through the strait, which skirts close to the UAE and Omani coasts, is safe and open, Iran has claimed numerous attacks on ships attempting to use those shipping lanes, which it has warned vessels against using.

The Ambrey report came hours after Iran's powerful Revolutionary Guard Corp said two tankers carrying over four million barrels of oil had caught on fire after an "accident" while attempting to use what Tehran considers the unauthorized route through the Strait of Hormuz.

The U.S. has imposed its own naval blockade on Iranian ports and vessels, striking several ships this autumn it accuses of trying to breach the restrictions. U.S. forces have also been escorting tankers along the southern route through the strait, some of which Iran has claimed to attack even while they are under American protection.

The U.K. navy's Maritime Trade Operations center separately reported "an incident involving a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz" on Wednesday evening, but the location given was different to that noted by Ambrey in its report.

It was unclear if the two reports by the Western maritime monitoring groups referred to the same two ships Iran said had experienced "massive explosions" on Wednesday.

U.S. home heating costs expected to rise over 7% this winter

U.S. households are expected to face higher costs this winter to warm their homes, especially those depending on heating oil, according to a new forecast.

Winter heating costs are expected to average $1,002 for all fuels for the season, up 7.1% from last winter, according to projections released this week from the National Energy Assistance Directors Association.

But a much sharper increase will hit properties reliant on home heating oil, a particularly popular source in the Northeast for which prices have swelled due to the Iran war.

Those households will pay $2,977 this winter, up 70.2% from last year.

The association pointed to declines in home heating prices in recent weeks, but "families should not assume that prices will continue to fall," said executive director Mark Wolfe.

Nationwide, more than 4 million households use heating oil as their primary fuel source, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, and of those, 82% are concentrated in the Northeast.

Iran war damage will cost Persian Gulf nations more than $200 billion, insurer says

The U.S.-Iran war will cost Gulf countries more than $200 billion in damage and economic losses could reach nearly four times that amount by 2030, insurer Allianz Trade said on Wednesday.

Gulf countries which hosted U.S. forces came under attack on a daily basis in the opening weeks of the war as Iran retaliated against U.S. and Israeli attacks.

"Physical damage across the region is assessed just above $200bn, mostly concentrated in Hormuz-locked economies, especially in energy infrastructure," the insurer said in a report.

More than 80 energy facilities were hit in the war, mostly in Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain, the report said, with a third of them "severely damaged."

Beyond physical damage to energy infrastructure, the war has affected other sectors like tourism and real estate, which will see slower growth over the years.

"The total cost of the war for the GCC is set to reach $790bn, a cumulative gap of 5.9 percent of GDP relative to the pre-war trend through 2030," the report said.

"This reflects the cumulative loss of economic output over 2026-30, the difference between what the region was forecast to produce on its pre-war growth trend and what it is now projected to produce post-war," it said.
