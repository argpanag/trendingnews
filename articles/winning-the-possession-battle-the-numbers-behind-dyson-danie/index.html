Winning the Possession Battle: The Numbers Behind Dyson Daniels’ Staggering Impact | Atlanta Hawks

TheTools · 21.11.2026 01:11 · source · permalink

By Kevin Chouinard

NBA teams have emphasized offensive rebounding more and more over the past six seasons. 

Starting with the 2020-21 season, league-wide offensive rebounding percentages have risen each season. According to basketballreference.com, teams grabbed on average 22.2 percent of their own misses during the 2020-21 season. This season, teams are getting rebounds on 27.3 percent of their misses.

The recipe is simple. More offensive rebounds equals more possessions; more possessions equals more points. NBA teams always strive to optimize the strategies that lead to winning, and in 2026, the game is to win the possession battle.

In Dyson Daniels, the Hawks have the league's ultimate possession generator.

If you have watched Daniels since his arrival in Atlanta, you are probably already familiar with the fact that Daniels gets the Hawks a ton of extra possessions through steals. He has nabbed a league-best 420 steals since the start of the 2024-25 season; the next-best mark for any other NBA player is Kris Dunn's 286 steals.

This season, though, Daniels has used offensive rebounds to become even more of a possession demon. Through games played on or before Nov. 18, Daniels is tied for the fourth-most offensive rebounds in the NBA: 50. And the only three players whom he trails – Moussa Diabate, Walker Kessler, and Donovan Clingan – stand 6-foot-10, 7-foot-2, and 7-foot-2, respectively.

In fact, Daniels' credentials as a possession generator are staggering. He leads the league in both steals and deflections, and he is tied for lead in loose balls recovered.

  • Steals: 42 (1st)
  • Offensive rebounds: 50 (t-4th)
  • Deflections: 89 (1st)
  • Loose balls recovered: 15 (t-1st)

On offense, Daniels excels at cutting, driving and creating for his teammates. As a result, Daniels gets all manner of players guarding him: sometimes teams try to put a center on him in an attempt to keep a rim protector in the paint. Sometimes teams defend him with a small guard to have more length contesting the shots of the Hawks' higher-volume shooters. 

Daniels said his approach to offensive rebounding stays consistent regardless of the type of player defending him.

"One thing I do is always go – no matter who's guarding me. It just might change depending on the different actions that I'm in, different spots on the floor where I'm crashing from, but yeah, whether it's a guard or a big, I'm going to go regardless."

The Hawks' offensive alignment often starts with Daniels inside and center Onyeka Okongwu, the team's second-leading offensive rebounder, outside.

"Obviously, Dyson is like 6-foot-8," Okongwu said, "so usually, the thing about not guarding him is that he will usually have one of the smaller guards on him, and he is much bigger and more athletic than a lot of those guys. I love playing with Dicey. He does a lot of things well. He does everything for us, honestly, on both ends of the floor."

Even taking into account all of the different ways in which Daniels helps the Hawks max out the number of possessions that they get, to highlight those aspects alone would undersell his overall impact. 

"The box score never reflects all that Dyson does," Head Coach Quin Snyder said, "because of all the little things that he does to help his team win. His ability to defend and push the ball and make plays -- he's unselfish offensively and we don't take him for granted."

The Hawks have been so much better with Daniels on the court than off.

"There are many ways to capture Daniels’ impact statistically, and it is most often cited that he is the league’s best havoc creator on defense," podcaster Brad Rowland of Locked on Hawks said. "That manifests in league leads in deflections and steals, and that does showcase one of his elite traits. Still, there is much more to Daniels’ game than steals, and a lot of that shows up in his impact on the team’s success when he’s on the floor. Using Cleaning The Glass data, the Hawks were 10.8 points per 100 possessions better with Daniels on the floor during the 2025-26 season, and that ranked in the 96th percentile. This season, that number is a team-leading 15.2 points per 100 possessions better. It is both logical and accurate to say that Atlanta is better defensively with Daniels on the floor, but the Hawks have also been consistently better on the offensive end when he is deployed."

When you read your favorite NBA outlet either last summer or next, and they do a list of the top-100, or top-50, or top-25 players in the league, you might open it and look for Daniels' name. He really does have that kind of impact on the game. At the same time, he might be the most unconventional player on the list, if he makes it at all. He is not always the easiest player to describe or quantify.

"While leaving some room for grace in that Daniels is perhaps best appreciated with consistent viewing," Rowland said, "I am often frustrated with how low (or even entirely absent) Daniels ends up on these league-wide ranking lists. Part of that may be that defensive impact, as a whole, likely remains underrated in basketball discourse, but Daniels posts only modest scoring numbers which might knock him down for some evaluators."

Yes, the object of the game is to score more points than your opponent. Points matter! 

Even so, if you have a player who is both a force of nature at keeping his opponents from scoring and a whiz at setting up efficient scoring opportunities for his teammates – while also being the league's best possession generator – that archetype is hard to beat.

And Dyson Daniels is one fabulous basketball player.
