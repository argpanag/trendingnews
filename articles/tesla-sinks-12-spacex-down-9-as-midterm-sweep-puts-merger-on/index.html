Even after the drop, Tesla trades far above its five-year average forward P/E.

Tesla (TSLA:NASDAQ) sank another 12% today. That adds to Wednesday's slide, which came after Democrats won the House, the Senate and the governor's office in Texas. Both Tesla and SpaceX (SPCX:NASDAQ) are headquartered in Texas.

A slower road to a merger

SpaceX fell 9% as well. On SpaceX's third-quarter call last month, Musk said merging the two would be a good idea, and both companies are exploring a deal.

Dan Ives is a partner and managing director at Yorkville and one of Wall Street's best-known Tesla bulls. He still expects a merger. But he wrote today that it "now faces a much tougher regulatory roadmap to get this deal done in 2027 given this new political blue roadmap in DC/Texas."

That's a problem for Tesla's bulls. When Barclays upgraded Tesla in late October, it called the merger a key part of the bull case and raised its target to $450.

Priced for Robotaxi, Optimus and the deal

You can't justify Tesla's price on its car and battery earnings alone. Analysts expect normalized EPS of $3.24 in 2028, which is still below the $4.07 Tesla earned in 2022:

Even so, Tesla's forward P/E stood at 189x before today's drop, far above its five-year average of 108x:

At today's price, it's still about 170x. That's the kind of multiple AI stocks get, which helps explain why Tesla fell with chip and data-center stocks today.

Data-center power took a hit too

Bloom Energy (BE:NYSE) sank 17%, even though NTT DATA announced a 900 MW fuel cell deal with Bloom this morning. Goldman Sachs analysts expect Democratic wins at the state and local level to bring "short-term data center moratoria, potentially affecting about 45% of planned US data center capacity expansion."

That's a risk for Tesla too. Its energy business brought in 22% of the company's gross profit in 2025. And Morgan Stanley autos analyst Andrew Percoco just called Tesla the "very obvious player" in data-center batteries.
