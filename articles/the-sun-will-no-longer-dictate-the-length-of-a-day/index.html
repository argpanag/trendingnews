The sun will no longer dictate the length of a day

TheTools · 16.10.2026 01:08 · source · permalink

World’s timekeepers vote to prevent clocks going backwards as Earth’s rotation quickens

A day on Earth is no longer linked to the rising and setting of the sun, after the world’s timekeepers voted to get rid of the leap second.

Since the 1970s, leap seconds have been added to global clocks 27 times, to keep atomic time in sync with Earth’s daily rotation, which has slowed over billions of years.

On such occasions, clocks would pause for a second at 11:59:59 on New Year’s Eve to allow the Earth’s rotation to catch up with atomic time – which is set by the steady tick of electrons rather than planetary movement. The last one happened in 2016.

But on Thursday afternoon, the General Conference on Weights and Measures in Paris approved a resolution to allow the difference between civil time (what people set their clocks to) and solar time to increase from one second to one hour.

It means there will be no more adjustments to clocks until the difference between a solar day and a 24-hour Earth atomic day has slipped by 60 minutes – hundreds of years from now.

‘This is a historical moment’

Noel Dimarcq, a metrologist at the Côte d’Azur Observatory in Nice, France, who formally introduced the resolution, said: “This is a historical moment, a decisive step for UTC [coordinated universal time, an atomic clock equivalent of the solar-based Greenwich Mean Time]. We now have an international reference time scale continuous for the next millennia.”

Keeping atomic clocks in line with solar time was previously deemed important because if it were not corrected it would eventually result in clocks showing the middle of the day occurring at night – although it would take thousands of years for such a difference to become apparent.

A drift of about 25 minutes happens every 500 years.

However, experts say the change is needed because leap seconds have been causing software problems. Earth has been speeding up since 2020, raising the prospect that a negative leap second could soon be needed, making the situation more confusing.

Earth’s time will now run on a new system, called “continuous UTC”, which will come into effect on May 20, 2027 – although it has effectively started already.

Graham Jones, an astrophysicist at the Time and Date website, said: “This has been a long time coming. In the end, the threat of a negative leap second really helped to focus everyone’s minds: in a way, it’s great to see the world come together and take a bold decision.

“And we’re not completely losing our link to solar time. There’s still a limit to how far civil time and solar time can drift apart – we haven’t given ourselves over to atomic time completely.”

Typically, leap seconds were added every two or three years. BT’s speaking clock would add a second’s pause before its third pip while Radio 4 added an extra pip to its 1am bulletin.

In some years, Earth ran bang on time and no adjustment was needed at all.

‘Always errors’ with leap seconds

There have been consequences of tinkering with time. Because leap seconds are only introduced sporadically, it was difficult to implement them in computer code and mistakes could cause systems to fail temporarily.

When a leap second was added in 2012, Mozilla, Reddit, Foursquare, Yelp, LinkedIn and StumbleUpon all reported crashes, and there were problems with the Linux operating system and programs written in Java.

Many computing systems use the network time protocol to keep themselves in sync with the world’s atomic clocks, but most are not programmed to deal with an unexpected extra second.

Google developed a special technique to deal with what it refers to as a “leap smear” and gradually adds milliseconds to its system clocks before the official arrival of the leap second.

Dr Christian Bizouard, at the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service, told Time and Date: “When we apply leap seconds, there are always errors.

“When the leap second system was invented, society did not work with very, very small time resolution. But now there are many applications that need time resolution below one second, for example, financial systems. For that reason, changing UTC by one second is a problem.”

Britain originally argued against abandoning the leap second, fearing that once the link with solar time was broken, it could never be restored, because adding a leap minute or hour to global time would be virtually impossible.

However, on Thursday all 69 member states of the International Bureau of Weights and Measures voted for the change.
