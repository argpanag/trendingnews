Abstract

Mental imagery is a hallmark of human cognition, yet its neural mechanisms remain poorly understood. Speech imagery (the internal simulation of speech without overt articulation) has been proposed to partially share substrates with speech production, but its spatiotemporal dynamics remain controversial. Here, we leveraged high-resolution electrocorticography to investigate shared and modality-specific coding of articulatory kinematic trajectories during speech imagery and articulation. Linear modeling revealed robust articulatory kinematic trajectories encoding in frontoparietal cortex across modalities. Supramodal populations across middle premotor, subcentral and postcentral–supramarginal regions exhibited spatiotemporal stability during integrative planning. In contrast, modality-specific populations for speech imagery and articulation were somatotopically interleaved in primary sensorimotor cortex, revealing a distinct spatiotemporal organization. We further developed a generalized decoding framework that achieved high prediction accuracy for speech imagery (median 80.4%; chance 16.7%), comparable to speech articulation (78.3%). These findings uncover a somato-cognitive organization linking supramodal planning with modality-specific representations, informing imagery-based brain–computer interfaces.

Main

Mental imagery is a unique adaptive trait of human cognition for linking past experiences, current states and future scenarios by simulating and creating mental events1. For example, mental imagery of speech (speech imagery, also commonly termed imagined speech) represents the internally generated, quasi-perceptual experience of speech without overt or audible articulation2,3. This phenomenon exemplifies both an instantiation of language as a vehicle for thought and a specific form of environment-disengaged action4. While overt speech production involves a well-documented sequence comprising conceptual formulation, lexical selection, phonological encoding, motor planning and the execution of articulatory movements2,3,5,6, the neural mechanisms governing speech imagery remain poorly characterized.

Addressing this gap requires clarifying the spatiotemporal relationship between speech imagery and overt articulation. Emerging neurolinguistic models, supported by findings from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), magnetoencephalography (MEG) and scalp electroencephalography (EEG), suggest that initial processes involved in motor planning for both speech imagery and articulation exhibit considerable similarities3,7,8,9. Functional localization studies reveal overlapping frontoparietal activation in regions such as the premotor cortex and supramarginal gyrus (SMG), alongside reduced or altered involvement of primary sensorimotor cortex (SMC)2,7,8,9,10,11,12. These observations have motivated models in which speech imagery may rely on efference copies of motor commands, processed through frontoparietal forward mechanisms to support internal sensorimotor simulation and prediction of speech outcomes7,8,9; however, definitive characterization of the neural organization governing internal and external speech processes remains elusive, limited by the spatiotemporal constraints of noninvasive methods and the scarcity of intracranial recordings during speech imagery. To overcome these constraints, high-density electrocorticography (ECoG) with broad frontoparietal coverage offers an opportunity to delineate these mechanisms with enhanced spatiotemporal precision.

Beyond its theoretical implications, the characterization of speech imagery has direct translational relevance. This phenomenon holds promise for neural decoding applications aimed at restoring communication in individuals with severe speech impairments (for example, stroke, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or locked-in syndrome)13. Recent intracranial EEG studies have made strides in decoding vocalized, attempted and mimed speech by leveraging articulatory movement encoding in the frontoparietal cortex14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22. More recently, imagined speech decoding has also been achieved using single-neuron Utah array recordings, further supporting the feasibility of decoding internally generated speech processes23,24. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether high-density ECoG recordings encompassing distributed frontoparietal networks can capture the continuous internal dynamics of articulation with sufficient fidelity for speech imagery decoding.

Here, to address these questions, we employed high-density ECoG recordings from nine participants to dissect the encoding of articulatory kinematic trajectories (AKTs) during syllable articulation (SA) and syllable imagery (SI). AKTs provide continuous, dynamic representations of speech articulation that extend beyond categorical descriptions and offer a biologically grounded framework for characterizing neural encoding during both overt and covert speech processes19. Using a temporal receptive field (TRF) model19,25,26, we identify modality-specific AKT encoding preferences across frequency bands, characterize shared and distinct neural substrates, and map their spatiotemporal and somatotopic organization. To further evaluate the functional relevance of these encoding patterns, we implemented a unified decoding framework for syllable classification, speech synthesis, and AKT reconstruction across both modalities. Together, these analyses reveal a somato-cognitive architecture underpinning speech imagery and articulation, which may inform the future development of brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) for individuals with a broad spectrum of speech output impairments.

Results

Overview of experiments

Nine participants (S1–S9) were recruited in this study (Extended Data Table 1 provides demographics and clinical information). All participants received awake surgeries for the treatment of brain tumors. During the awake surgery, the participants performed the SA–SI contrast task while simultaneous audio and 256-channel high-density ECoG recordings were obtained. Among the participants, five (S1–S5) completed Paradigm 1 (Fig. 1a), which involved 60 repetitions of articulation and silent imagery of six syllables, each initiated by an ipsilateral hand key press. To control for any potential influence of hand motor responses, 40 repetitions of ipsilateral key presses were performed. Electrodes responsive solely to these key presses were excluded from further analyses. The remaining four participants (S6–S9) completed Paradigm 2 (Fig. 1b), which consisted of 45 repetitions of articulation and imagery tasks across eight syllables, each initiated by the appearance of a black cross symbol. To ensure the complete absence of motor and auditory output during speech imagery tasks, we implemented preoperative training combined with continuous intraoperative video/audio surveillance and electromyographic (EMG) monitoring. Distinct spectral signatures govern AKT encoding across SA and SI modalities

We first analyzed the neural representation of the AKTs during the SA and SI tasks. Using an established speaker-independent acoustic-articulatory inversion (AAI) algorithm19, we derived 13-dimensional AKTs from synchronized audio recordings during SA (Fig. 1c,d). These trajectories captured (1) two-dimensional movements (x, y) of six articulators (upper/lower lips, jaw and tongue tip/body/dorsum); and (2) one-dimensional laryngeal activity (scaled log F0)19. For SI tokens, we used the corresponding SA-derived AKTs as internal kinematic proxies.

Subsequently, we computed the analytic amplitude of the signals from the local field potential across six frequency bands: θ (4–8 Hz), α (8–12 Hz), β1 (12–24 Hz), β2 (24–40 Hz), low γ (40–70 Hz) and high γ (70–150 Hz)12,16. This analysis encompassed 1,831 electrodes distributed across our anatomically defined regions of interest (ROIs) (434 over precentral gyrus (PRCG), 419 over postcentral gyrus (POCG), 126 over pars opercularis (POP), 71 over pars triangularis (PTRI) of Broca’s area, 324 over middle frontal gyrus (MFG) and 457 over SMG) in nine participants (Fig. 1e,f). The β1 band showed maximal modality responsiveness (SA, 55.2%; SI, 33.8% of electrodes), exceeding other bands. In contrast, high γ activity exhibited markedly lower engagement, with 38.2% of electrodes responsive during SA and only 7.1% during SI (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b).

To quantify how AKTs are represented in cortical activity, we modeled the relationship between time-varying AKTs and neural signals using a time-delayed ridge regression (TRF model)19,25,26. This approach revealed notable modality-dependent encoding profiles: SA encoding peaked in high γ band (median R2: 0.034, interquartile range (IQR) 0.014–0.087; all P < 0.01 versus other bands, Mann–Whitney U-tests, Benjamini–Hochberg corrected), with secondary β1 band representation (median R2: 0.031, IQR: 0.011–0.069; P  < 0.0001 versus θ, α and low γ bands, P  = 0.2409 versus β2) (Fig. 1g); however, no significant difference in SA–AKT encoding strength was observed for electrodes jointly responsive in the β1 and high γ bands (P  = 0.9620; Fig. 1h). In contrast, SI encoding was dominated by β1 activity (median R2 = 0.012, IQR = 0.005–0.032; all P  < 0.01 versus other bands; Fig. 1i), with weaker high γ representation (median R2 = 0.008, IQR = 0.003–0.022). Notably, in electrodes responsive to both bands, SI–AKT encoding was significantly stronger in β1 than in high γ (P  = 0.0012; Fig. 1j). This spectral dissociation persisted across all frontoparietal regions (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d) and was further supported by ablation analyses of frequency band contributions (Supplementary Fig. 1), suggesting fundamental differences in how internal simulation versus motor execution engages the speech network.

Frontoparietal β1 activity underpins shared and specific AKT encoding in SA and SI modalities

Given our findings on the frequency band encoding preferences, along with extensive previous research on high γ encoding in overt speech10,11,12,16,18,19, our analysis focuses on the β1 band to gain insights into speech imagery. We found that 62.2% of the 1,182 responsive electrodes were responsive exclusively to SA (47.7%) or to SI (14.5%) in the β1 band, and 37.8% of all responsive electrodes responded to both modalities (Fig. 2a,b). Anatomical distributions differed significantly (P  < 0.0001, χ2 test), with dual-responsive electrodes most prevalent in PRCG (42.4%), followed by POCG (29.8%), MFG (15.1%) and SMG (14.9%) (Fig. 2a).

Applying stringent selection criteria (significant encoding defined as P  < 0.05, permutation test, Benjamini–Hochberg corrected), we identified functionally distinct neural populations exhibiting differential encoding of AKTs across speech modalities. In unimodal encoding electrodes, SA-specific electrodes exhibited β1 activity that tracked overt articulatory movements, whereas SI-specific electrodes showed significant encoding of internal articulatory dynamics despite the absence of overt movement. Example electrodes illustrating these patterns are shown in Extended Data Fig. 2a–f,n.

Among dual-responsive electrodes, unsupervised clustering analysis (Duda–Hart statistic: d > 1.645, P  < 0.05; silhouette score-optimized) identified two functionally segregated populations within the R2 value distribution space (SA–AKT versus SI–AKT models, Fig. 2c). The first cluster (Cluster 1, pink dots in Fig. 2c,d) exhibited a strong positive correlation between SA–AKT and SI–AKT encoding (Spearman’s r = 0.56, P  < 0.0001), suggesting shared articulatory representations across modalities. In contrast, the second cluster (Cluster 2, light green dots in Fig. 2c) showed significantly reduced SI–AKT encoding relative to SA (median R2 (IQR) 0.149 (0.107–0.187) versus 0.022 (0.012–0.033), P  < 0.0001; Fig. 2e), reflecting modality-specific encoding. Example electrodes illustrating these patterns are provided in Extended Data Fig. 2g–n.

To further characterize these populations, we quantified the unique contribution (ΔR2) of individual articulators to the full AKT model across electrodes. Cluster 1 maintained robust cross-modal consistency between SA and SI (cosine similarity = 0.32, P  < 0.0001, Fig. 2f,g,j). This stability suggests these neural ensembles participate in a supramodal speech planning network that transcends production modality. Conversely, modality-specific populations (comprising both Cluster 2 and unimodal encoding electrodes) did not exhibit significant similarity between SA and SI encoding patterns (cosine similarity = 0.14, P  = 0.2310, Fig. 2h,i,k). This modality-specific specialization likely reflects microanatomical segregation of functionally distinct neural subpopulations within the same cortical territory, with differential engagement based on behavioral state27.

Our results suggest that speech imagery constitutes an active cognitive process involving detailed internal articulatory representations. While partial overlap with speech articulation (Cluster 1) confirms shared planning mechanisms2,3,5,6, the discovery of modality-specific neural signatures (particularly SI-exclusive AKT encoding and Cluster 2’s functional switching) indicates unique computational properties governing imagined speech. Identifying these internal kinematic codes provides a neurophysiological basis for decoding imagined speech.

Compared with the β1 band, high γ activity primarily reflects modality-specific, SA-dominant articulatory encoding, with limited evidence for supramodal organization (Extended Data Fig. 3). Individual-level spatial distributions of shared and modality-specific responsive electrodes, together with activation maps across SA and SI, further illustrate these modality-dependent patterns in β1 and high γ activity (Extended Data Figs. 4–6).

Distinct spatiotemporal dynamics of supramodal and modality-specific articulatory representations

SA-specific encoding electrodes (Fig. 3a,f) and SI-specific encoding electrodes (Fig. 3c,i) were concentrated bilaterally around the central sulcus, predominantly covering the ventral primary sensorimotor cortex (areas 4, 3b, 1 and 2, according to a multimodal parcellation atlas28). In contrast, β1-based supramodal (SA–SI shared encoding) electrodes (Fig. 3b) exhibited three distributed clusters (Fig. 3b): (1) middle premotor cortex (mPMC; spanning areas 55b and dorsal 6v with peak density in dorsal 6v); (2) subcentral gyrus (subCG; encompassing areas 43 and ventral 6v centered on area 43); and (3) postcentral–supramarginal junction (POCG–SMG; covering areas OP4, PFop and PF with focal concentration in OP4). These three peaks of supramodal electrodes were positioned anterosuperior, anteroinferior, and posteroinferior relative to those of SA-specific and SI-specific electrodes (Fig. 3e,h), exhibiting significant spatial displacement along the y axis (two-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test: P  = 0.018 versus SA-specific; P  = 0.038 versus SI-specific) and along the z axis relative to SA-specific electrodes (P  = 0.021) (Fig. 3e–j).

To examine the lead–lag dynamics between supramodal and modality-specific clusters, we computed cross-correlations across simultaneously recorded electrode pairs within each participant. Significant electrode-to-electrode couplings were aggregated to yield cluster-level estimates of mean lag and mean maximal cross-correlation strength (|r|) (Fig. 3k–p). Shared encoding clusters exhibited cross-modally stable lead–lag dynamics from POCG–SMG to mPMC and subCG, with no significant cross-modal differences (P  > 0.05, Fig. 3k,m,n,p). Such directed coupling likely reflects communication along the arcuate and superior longitudinal fasciculus-III pathways, consistent with established speech models and previous stimulation-based mappings of speech planning and verbal working memory5,29,30. In addition, subCG showed temporal precedence over mPMC, possibly reflecting communication via short-range cortico-cortical pathways.

During SA modality, lead–lag relationships were organized such that supramodal clusters (mPMC, subCG and POCG–SMG) tended to precede SA-specific populations in the ventral sensorimotor cortex (VSMC), consistent with a top-down organization from supramodal planning to articulatory execution (Fig. 3m). In SI modality, the same supramodal clusters also exhibit preceding activity relative to SI-specific populations within the VSMC (Fig. 3p), which may support internal articulatory representations and cognitive speech simulation that evokes quasi-perceptual experience2.

Supramodal integration versus modality-specific somatotopic organization of articulators

Subsequently, we aimed to determine the somatotopic arrangements across articulators for SA–SI shared, SA-specific and SI-specific encoding electrodes, respectively. To this end, we constructed weighted functional maps for each articulator (lips, tongue, jaw and larynx) in the y–z plane of MNI space, using each articulator’s unique contribution (ΔR2) as the weighting factor in the KDE to visualize the spatial encoding patterns for the three categories of electrodes.

Our spatial analysis revealed fundamentally distinct organizational principles between supramodal and modality-specific articulatory representations. The distribution of articulator weights across shared encoding electrodes did not differ significantly between modalities (permutation test, Fig. 4a,b,e); instead, they exhibited significant cross-modal similarity (cosine similarity = 0.31, P  < 0.0001; Extended Data Fig. 7g). Within each modality, most articulator pairs did not show statistically significant differences in spatial weight distributions (Fig. 4a,b,e). Notably, spatially overlapping functional peaks encoding multiple articulators were observed across three key regions (the mPMC, subCG and POCG–SMG; Fig. 4a,b,e and Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). This conserved spatial patterning across both speech modalities suggests an integrative role for these supramodal regions in articulatory planning6,31,32,33, an interpretation that aligns with causal evidence from direct cortical stimulation, where stimulation of these regions produces global speech arrest instead of isolated articulator responses30,32,34.

In contrast, modality-specific encoding electrodes exhibited distinct and dissociable somatotopic arrangements across modalities (all articulators, Bonferroni-corrected P  < 0.01 between modalities, permutation tests; Fig. 4c,d,f, and Extended Data Fig. 7h for cosine similarity analysis). During overt speech articulation, SA-specific electrodes showed a well-defined ventral–dorsal somatotopic gradient within the VSMC, featuring dual laryngeal representations at the ventral and dorsal extremes (Fig. 4c). The ventral laryngeal representation is widely considered the human homolog of the laryngeal motor area identified in nonhuman primates, whereas the dorsal representation likely reflects a human-specific, experience-dependent expansion supporting fine-grained laryngeal control for speech production35. Between dual laryngeal peaks, tongue, lip and jaw articulators were organized in a sequential dorsal-to-ventral topography (all pairwise Bonferroni-corrected P  < 0.05; Fig. 4c,f and Extended Data Fig. 7c), similar to previously reported somatotopic organizations during speech production18,33.

By contrast, SI-specific encoding electrodes exhibited a weak and spatially diffuse organization. Pairwise comparisons revealed that only tongue and larynx representations survived correction for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni-corrected P  < 0.05), whereas all other articulator pairs failed to reach significance (Fig. 4d,f and Extended Data Fig. 7d). This attenuated somatotopic structure may be influenced by the sparse representation of SI-specific electrodes (Fig. 3i). In fact, SI-specific electrodes were often interspersed among SA-specific populations, rather than forming a dedicated, macroscopic somatotopic map.

Together, these results support an architecture organized along a gradient of integrative complexity: supramodal frontoparietal nodes support integrated, modality-invariant speech planning, whereas sensorimotor populations encode articulator-specific representations, preserving clear somatotopy during overt articulation but adopting a sparser, reweighted organization during internal simulation (see Extended Data Fig. 7e,f for a joint visualization of articulator peak distributions and corresponding cortical parcellations).

The syllable classifier achieved high decoding accuracy across β1-based SA and SI encoding electrodes, with comparable performance (SI, median accuracy = 0.804, IQR 0.590–0.808; SA, 0.783, IQR 0.633–0.833; P  = 0.3008, Wilcoxon signed-rank test). Crucially, decoding in the SI modality was significantly above chance (median chance = 0.167, P  = 0.0003, Mann–Whitney U-test), indicating that internal articulatory representations are sufficiently stable to support categorical decoding (Fig. 5i). Across modalities, β1-based neural decoding based on encoding electrodes significantly outperformed decoding using all 256 electrodes (Extended Data Fig. 8b,c). Moreover, downsampling the electrode set to 25% (approximating a 32-channel clinical grid) led to marked performance degradation, with median accuracy reductions of 0.116 in SA and 0.212 in SI (Extended Data Fig. 8a–c), underscoring the importance of high-density coverage for achieving precise neural speech decoding. Finally, classification accuracy increased monotonically with the proportion of tokens used (20–100% of 360 tokens per participant; Extended Data Fig. 8d), indicating that performance in both modalities has not yet reached plateau.

Given that no audio is generated in the SI modality (as shown in Fig. 5f for covertly reading /ba/), we used the audio recorded in the SA modality for the same trial within the same block as the shared ground truth (GT) (see Fig. 5e for overtly reading /ba/) for both the SA and SI modalities to train the speech synthesizer. The MCD between the synthesized and real speech spectrograms, computed using β1-based SA and SI encoding electrodes, was significantly below the 8 dB threshold (SA, median 3.25 dB, IQR 3.13–3.47 dB; SI, median 3.61 dB, IQR 3.24–3.83 dB; both P  = 0.0039, Fig. 5j), which is considered acceptable for voice recognition systems21. SI decoding achieved speech synthesis quality comparable to SA (P  = 0.5703), supporting the viability of mental imagery for high-fidelity output (Fig. 5j; 5g,h shows the synthesized /ba/ Mel spectrograms for both modalities).

To provide a perceptually interpretable evaluation of speech synthesis quality, we conducted a forced-choice intelligibility test and a MOS assessment21, in which five listeners identified perceived syllables and rated perceptual quality for GT (the original recorded sound from the SA task) and for speech synthesized from the SA and SI conditions. To limit dataset size, five repetitions were randomly selected for each participant, condition and syllable; MCD values from the sampled data closely matched those from the full dataset for both SA and SI (Wilcoxon tests; SI, P  = 0.4961; SA, P  = 0.0742), confirming the representativeness of the sampling procedure. Intelligibility accuracy was comparable across modalities, with median accuracies of 0.68 (IQR 0.60–0.68) for SI, 0.67 (IQR 0.62–0.70) for SA, and 0.65 (IQR 0.63–0.72) for GT, with no significant difference among three conditions (Kruskal–Wallis test, P  = 0.7993; Extended Data Fig. 8j). MOS ratings were similarly high across modalities (GT, median 3.84, IQR 3.76–4.21; SA, median 3.88, IQR 3.77–3.93; and SI, median 3.90, IQR 3.81–3.92), with no significant difference among conditions (Kruskal–Wallis test, P  = 0.8135; Extended Data Fig. 8i). To further assess perceptual structure beyond scalar measures, we computed confusion matrices for GT, SA and SI conditions and quantified their similarity. Pairwise Spearman rank correlations revealed highly similar confusability patterns across modalities (r = 0.87–0.90, all P  < 0.0001; Extended Data Fig. 8k), indicating that synthesized speech preserved the perceptual structure of natural speech and exhibited consistent error patterns across overt and imagined speech synthesis.

Similarly, using inferred articulatory trajectories from SA trials as the GT, we decoded AKTs from β1 activity using all SI encoding electrodes (Fig. 5k–m). Synthesized trajectories in the SI modality showed strong correlations with GT (median r = 0.60, IQR 0.52–0.67), slightly lower than those observed in SA (0.67, IQR 0.63–0.71; P = 0.0195), yet both significantly exceeded chance levels (P  = 0.0004) (Fig. 5n). Similar decoding performance (median r = 0.62, IQR 0.52–0.68) was obtained when restricting analyses to SI-specific encoding electrodes (Extended Data Fig. 8e), reinforcing that SI-related β1 activity provides a reliable substrate for encoding internal articulatory dynamics.

As a result, deep neural network models trained and tested within each task yielded robust decoding efficacy for SA and SI tasks. Next, we investigated whether crossmodal articulatory movement decoding could be achieved using SA–SI shared electrodes. We trained four articulatory movement synthesizers based on these electrodes: SA2SA, SA2SI, SI2SI and SI2SA (naming format indicates ‘training set to testing set,’ for example, SA2SI represents training with SA modality β1 activity and testing with SI modality β1 activity). To directly assess the fidelity of crossmodal decoding, we also computed a crossmodal generalization index for each participant, defined as the ratio between the product of crossmodal correlations and that of within-modal correlations: \(({r}_{{\rm{SA}}2{\rm{SI}}}\times {r}_{{\rm{SI}}2{\rm{SA}}})/({r}_{{\rm{SA}}2{\rm{SA}}}\times {r}_{{\rm{SI}}2{\rm{SI}}})\). Within β1 shared encoding electrodes, decoding accuracy was comparable across both within- and cross-modal conditions (P  = 0.6606), with all models significantly above chance (all P  = 0.0004) (Fig. 5o). These results further support that SA–SI shared electrodes encode supramodal articulatory representations, offering a principled basis and implantation target for cross-modal speech BCIs. Notably, in the β1 band, shared encoding electrodes exhibited a median crossmodal generalization index of 0.934 (IQR 0.865–0.984), significantly higher than that of β1 modality-specific electrodes (0.884 (0.835–0.964), P  = 0.0391) and high γ modality-specific electrodes (0.611 (0.534–0.701), P  = 0.0039) (Extended Data Fig. 9d).

We next aimed to validate whether our articulatory movement synthesizer had effectively learned the stable relationship between β1 activity and AKTs. If this were the case, the synthesizer should exhibit extrapolative generalization, meaning it should be capable of predicting the movement trajectories of syllables not included in the training set. To assess this, we employed a leave-one-syllable-out (L1SO) strategy, iteratively excluding one syllable, training the synthesizer on the remaining syllables, and testing the excluded syllable to compute the correlation (r) between predicted AKTs and GT. We found that the L1SO articulatory movement synthesizer still performed well for all syllables in both SA and SI encoding electrodes, with the median correlation coefficient for all syllables significantly exceeding chance levels (P  < 0.05 for all syllables) (Fig. 5p; see Fig. 5l,m for synthesized articulatory movements of /ba/ in SA and SI modalities using L1SO strategy). In summary, the L1SO model demonstrated strong performance in both the SA (median r = 0.66, IQR 0.52–0.70) and SI (median r = 0.60, IQR 0.49–0.64) modalities (all P  = 0.0004 versus chance level, Fig. 5q). To further assess the robustness of extrapolation, we systematically increased the number of syllables excluded from training, leaving out two or three syllables (L2SO and L3SO, respectively). Decoding performance was compared across L1SO, L2SO and L3SO models derived from the same syllable set (/ba/, /bu/, /da/, /du/, /ga/, /gu/, /pa/, /ta/ and /ka/) to control for stimulus composition. Median correlation was comparable between L1SO and L2SO but modestly declined when three syllables were excluded (L3SO) in both modalities (P  < 0.01, Extended Data Fig. 8g,h), yet remained robustly above chance (all P  < 0.05, Extended Data Fig. 8f). Collectively, our articulatory movement synthesizer demonstrated robust extrapolative generalization across both modalities, indicating that it successfully captured a neurocomputationally interpretable mapping between cortical signatures and intended articulatory dynamics.

Moreover, this triple-stream decoding framework was also applicable to multi-electrode high γ decoding (Extended Data Fig. 9), enabling a direct comparison between high γ and β1 bands. For speech synthesis, β1 yielded significantly higher quality than high γ in the SI modality (median MCD (IQR) β1, 3.612 (3.244–3.829); high γ, 3.639 (3.326–3.992); P  = 0.0117, Extended Data Fig. 9b). In contrast, for AKT synthesis during SA, β1 performance was significantly lower than high γ (median r (IQR) β1, 0.674 (0.625–0.714); high γ, 0.854 (0.691–0.886); P  = 0.0039, Extended Data Fig. 9c). No significant differences were observed for the remaining decoding streams or modalities (Extended Data Fig. 9a–c). Overall, these results recapitulate the frequency band preferences observed at the single-electrode level, although such differences were attenuated in the multi-electrode setting. As noted above, crossmodal decoding using high γ remained poor, likely reflecting the paucity of shared encoding patterns (Extended Data Fig. 9d). By contrast, extrapolation to unseen syllables remained robust when using high γ modality-specific encoding electrodes (Extended Data Fig. 9e–h).

Discussion

Our high-density ECoG recordings reveal shared and modality-specific mechanisms for articulatory control in speech imagery and articulation. Three key advances emerge: first, frontoparietal AKT encoding exhibits a spectral dissociation, with high γ dominance and secondary β1 contributions in SA, contrasting with a primary β1 signature in SI. Second, β1-band analysis identifies spatiotemporally stable supramodal populations in mPMC, subCG and POCG–SMG that support integrated multi-articulatory planning. These populations show distinct spatiotemporal dynamics relative to somatotopically interleaved primary sensorimotor populations, segregating internal simulation during speech imagery from external execution during articulation. Third, neural decoding built upon these dynamics enables robust within-modal classification, speech synthesis and kinematic prediction, as well as extrapolative decoding of unseen syllables using SA and SI encoding electrodes, while also supporting crossmodal generalization in shared encoding electrodes.

Distinct spectral signatures across speech modalities

Our findings reveal a fundamental dissociation in spectral encoding strategies between speech imagery and articulation. High γ activity emerges as the dominant carrier of AKT information during overt speech articulation, particularly in frontoparietal regions, consistent with its established role in encoding fine-grained articulatory features, including AKT sequences, place of articulation and phonetic details10,11,12,16,18,19,36. High γ responses are widely interpreted as reflecting local population spiking, tightly coupled to neuronal firing rates and execution-related motor output representations37. Accordingly, high γ engagement scales with overt articulator movement and remains spatially constrained, and although residual high γ activity persists during speech imagery, its functional engagement is markedly attenuated10,11,12,36, both in cortical recruitment and in encoding strength.

In contrast, the significant β1 dominance observed during SI–AKT encoding suggests a distinct circuit regime. Transient β-band activity is thought to arise from two complementary sources: local modulation of neuronal spiking and mesoscopic network-level dynamics associated with top-down control38. This physiological profile provides a parsimonious explanation for the broader spatial distribution of β1 responses relative to high γ across frontoparietal cortex in both speech modalities. Notably, the β1 band not only achieves secondary encoding performance during articulation (surpassed only by high γ) but also emerges as the principal information carrier during imagery throughout frontoparietal regions.

This finding aligns with a well-established electrophysiological literature demonstrating that β-band event-related (de)synchronization (ERD/ERS) is a robust signature in a range of endogenously generated and overt motor processes, including motor planning and execution, action simulation and imagery, and postural maintenance, supporting its role as a modality-spanning substrate for internal and overt motor representations36,39,40,41,42,43,44,45. Extending this framework from limb motor control to speech, previous EEG, ECoG and MEG studies have demonstrated β-band activity during covert speech production, exhibiting partially overlapping yet dissociable spatiotemporal profiles relative to overt articulation45,46,47.

Building on these previous findings, our direct, high-resolution recordings with broad cortical coverage, combined with TRF modeling, enable a quantitative dissociation of shared and modality-specific β1 encoding patterns and their spatiotemporal organization across cortex. Together, these observations suggest that β1 activity may provide a multiscale window to capture network-level aspects of speech production and imagery within a unified physiological framework.

An expanded somato-cognitive architecture for speech imagery and articulation

Recent work has reconceptualized the sensorimotor cortex as a somato-cognitive action network (SCAN), comprising interdigitated effector-specific zones and integrative inter-effector regions33,48. By focusing on β1 activity, our findings extend this ‘new homunculus’ framework by revealing functional distinctions between supramodal and modality-specific populations involved in speech planning, internal simulation and external execution. Comparable organizational motifs have been described in limb motor control, where overlapping populations preserve shared planning dynamics while modality-specific subsets flexibly reconfigure according to the demands of imagery or execution49,50. Notably, this distributed organization aligns with the core architectural principles of contemporary speech production models, such as Directions Into Velocities of Articulators (DIVA), State Feedback Control, dual-stream and dynamic hodotopical frameworks3,5,6,51,52, while simultaneously providing a neurophysiological substrate for speech imagery accounts derived from these production architectures2,8.

In addition to previously described inter-effector middle premotor and subcentral hubs33,48, we identify a key parietal node at the POCG–SMG junction that, together with frontal counterparts, forms an extended frontoparietal network for articulatory planning. Within this network, activity in the parietal node precedes that in the mPMC and subCG. This organization accords with speech motor control frameworks in which parietal node contributes to the generation of articulatory intentions that are subsequently transformed into motor programs within frontal planning areas (‘speech sound map’ posited in the DIVA model)3,7,51.

Functionally, our data reveal that supramodal populations within these hubs exhibit three key features: robust cross-modal stability across spatiotemporal profiles and AKT encoding patterns; spatially overlapping encoding of multiple articulatory gestures, supporting integrative control; and consistent temporal precedence over somatotopically organized, modality-specific populations. Consistent with this interpretation, direct cortical stimulation studies show that transient perturbation of these supramodal hubs reliably induces complete speech arrest rather than selective articulator-specific responses, implicating these regions in coordinated multi-articulatory planning rather than isolated motor output30,32,34.

Converging network-level evidence comes from fMRI studies showing strong connectivity between these hubs and the cingulo-opercular network (CON), a domain-general system implicated in goal-directed action and performance monitoring33. This is complemented by anatomical studies showing their tight coupling via the superior longitudinal fasciculus-III/arcuate fasciculus complex, critical tracts within the dorsal phonological stream5,28,30,53. Stimulation of these fiber tracts likewise disrupts speech, resulting in phonemic paraphasia or complete speech arrest29. Consistent with their integrative role, Utah array recordings have further shown that limited coverage within these hubs suffices for decoding both articulated and imagined speech, even during naturalistic speech involving complex multi-articulator coordination17,23,24. Together, these findings support the interpretation that supramodal populations encode cognitive-level articulatory plans generalizable across modalities2.

In contrast, modality-specific populations show distinct anatomical and functional profiles. Predominantly localized to the primary sensorimotor cortex along the central sulcus, SA- and SI-specific populations are interleaved in a mosaic-like distribution that, despite spatial overlap, diverges in crossmodal encoding patterns. SA-specific populations display a characteristic somatotopic organization, with laryngeal peaks at the dorsal and ventral extremes of VSMC, aligned with laryngeal motor regions identified in previous ECoG and fMRI studies18,35. Cortical stimulation at these vocalic sites induces involuntary vocalizations, pitch change, laryngeal constriction or paralysis, providing causal evidence for their functional specificity20,34,48. Temporally, supramodal activity consistently precedes SA-specific activation, echoing the leading–lagging relationship between phonetic encoding and articulatory output observed in large-scale neuroimaging meta-analyses54,55.

In contrast, SI-specific electrodes exhibited a remapped functional organization with attenuated somatotopic segregation. This reduced somatotopic differentiation likely stems from their sparsely distributed and spatially dispersed organization. This may also explain the limited detection of SI-specific activity in primary sensorimotor cortex in previous fMRI studies56,57. The dominant motor signal during articulation likely masks more subtle SI-related activations in cross-modal contrasts.

Notably, our high-density ECoG recordings identified distinct populations that exclusively encode internal AKTs during speech imagery, providing direct neurophysiological evidence for cognitive-level articulatory outputs. This SI-specific activity observed here is consistent with the concept of ‘residual neural activity’ proposed in previous theoretical and empirical work on speech imagery8,9,58. Within this framework, internally generated articulatory plans formed in higher-order planning regions are forwarded to sensorimotor circuits as efference copies. When external somatosensory feedback is absent, these internal forward estimates persist as ‘residual neural activity’, which may underlie the kinesthetic, quasi-perceptual experience associated with imagined speech8,9. At the same time, they may suppress overt execution via local inhibitory interactions with neighboring SA-specific circuits, enabling mental speech simulation without triggering actual articulation3,6,8,9,52,59.

Together, these findings extend the SCAN framework to a broader frontoparietal architecture linking overt articulation and covert imagery, in which supramodal articulatory planning systems interface with modality-specific execution circuits to form a unified somato-cognitive map of speech control.

Implications for neurally interpretable within- and crossmodal speech BCIs

Our encoding results establish the basis for a biologically grounded framework for decoding both spoken and imagined speech. Despite the constraints of a limited ECoG dataset, we developed a triple-stream architecture that accurately classified syllables, synthesized acoustic output, and predicted AKTs by leveraging frontoparietal β1 activity, an underutilized neural signal in previous BCI efforts. Within each modality, decoding performance was consistent with previous ECoG studies that employed datasets of comparable duration and similarly constrained syllable- or phoneme-level tasks11,12,14,23,24,60. Performance in the speech imagery condition had not yet reached a clear plateau (Extended Data Fig. 8d). With larger datasets and the incorporation of language-model-derived constraints, future extensions of this framework may further improve decoding performance16,17.

A central finding of this work is the identification of articulatory representations that are shared across speech articulation and imagery within the frontoparietal cortex. These supramodal representations exhibited stable encoding across modalities, enabling crossmodal generalization with prediction consistency approaching within-modality performance. This degree of crossmodal transferability suggests a shared speech planning architecture, capable of integrating both overt articulation and covert speech imagery. Such systems may be particularly valuable for individuals with progressive speech impairment, including those affected by ALS. Critically, the localization of these supramodal representations to the middle premotor cortex, subcentral gyrus and POCG–SMG junction provides anatomically and functionally grounded targets for future crossmodal BCI implantation. More broadly, these findings suggest that future speech BCIs may benefit from engaging higher-order speech planning nodes (for example, the SMG23) and bypassing downstream corticobulbar pathways. Such an architecture could, in principle, extend beyond brainstem or lower tract dysfunction to encompass cortical impairments such as Broca’s aphasia, thereby broadening the clinical applicability of speech BCIs.

Furthermore, the model generalized effectively to untrained syllables, demonstrating the utility of AKTs as a stable and interpretable intermediary between neural activity and phonemic categories. This property enhances decoding transparency and provides a proof-of-principle demonstration of compositional structure at the articulatory level, which could serve as a conceptual stepping stone toward future studies of open-vocabulary or naturalistic speech decoding16,17.

In conclusion, these results highlight a principled framework for within-modal and cross-modal speech BCIs across speech imagery and articulation. By dissociating supramodal and modality-specific articulatory representations, this work highlights how biologically interpretable speech planning signals may support flexible decoding across modalities. Such an approach may inform the design of future neural prosthetic systems, with potential relevance for speech restoration across a broad spectrum of speech output impairments, including locked-in syndrome, Broca’s aphasia and progressive conditions such as ALS11,12,13,24.

Limitations

There are several limitations that remain to be addressed for future works. First, although high-density ECoG provides excellent spatial and temporal resolution, it lacks the single-neuronal specificity required to resolve fine-grained encoding differences among closely adjacent but functionally distinct neuronal populations. Second, the decoding framework was evaluated under controlled conditions with limited syllabic diversity and trial numbers, and its generalizability to larger syllable repertoires and continuous speech remains to be established. Moreover, the GT audio may have been contaminated by intraoperative background noise, which could introduce confounding factors that compromise the perceptual evaluation of synthesized speech. Future validation in chronic, long-term implanted patients will be essential for robust assessment of decoding performance. Third, although we identify discriminable motor-related representations during SI, these findings do not exclude the concurrent involvement of higher-level abstract linguistic representations beyond the scope of the present analyses. Fourth, EMG recordings were available for only a subset of participants (participants S6–S9), limiting our ability to systematically assess subthreshold articulatory muscle activity across the full cohort. Finally, although our analyses focused on frequency-specific analytic amplitude to enable systematic cross-band comparisons, this approach may not fully capture the temporal structure of low-frequency neural activity. Future studies incorporating phase-resolved measures and transient burst analyses may provide complementary insights into neural encoding and large-scale control processes.

Methods

Participants

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki and other relevant ethical regulations, and approved by the Huashan Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Fudan University (KY2019-538 and KY2022-504) and the Institutional Review Board of ShanghaiTech University (Q2024-001). A total of nine participants from Huashan Hospital participated in this study (Extended Data Table 1). All participants (median age 35 years (IQR 32–51); four males, five females; nine left hemisphere) were eloquent brain tumor patients undergoing awake language mapping as part of their surgery. During the intraoperative language mapping, high-density electrode grids were temporarily placed onto the frontoparietal cortex to record local field potentials from the cortex, and the participants were instructed to perform the experimental tasks.

Participants were asked to participate in the research study only if they were undergoing awake surgery with direct cortical stimulation as part of the regular clinical routine, meaning that this was deemed necessary for the safe resection of their tumor. The ECoG data were acquired during the clinically indicated surgical procedure, adding no extra cost or postoperative follow-up. Consequently, no financial compensation was provided for study participation. Each participant was consented before the surgery, at which time it was explained in a transparent manner (as detailed in the IRB-approved written protocol/consent document) that the research task was for scientific purposes and would not directly impact their care. It was clearly articulated to each participant that participation in the research task was completely voluntary. All participants gave their written informed consent before testing.

Experiment paradigm

Two distinct paradigms were employed in this study. Five participants (S1–S5) completed Paradigm 1, and four participants (S6–S9) completed Paradigm 2 (Fig. 1a,b). Both paradigms were presented using E-Prime 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools) and were temporally synchronized with ECoG and audio recordings. In Paradigm 1, each trial involved alternating SA and SI blocks with six syllables (/ba/, /da/, /ga/, /bu/, /du/ and /gu/) repeated 60 times per task, plus 40 ipsilateral key press trials as a motor control. Paradigm 2 included auditory cues and eight syllables (/ba/, /da/, /ga/, /pa/, /ta/, /ka/, /sa/ and /sha/) repeated 45 times per task. The ipsilateral key press (Paradigm 1) or the black cross go-cue (Paradigm 2) served as the reference time point for subsequent neural, audio and feature matrix segmentation. For detailed trial structures, see Fig. 1a,b legends.

Four methodological controls were implemented to eliminate potential articulatory muscle activation during the speech imagery task: (1) preoperative training with explicit instruction to avoid articulatory movements or voicing; (2) real-time visual monitoring by a researcher (Z.Z.) during training with immediate correction; (3) intraoperative video/audio surveillance using the Brain Mapping Interactive Stimulation System (Shenzhen Sinorad Medical Electronics Co.)61; and (4) intraoperative EMG recordings from orofacial muscles (for example, orbicularis oris and mylohyoid) for participants S6–S9 to ensure that the included SI trials exhibited no detectable EMG activity exceeding the predefined threshold.

ECoG data acquisition and preprocessing

Neural signals were recorded from two 128-channel ECoG grids (8 × 16, 3 or 4-mm spacing) using a multichannel amplifier (Tucker–Davis Technologies), sampled at 3,052 Hz. Channels with undetectable signal or continuous epileptiform activity were excluded; time segments with artifacts were manually removed. Remaining signals were notch-filtered at 50, 100 and 150 Hz.

To extract frequency-specific activity, band-pass filtering isolated six bands (θ, 4–8 Hz; α, 8–12 Hz; β1, 12–24 Hz; β2, 24–40 Hz; Lγ, 40–70 Hz and Hγ, 70–150 Hz)12,25. The analytic amplitude (envelope) of each band was computed using the Hilbert transform and smoothed using a Gaussian filter. The signal for each frequency band was obtained by averaging the analytic amplitude across eight logarithmically spaced sub-bands within the specified frequency range25. Finally, the signal was downsampled to 100 Hz and z-scored using the entire recording block for normalization22.

Cortical surface extraction and electrode localization

Pial surface reconstructions were generated from preoperative T1-weighted MRI using FreeSurfer. Electrode positions were localized by co-registering intraoperatively recorded grid corner coordinates (Medtronic neuronavigation) and photographs to preoperative MRI; remaining electrodes were interpolated using the img_pipe package62. Electrode positions in individual spaces were normalized to MNI 152 (2009, asymmetric) template, and anatomically labeled using Desikan–Killiany63 and HCP-MMP atlases28.

Responsive electrode selection

After the neural activity computation in each frequency band, response electrodes for both SA and SI were identified. The average time difference between syllable onset in the audio (SA task) and the reference time point (ipsilateral key press in Paradigm 1 or the go-cue appearance in Paradigm 2) was calculated. This value was then used to align the ECoG data to the syllable onset in the SA modality or internal syllable onset in the SI modality (set as 0 ms), followed by segmentation and averaging across tasks.

The neural activity between –500 ms and –400 ms served as baseline resting-state activity. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Bonferroni correction, α = 0.05/160, where 160 is the number of time points tested) compared average neural activity between baseline and each time point from –400 ms to +1,200 ms. Electrodes with ≥100 ms (ten consecutive time points) of significant difference were deemed responsive. Electrodes responsive to ipsilateral key presses (Paradigm 1 control) were excluded.

Speech feature extraction

We employed a similar Praat parameter extraction approach in line with our previous research64,65. We extracted the pitch contour (log F0, F0 = fundamental frequency) of each syllable from the audio recorded during the SA modality with an autocorrelation method in Praat (https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/praat/, v.6.1.01)66, which was subsequently used to compute the one-dimensional trajectory for the larynx. Additionally, we addressed halving and doubling errors during the extraction process. Individual pitch minimum and maximum values were determined for each participant, and a timestep of 0.01 s was employed. All other parameters adhered to Praat’s default settings.

Speaker-independent acoustic-to-articulatory inversion

Following previous studies19,67, we applied a speaker-independent AAI algorithm to convert synchronized audio recorded during the SA modality into 13-dimensional AKTs for each syllable. These AKTs captured two-dimensional (2D) movements (x, y) of six articulators (upper/lower lip, jaw and tongue tip/body/dorsum) and 1D laryngeal movement (scaled log F0, normalized to –1 to 1 a.u.), with validity established against electromagnetic articulography19. For SI trials, corresponding SA AKTs from the same trial served as internal kinematic proxies. The code is available at https://github.com/articulatory/articulatory.

K-means clustering for encoding electrode classification

For dual-responsive electrodes, we aimed to determine whether β1 (or high γ) neural activity encodes AKTs in a comparable manner across SA and SI modalities. To address this, we first calculated the R2 difference between the SA–AKT and SI–AKT models for each dual-responsive electrode. We applied the Duda–Hart test to evaluate whether the differences surpassed the threshold (Duda–Hart statistic d > 1.645, P  < 0.05), which would indicate the necessity for classification into at least two clusters69. Unsupervised k-means clustering (scikit-learn) was then performed with cluster numbers ranging from 2 to 10, with the optimal number selected by the highest silhouette score70. Within each cluster, crossmodal similarity was assessed via Spearman correlation of R2 values between SA–AKT and SI–AKT models. Differences in performance were assessed using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test (P  < 0.05).

Cosine similarity and hierarchical clustering of encoding patterns

To assess shared encoding patterns, cosine similarity was computed between SA and SI encoding matrices (each entry: articulator-specific ΔR2). Significance was assessed via permutation testing (10,000 shuffles of electrode labels). Hierarchical clustering (Ward’s linkage) was applied to the AKT encoding matrix using SciPy and Seaborn71, with values normalized to 0–1 before clustering.

Spatial distribution of encoding electrodes

To examine spatial distributions of encoding categories (SA–SI shared, SA-specific and SI-specific), KDE with Scott’s bandwidth was applied in MNI y–z space, thresholded at 0.5 cumulative density. Local density peaks were identified using a maximum filter (SciPy). Spatial differences between categories along y and z axes were assessed via two-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests (P  < 0.05).

Similar KDE maps were generated for each articulator across different encoding categories, with electrode positions weighted by their unique contribution (ΔR2) to the full AKT model. To assess separability among articulator representations, a permutation-based distance test was performed: articulator labels were shuffled 5,000 times per electrode group to generate a null distribution of integrated absolute differences between KDE maps. Empirical differences between articulator pairs were compared against this null to compute permutation-derived P values (Bonferroni-corrected, P  < 0.05) and z-scores. Crossmodal comparisons of articulator-weighted KDE distributions used the same framework.

Cross-correlation-based temporal lead–lag dynamics

To examine temporal dynamics between supramodal and modality-specific clusters, cross-correlations were computed between simultaneously recorded electrode pairs within each participant (scipy.signal.correlate, ±400-ms lags)25. Neural signals were first z-scored across time points to remove baseline differences, and cross-correlation was computed over lags of ±400 ms. To assess statistical significance, a Fisher Z-transform was applied to compute the two-tailed P values. Electrode–electrode couplings with Bonferroni-corrected P  < 0.05 were considered significant. Maximum correlation coefficients (absolute r) and corresponding time lags (τ) were extracted for each pair.

Significant electrode–electrode couplings were then aggregated at the cluster level by averaging maximum correlation coefficients and corresponding time lags across all pairs connecting two clusters. This procedure enabled estimation of directed, cluster-level lead–lag dynamics. Network visualizations were generated using the NetworkX (https://networkx.org/), with arrow color representing mean τ (lead–lag timing).

Triple-stream parallel neural decoding framework

To decode speech from β1 or high γ activity, we developed a triple-stream parallel neural decoding framework (Fig. 5a) using the PyTorch and Keras72,73. This framework decodes β1 or high γ activity from SA or SI encoding electrodes into three highly interrelated components: syllable classification (Fig. 5b), audio synthesis (Fig. 5c), and AKT reconstruction (Fig. 5d). As SI generates no audio or motor output, the speech feature matrix from the corresponding SA trial served as the training target. Input data were structured as channels × time steps (channels = encoding electrodes; time window, –0.5 to +1.5 s around onset, 200 time steps at 100 Hz). For AKT synthesis, the first and last four time steps were excluded to avoid edge effects. Each modality contributed ~360 trials total (S1–S5, 6 syllables × 60 repetitions; and S6–S9, 8 syllables × 45 repetitions).

The syllable classifier (Fig. 5b) is an adapted 34-layer Residual Network (ResNet-34) architecture74. The first convolutional layer employed a 7 × 7 kernel, followed by batch normalization, 2D max-pooling, four residual blocks (ResBlocks)75 and 40% dropout. The network was trained to minimize joint cross-entropy loss using the stochastic gradient descent (initial learning rate 0.01, decay 1 × 10⁻6, momentum 0.9, Nesterov acceleration). Training halted when validation loss plateaued. Performance was evaluated as decoding accuracy via tenfold cross-validation.

The speech synthesizer (Fig. 5c) employs a deep convolutional neural network architecture composed of four sequential modules (Conv2D + ResBlock74 + 2D max-pooling, 3 × 3 kernels) and followed by a fully connected layer that synthesizes Mel spectrograms (80 Mel-frequency bins × 130 time frames) from neural activity. The network was trained to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) loss using NAdam optimizer (learning rate 0.0001, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ε = 1 × 10−8, and weight decay = 0.002). Subsequently, the synthesized Mel spectrograms were converted into audio using a pretrained HiFi-GAN vocoder76. For perceptual evaluation, five listeners performed a forced-choice identification task and provided MOS for GT audio and SA/SI synthesized speech. For each participant and syllable, five trials (as defined in Fig. 1a,b) were randomly selected. Within each selected trial, the first utterance or ‘internal utterance’ was used: the audio recorded during the SA condition served as the GT, with corresponding SA/SI neural activity used for synthesis. MCD from sampled data matched full dataset values (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, P  > 0.05). Intelligibility accuracy was defined as the proportion of correctly identified syllables in the forced-choice task. MOS ratings were recorded on a five-point scale, where 5 indicated speech that was effortless to understand, 4 required attention but little effort, 3 required moderate listening effort, 2 required considerable effort, and 1 indicated speech that was unintelligible despite effort21. Group-level comparisons of intelligibility accuracy and MOS ratings across GT, SA and SI conditions were performed using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Confusion matrices were constructed for each condition, with pairwise similarities quantified via Spearman rank correlation.

The articulatory movement (Fig. 5d) synthesizer employs a vector-quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE)78. An encoder (residual + nonlocal blocks) extracted features, which were mapped onto a 256-dimensional articulatory codebook; a decoder (residual + nonlocal blocks) reconstructed 13 × 192 AKTs74. Training minimized a custom loss function, \({L}_{\rm{total}}={L}_{\rm{rec}}+{L}_{\rm{codebook}}\), where Lrec is the L1 reconstruction loss and Lcodebook is the loss for aligning the encoder outputs with the discrete codebook embeddings. Optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer (learning rate 2.25 × 10−5, β1 = 0.5, β2 = 0.9, and ε = 1 × 10−8). Performance was evaluated as mean Pearson’s r across 13-dimensional AKTs via fivefold cross-validation.

To validate the robustness of this triple-stream parallel decoding framework, we conducted tests on both SA and SI modalities in nine participants. Median syllable decoding accuracy was compared against chance levels (1/6 for six-class tasks in participants S1–S5; 1/8 for eight-class tasks in participants S6–S9). Median MCD was compared against the 8-dB threshold, the upper limit considered acceptable for voice recognition systems21. Additionally, median r values were compared against chance levels, defined as the correlation between AKTs synthesized by an untrained, randomly initialized model and actual AKTs.

To assess the contribution of informative neural signals, decoding analyses were performed using either all 256 electrodes (full-256) or only those exhibiting significant AKT encoding (sig. encoding). To evaluate the effect of electrode density, we systematically downsampled the set of significant encoding electrodes to 25% of their original number (downsampled sig. encoding), approximating the spatial coverage of a clinically typical 32-channel ECoG grid (Extended Data Fig. 8a). To assess the effect of frequency band at the multi-electrode level, decoding performance across β1 and high γ encoding electrodes from the three streams was also compared. Additionally, the impact of training dataset size was examined by varying the proportion of available syllable tokens used for model training from 20% to 100% (20–40–60–80–100% of 360 tokens per participant).

To further investigate whether cross-modal articulatory movement decoding could be achieved using SA–SI shared encoding electrodes, we trained four articulatory movement synthesizers: SA2SA, SA2SI, SI2SI and SI2SA (naming format indicates ‘training set to testing set,’ for example, SA2SI denotes training with SA modality neural activity and testing with SI modality neural activity to evaluate the correlation between synthesized and GT movements). For each participant, a crossmodal generalization index was computed as the ratio of the product of cross-modal correlations to the product of within-modal correlations:\(\frac{{r}_{\rm{SA}2{SI}}\times {r}_{\rm{SI}2{SA}}}{{r}_{\rm{SA}2{SA}}\times {r}_{\rm{SI}2{SI}}}\), providing a direct and normalized measure of crossmodal fidelity. Median cross-modal generalization indices across nine participants were compared between β1 shared, β1 modality-specific,and high γ modality-specific electrodes using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Last, we examined whether the articulatory movement synthesizer effectively captured a stable relationship between neural activity and AKTs, allowing for extrapolative generalization. Specifically, the model’s ability to predict movement trajectories of syllables excluded from the training set was assessed using L1SO. In each iteration, one syllable was excluded from training, and the model was trained on the remaining syllables. The excluded syllable was then used as a testing set, and the correlation (r) between predicted and actual AKTs was computed. Chance-level r values were determined using an untrained, randomly initialized model. This was extended to leave-two- (L2SO) and leave-three-syllables-out (L3SO) paradigms. Decoding performance across L1SO, L2SO and L3SO models was compared using two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests separately for SA and SI encoding electrodes to assess modality-specific extrapolative generalization.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The sample size (nine participants) was determined by patient availability and consent, with electrode coverage comparable to similar high-density ECoG studies. No participants were excluded. All included trials met quality criteria: clear audio and neural signals for SA; video/audio surveillance and (in S6–S9) EMG monitoring confirmed absence of overt movement during SI. Each syllable was repeated 45 or 60 times per participant depending on paradigm to ensure statistical reliability. Syllables were randomly ordered across trials to minimize order effects. Model replication used five- or tenfold cross-validation with stratified sampling to balance syllable representation across folds. Investigators were not blinded to task conditions, as condition knowledge was required for trial segmentation and modality-specific modeling; shuffled-label control models were used to establish chance-level benchmarks. All tests were two-sided unless noted. Multiple comparisons were corrected using Benjamini–Hochberg (encoding model, responsive electrode selection) or Bonferroni (somatotopy, cross-correlation). Permutation tests (1,000–10,000 iterations) assessed significance for encoding electrode identification, cosine similarity, and somatotopic comparisons. Nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney U-test, Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis) were used for decoding performance due to non-normal distributions. Reproducibility was supported by consistent procedures across participants, with two independent paradigms yielding comparable results.
