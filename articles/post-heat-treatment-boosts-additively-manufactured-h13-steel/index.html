Post-Heat Treatment Boosts Additively Manufactured H13 Steel Toughness by

TheTools · 21.11.2026 08:47 · source · permalink

A multi-stage post-heat treatment protocol applied to laser powder bed fusion AISI H13 tool steel has demonstrated the capacity to increase impact toughness by up to 1200 percent compared to the as-printed condition, transforming an intrinsically brittle additively manufactured alloy into a resilient material suitable for demanding hot-work tooling applications. Conducted by an engineering research team examining additive manufacturing regimes, the comprehensive investigation evaluated how systematically varying laser scanning speeds alongside tailored thermal conditioning modifies the microstructure, tensile performance, compressive strength, and tribological durability of printed tool steel. Laser powder bed fusion has rapidly gained prominence across the die and mold sector because of its capacity to generate intricate, conformal geometries directly from digital designs, offering a compelling alternative to traditional subtractive machining and forging techniques. However, the extreme cooling rates and cyclical thermal gradients characteristic of powder bed fusion induce substantial residual stresses, cellular microsegregation, and microstructural brittleness that historically restricted the functional deployment of additively produced components. By establishing a rigorous post-processing framework encompassing annealing, quenching, and double tempering, the study reveals how specific solid-state phase transformations can be modulated to recover mechanical performance while identifying the operational limits imposed by process-induced porosity.

To investigate these transformative physical mechanisms, the researchers utilized commercial gas-atomized AISI H13 tool steel powder exhibiting spherical particle sizes ranging between 30 and 50 micrometers. AISI H13 is an established chromium-molybdenum-vanadium alloy favored in high-temperature forging and die-casting operations due to its exceptional thermal fatigue resistance, high hot hardness, and elevated yield strength. The experimental matrix was fabricated on an industrial laser powder bed fusion system operating at a fixed laser power of 200 watts and an invariable layer thickness of 70 micrometers, parameters selected in alignment with volumetric energy density standards documented in metallurgical literature. Within this processing window, the laser scanning speed was deliberately manipulated across four discrete levels spanning 150, 300, 450, and 600 millimeters per second. This broad operational envelope intentionally traversed the boundary from high volumetric energy density inputs that foster thorough melting and dense consolidation down to low energy inputs nearing lack-of-fusion instability. By isolating scanning speed as the sole variable during additive fabrication, the authors successfully established distinct solidification baselines characterized by varying initial fractions of martensite, retained austenite, and localized void defects.

To maintain rigorous experimental control and eliminate geometric artifacts, all test specimens were produced in an identical vertical build orientation normal to the substrate plate. Standardized mechanical specimens were subsequently extracted from consistent orientations and coordinate locations using wire electrical discharge machining. The experimental geometry encompassed sub-size tensile bars prepared in accordance with ASTM E8/E8M dimensions, standard Charpy V-notch impact bars compliant with ASTM E23 specifications, cylindrical compression cylinders adhering to ASTM E9 requirements, and flat pin-on-disc specimens dimensioned to ASTM G99 standards. Thermodynamic modeling conducted via Thermo-Calc software determined the critical austenite transformation temperatures, specifically identifying the AC1 and AC3 boundaries to guide subsequent thermal cycles. The multi-stage post-processing regimen subjected the wire-cut specimens to a spheroidizing annealing stage, followed by vacuum austenitization and quenching, and culminated in a double tempering sequence executed at 550 degrees Celsius. Quantitative microstructural assessments were performed across each processing stage utilizing side-section scanning electron microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping, mercury displacement porosimetry, and quantitative X-ray diffraction phase analysis resolved through specialized analysis software.

Microstructural examination of the as-built H13 components confirmed that laser scanning speed exerts an immediate and profound influence on solidification dynamics and phase synthesis. In the unheated condition, rapid cooling produced a fine cellular and columnar martensitic substructure enveloped by intercellular retained austenite. Quantitative X-ray diffraction revealed that at the lowest scanning speed of 150 millimeters per second, the as-built microstructure comprised 80.3 percent martensite alongside 16.6 percent retained austenite. Accelerating the scanning speed to 600 millimeters per second elevated the martensite fraction to 89.9 percent while diminishing the retained austenite volume to 12.9 percent. This upward shift in martensite volume stems directly from the steeper cooling velocities and contracted thermal cycles induced under diminished energy input. However, this accelerated solidification simultaneously triggered severe defect formation. Porosity measurements demonstrated that internal void volume expanded from 6.5 percent at 150 millimeters per second to 11.0 percent at 600 millimeters per second. Furthermore, the morphology of these defects shifted from fine, isolated spherical pores measuring approximately 20 micrometers to expansive, irregular lack-of-fusion voids extending up to 70 micrometers with aspect ratios reaching six to one.

The mechanical consequences of these process-induced microstructural variations were pronounced across the baseline tensile evaluations. In the as-built state, specimens fabricated at 150 millimeters per second registered the highest ultimate tensile strength of approximately 1400 megapascals with an elongation of approximately 7 percent, bolstered by dense material continuity and high dislocation densities. Conversely, components produced at 600 millimeters per second exhibited reduced strength and embrittlement, with tensile elongation dropping below 5 percent. The elongated lack-of-fusion cavities present at higher speeds acted as potent stress concentrators, promoting early microcrack initiation around unmelted powder particles and precipitating premature structural failure under uniaxial tension. Hardness measurements echoed this downward trend: despite housing a higher proportion of inherently hard martensite, specimens manufactured at elevated speeds exhibited lower bulk hardness because the softening influence of internal voids and non-melted boundaries outweighed the phase contribution of the martensitic matrix. These baseline findings underscored that while high scanning speeds increase throughput, they introduce structural flaws that fundamentally impair tensile integrity.

The implementation of post-deposition heat treatment fundamentally restructured the internal phase architecture, demonstrating that post-processing thermal stages exert far greater control over final material performance than initial printer parameters. Austenitization and quenching transformed unstable retained austenite into a highly refined martensitic matrix across all samples. This structural transition yielded a modest decline in tensile strength but produced a noticeable recovery in plastic deformation, particularly in the dense specimens printed at lower scanning speeds where void concentrations remained low. Following quenching, double tempering at 550 degrees Celsius triggered the partial decomposition of supersaturated martensite and activated atomic diffusion. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy confirmed the extensive precipitation of fine, highly dispersed secondary alloy carbides rich in chromium, vanadium, molybdenum, and carbon, such as Cr7C3, VC, and Mo2C. This precipitation sequence, paired with internal residual stress relief and matrix softening, shifted the mechanical equilibrium by reducing tensile strength by roughly 27 percent relative to the quenched state while boosting elongation by approximately 34 percent.

The most striking mechanical improvements achieved through post-processing manifested in impact toughness and compressive resistance. In Charpy V-notch testing, as-built samples printed at 150 millimeters per second exhibited an initial impact toughness of merely 10 joules, reflecting severe as-solidified brittleness. Subsequent quenching elevated toughness to approximately 80 joules, and double tempering further escalated energy absorption to 122 joules, establishing a twelve-fold enhancement over the raw printed material. This dramatic resilience stems from the stress-relieving capacity of tempered martensite and the presence of ductile retained austenite, which effectively blunts crack tips and arrests fracture propagation. However, the data revealed a critical threshold regarding internal integrity: when porosity exceeded 7.5 percent, impact toughness declined precipitously, and the toughening benefits of heat treatment became severely muted. In compression testing under a constant strain rate of 10 millimeters per minute, quenching virtually doubled compressive capacity, driving values from 3000 megapascals to 6000 megapascals at 150 millimeters per second, and elevating the 600 millimeter per second samples from 4500 megapascals to over 9000 megapascals, as compressive loading forced internal voids and microcracks closed rather than tearing them open.

Tribological evaluations conducted across sliding distances up to 2000 meters demonstrated that wear behavior in additively manufactured H13 is governed by a complex interplay of phase constitution, contact pressure, and defect morphology rather than bulk hardness alone. Testing under a modest contact pressure of 0.5 megapascals revealed an adhesive wear regime dominated by localized asperity bonding and shearing, characterized by shallow micro-ploughing grooves and an extended incubation stage lasting up to 1500 meters at lower scanning speeds. When the contact pressure escalated to 4.2 megapascals, the operational regime transitioned toward aggressive abrasive and oxidative wear, evidenced by deep micro-cutting tracks, surface fracture, and fragmented debris. Under these demanding contact conditions, quenched specimens maintained superior wear resistance compared to their tempered counterparts. Although tempering enhances ductility and fracture toughness, the associated softening of the martensitic matrix and the coarsening of secondary carbides accelerated material removal, causing wear rates in tempered specimens to climb toward 1.8 milligrams per minute under high pressure.

Counterintuitively, specimens fabricated at the maximum scanning speed of 600 millimeters per second displayed surprisingly robust wear resistance across both pressure levels, despite possessing inferior tensile properties and substantial porosity. Under 0.5 megapascals of pressure, the quenched 600 millimeter per second condition attained the lowest recorded wear rate of approximately 0.55 milligrams per minute, while maintaining the lowest wear rate of approximately 1.2 milligrams per minute at 4.2 megapascals. Detailed morphological analysis revealed that open surface pores inherent to high-speed fabrication acted as functional micro-reservoirs that trapped detached wear debris. By capturing loose particulate matter, these voids prevented loose particles from participating in three-body abrasive scouring along the contact interface. Furthermore, localized contact stresses promoted strain-induced transformation of metastable retained austenite into hard martensite directly along the sliding track, dynamically reinforcing the surface layer. Consequently, while lack-of-fusion voids compromise tensile loading by concentrating stresses, their presence can paradoxically mitigate surface degradation under continuous dry sliding contact.

When benchmarked against conventionally manufactured wrought and cast H13 tool steel, the post-heat-treated additively manufactured alloy exhibited competitive microhardness, comparable compressive endurance, and noticeably superior impact toughness, affirming its viability for tooling applications. Nevertheless, the authors emphasized clear physical boundaries governing the interpretation of their results. Because all specimens were synthesized along a single vertical build orientation and extracted uniformly relative to the deposition plane, the observed behaviors reflect scanning speed and heat treatment effects under controlled anisotropic conditions. In industrial tooling components featuring intricate geometries and variable thermal dissipation paths, anisotropic grain alignment, melt-pool boundaries, and directional columnar growth could alter crack initiation thresholds and wear performance. By demonstrating that a sequence of annealing, quenching, and double tempering can effectively unlock 122 joules of impact toughness while managing wear mechanisms, the research provides a clear roadmap for tailoring laser powder bed fusion parameters to produce robust, high-performance hot-work steel dies.
