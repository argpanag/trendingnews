Stephen Richer: A smooth election so far

The U.S. midterm election has thus far gone off without any major issues, according to Stephen Richer, a legal scholar at the Cato Institute, a senior fellow at the Harvard Kennedy School's Ash Center, and an NBC News election analyst.

"Election officials have spent the past year preparing for the worst," he said in an email. "So far, it hasn’t happened."

"Far from it, in fact," he added. "Election Day has been smooth, building on a highly successful early-voting period in which over 55 million Americans voted."

That, of course, does not mean that there have not been some local hiccups.

"One of the few material voting issues comes from Westchester County, New York, where a programming error has prevented the tabulation of ballots at some voting locations," Richer wrote. "New York uses hand-marked paper ballots. Those ballots unable to be read by the tabulators will be securely stored and tabulated later in the evening when the problem is fixed. It should not prevent any voters from voting."

"Broward County, Florida, is also experiencing some technical challenges," he added. "NBC News reported earlier that a software glitch sent some voters home early in the morning. They will need to return to a voting location later today."

"Such technical challenges are, of course, undesirable, and their impact on the individual voter should not be ignored," he continued. "But these challenges are also par for the course. There are approximately 100,000 voting locations open across 9,000 voting jurisdictions in the country. Over 50 million Americans will vote today. In a world of imperfect machines and imperfect humans, something happens every general election. Such errors should be clearly communicated so as to dispel any unfounded allegations, and election officials must quickly deploy contingency plans so that every voter can vote."

"Various arms of the federal government have sent out unorthodox messages reminding noncitizens that it is unlawful for them to vote in state and federal contests," Richer concluded. "There are no reports of federal interference."

North Carolina to extend voting time at a handful of locations

The North Carolina State Board of Elections voted to extend voting hours at a handful of precinct locations following timing issues, including short delays in opening a location.

The board held an emergency meeting this afternoon, which it said was “a routine part of Election Day operations and is classified as an emergency meeting only to meet public notice requirements.”

Polls close in most precincts at 7:30 p.m. The board voted to extend voting by 45 minutes at the Tabor City Courthouse in Columbus County, as well as shorter extensions at a few polling places in four other counties. The extensions mean that the state will be delayed by 45 minutes in posting statewide election results, the board said in a post to Facebook.

A board member explained the polling extension rules at the start of the board meeting this afternoon: “If the polls are delayed in opening for more than 15 minutes, or if they are interrupted for more than 15 minutes after opening, then the state board has the discretion to extend the closing time by an equal number of minutes.”

Sacramento man charged with delaying and obstructing delivery of political mail

A 35-year-old Sacramento man was charged today with delaying and obstructing the delivery of U.S. mail, including political mail, according to the U.S. Attorney's Office in the Eastern District of California.

The Justice Department said that the defendant, Antoine Rashad Polite, who was a U.S. Postal worker, "improperly withheld and delayed the delivery of hundreds of pieces of political mail intended for residents on his route" between Sept. 23 and Oct. 27.

Citing the criminal complaint, the DOJ said "the withheld mail included campaign materials relating to candidates and ballot propositions appearing on the Nov. 3, 2026, California general election ballot. Postal officials ultimately recovered and delivered the vast majority of the mail to their intended recipients."

If convicted, Polite faces up to six months in prison for obstruction of mail and one year in prison for delay or destruction of mail.

“An FBI Questioned Document Unit Examiner (i.e., handwriting analyst) has reviewed the 2026 mailing to the Oklahoma State Election Board and compared it to the initial 2024 mailings and has preliminarily determined through observation that the handwriting on the exterior of the envelopes was consistent with having been written by the same individual,” the affidavit said.

DOJ will deploy a record number of monitors on Election Day

The Justice Department plans to send a record number of election monitors to polling places in all 50 states for today's midterm elections.

While Democratic and Republican administrations have sent federal workers to observe elections for decades, traditionally their mission has been to help ensure Americans exercise their right to vote. Harmeet Dhillon, the head of the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division, has said monitors would also seek to ensure that citizens were voting and that their votes weren’t canceled out by someone who “shouldn’t be voting.”

Though election monitors are just observers, there has been occasional pushback from states; in 2024, Republican-led Texas, Florida and Missouri sought to block the Democratic Biden administration from sending monitors, arguing that their state laws did not permit federal officials to be at polling places.

This year, there will be roughly 1,000 monitors nationwide, according to the Justice Department. By comparison, 289 were deployed for the 2022 midterms and 714 for the 2024 presidential election.
