David Rosenberg: Bank of Canada rate hike right now would be playing with economic fire

TheTools · 23.10.2026 18:57 · source · permalink

It is astounding that the markets are pricing in a rate hike by the Bank of Canada next week as a virtual certainty and not just that, but a two-in-three chance of another move in December, so Merry Christmas to anyone looking to buy a home or car.

All of this repricing is not coming out of the blue, but rather reflects a distinctive hawkish tone of late coming from governor Tiff Macklem. I can understand the reasons why, but I don't agree with them, nor do I support a rate hike right now given the fragile state of the Canadian economy and the nature of this statistical inflation, which comes from a global supply shock as opposed to any homegrown demand pressures — a supply shock I see as more of a deflationary squeeze on real personal incomes and profit margins than anything else.

Look at the domestic economy. A one per cent trend in real gross domestic product (GDP), which isn't a recession but is still pathetically weak, plus the economy has lost more than 40,000 jobs this year, the housing market is in complete disarray, there's a flat trend in manufacturing activity and there's a contraction in the residential real estate sector.

All the inflation is one thing: gasoline prices, which have skyrocketed 28 per cent over the past year. The 93 per cent share of the consumer price index (CPI) excluding energy is running right in line with the central bank's two per cent target. Virtually every core inflation measure is at or below two per cent.

But Macklem is worried that headline inflation has been around three per cent for three straight months and that the longer this is sustained, the greater the risks will be of a more generalized spillover. The problem is that there is no evidence this is happening after eight months of the United States-Iran conflict.

The Bank of Canada has no control over imported inflation from high and rising energy costs; chasing headline inflation now in the context of a one-trick pony is nothing more than chasing prices at the pump, so we should ask the question of the appropriateness of targeting something completely out of your control.

If this was inflation driven by an overheated economy, that would be one thing, but that's not what we have on our hands currently. The pace of economic activity is expanding below its potential, as anemic as supply dynamics are, given the collapse in population growth.

The Bank of Canada and most of Bay Street had a conniption earlier this week with the release of the central bank's survey of consumer expectations because five-year inflation views rose to a four-year high, even though this metric has a very limited sample size, has a single cycle under its belt in its brief history and has absolutely no leading properties.
