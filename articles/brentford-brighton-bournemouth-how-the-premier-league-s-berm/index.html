Brentford, Brighton, Bournemouth: How the Premier League's 'Bermuda Triangle' defies the odds with recruitment and culture

TheTools · 23.09.2026 05:39 · source · permalink

Brentford, Brighton and Bournemouth have turned smart recruitment, succession planning and a strong club culture into a formula for Premier League success - and the established elite are increasingly finding themselves caught in their 'Bermuda Triangle'

A strange patch of Premier League territory stretches from west London to the south coast, where top clubs cannot pass through easily.

Brentford, Brighton and Bournemouth have formed the top-flight's 'Bermuda Triangle'.

Players leave and managers move on, yet the three Bs keep defying the odds. The trio have found a formula not only to survive but to compete against the extraordinary financial muscle of the big clubs and, increasingly, upset them.

Brighton have not been relegated since their promotion in 2017 and have qualified for Europe twice. Brentford followed four years later and have stayed up ever since, while Bournemouth have established themselves despite their comparatively modest resources, culminating in last season's sixth-place finish and a spot in the Europa League.

Fabian Hurzeler's Brighton lead the way in points taken from the 'Big 6' - Arsenal, Chelsea, Liverpool, Manchester United, Manchester City and Tottenham - of any club since the start of the 2022/23 season, when Bournemouth returned to the Premier League.

Only Aston Villa, Newcastle and Nottingham Forest, who all have more spending power, have upset the top clubs more often during that time than the next best Brentford and Bournemouth.

How have they done it?

The Bs share two common factors: outstanding recruitment and a strong culture.

They don't sign the best players available because they can't. They sign players whose potential they believe exceeds the price they're paying.

Ahead of the 2025/26 season, the three clubs earned a combined £485m from player sales. They went on to finish sixth, eighth and ninth.

Losing their best talent is difficult for supporters to stomach, but the clubs do not view it as a crisis. It is part of their business model to discover, develop, sell and reinvest not just in the squad but infrastructure too. All three have invested in their training facilities.

When Antoine Semenyo left Bournemouth for Manchester City in January, 20-year-old winger Raya had already been identified as a replacement and joined in the same month from Vasco da Gama. Semenyo became Bournemouth's record sale, but his departure did not leave the club scrambling for a replacement, succession planning was already under way.

At the other end of the scale, Jordan Henderson arrived at Brentford last summer to replace Christian Norgaard's leadership and experience.

That ability to anticipate and react to change is perhaps the defining feature of all three clubs. They recruit not just for the team they have but the one they expect to have. All three use a data-driven approach, but Brentford owner Matthew Benham says there is still an element of "good old-fashioned scouting" in theirs.

"Bryan Mbeumo was 19 years old when we spotted him, playing in the French second division," he said in March. "The model was 'meh' on him but the fact our scouts, and especially the technical director Lee Dykes, were so positive outweighed the data.

"If you have a player in their mid-20s who has played a couple of hundred games, the model better like him or we are not going to sign him."

Recruitment becomes culture

Brighton, under the ownership of professional sports betting entrepreneur Tony Bloom, use a custom analytics software to discover players across the world. Bournemouth owner Bill Foley has taken a different route since taking over in 2022, with his multi-club model designed to create connections between recruitment networks and player development.

The similarities go beyond how they find players. Their signings fit their tactical ideas - Brighton's control, Brentford's power and Brighton's technicality - but also their ethos.

Keith Andrews worked under Thomas Frank for 12 months before Brentford promoted him from set-piece coach. Brentford did similar when Frank succeeded Dean Smith in 2018. A continuation rather than a reset. Brighton losing Graham Potter or Roberto De Zerbi did not collapse the club either.

Marco Rose was appointed as Andoni Iraola's successor in April before the season had finished so he and the club could begin planning for this campaign. Iraola had told the club well in advance that he wanted to leave when his contract expired.

The Big 6 rarely sign unknown youngsters from Europe or South America as they need experienced talent to make an immediate impact. Brighton, Brentford and Bournemouth must think differently. They can nurture players' development and tolerate mistakes until potential becomes performance.

It is no longer a case of whether Brentford, Brighton and Bournemouth can survive in the Premier League. It is now how far this model can take them and whether the established order can find a way to navigate the 'Bermuda Triangle'.
