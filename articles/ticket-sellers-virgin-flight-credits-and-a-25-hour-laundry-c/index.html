Ticket sellers, Virgin flight credits and a 25-hour laundry cycle: meet Choice’s 2026 Shonky award winners

TheTools · 03.11.2026 14:00 · source · permalink

Australia’s worst products and services for the year have been named by consumer advocacy group Choice

Concert tickets that surge to $1,650, an airline pocketing $75m of unused flight credits and a washer/dryer cycle that takes 25 hours have all made the dishonour roll in consumer advocacy group Choice’s annual Shonky awards.

Now in their 21st year, the Shonkys spotlight Australia’s worst products and services as decided by Choice.

Choice awarded Virgin a Shonky gong for imposing strict deadlines on customers to use credits issued for cancelled flights between 2020 and 2022, amid Covid-19 travel restrictions. Customers had to book their trips by 30 June this year, and had to use the credits for flights taking off before 27 May 2027. This resulted in $75m of credits going unused and then being returned to Virgin’s bottom line, according to Choice.

A spokesperson for Virgin Australia said: “More than 93% of Covid credits were redeemed by guests, supported by strong bookings over the past four years.

“Following a number of extensions between 2023 and 2025, we regularly communicated the expiry date and later extended the fly-by date to 27 May 2027, giving guests almost another year to travel.”

Ticketmaster and Ticketek received Shonky awards for using what Choice deemed “sneaky” tactics – dynamic pricing and “in demand” seats – to unfairly inflate prices for live events amid the broader cost-of-living crisis.

Dynamic pricing – common in transport and hotel sectors, but a more recent phenomenon for live events – describes products whose prices fluctuate based on demand.

For example, when a popular concert goes on sale, ticket prices may go up as more customers join the online queue.

Choice said John Farnham fans who logged on to Ticketek looking to secure tickets to a tribute concert earlier this year found themselves facing variable prices. “Ultimate” tickets – the most premium option available, which were not subject to variable pricing – cost $1,650.

Similarly, NRL supporters who bought tickets to some matches in 2026 through Ticketmaster reported “in demand” seats selling for $50 more than regular seats, even though they were only one row and one bay apart.

A spokesperson for Ticketmaster said it did not use surge pricing or algorithms that automatically increase prices in response to real-time demand, and that prices were set by event organisers.

“For ‘in demand’ tickets, event organisers set the prices of sought-after seats closer to market value and may review these over the sales period,” they said. “These prices do not automatically increase because more fans join a queue.”

Guardian Australia contacted Ticketek for comment.

The Albanese government promised to restrict dynamic pricing in 2024, but did not include any prohibitions against the practice in its new laws designed to limit unfair trading practices, due to come into effect in 2027.

Choice’s chief executive officer, Monique Perry, said the organisation wanted to see expanded price-gouging laws to protect consumers.

“With Virgin, we’d love to see consumers get the $75m back or have unlimited time to [use] those flight credits,” she said.

Perry said Choice decided which products to consider for Shonky awards based on consumer feedback.

“Some of these are really serious for consumers and where we really have a call to action and sometimes it’s more about buyer beware,” she said.

Perry said calling out companies sometimes resulted in tangible actions, such as Commonwealth Bank pledging to refund $68m of fees after Choice gave it a Shonky in 2025 for charging $270m in excessive fees to low-income customers.

This year, Hisense got a Shonky for advertising a washer/dryer model as “super energy efficient”.

However, Choice says the energy rating can only be achieved on the 2025 Series 8i heat pump combo washer/dryer (HCF8I1215HB) if one uses its 13.5-hour “eco” dry cycle, and 728-minute wash cycle – more than 25 hours combined for a wash and dry.

A spokesperson for Hisense said: “The energy rating displayed on our product is determined in accordance with the Australian government’s regulated Energy Rating Scheme and the applicable Greenhouse and Energy Minimum Standards (GEMS) requirements. Energy ratings are calculated using prescribed Australian testing standards, established performance criteria and defined assessment methodologies.”

Perry described Hisense’s ratings as “gaming a broken system”. Manufacturers get to nominate which program will be assessed for energy ratings “and it’s usually one that produces the best energy-efficiency outcome, rather than one households would actually use”.

Choice scolded Petcover for its “economy” insurance policy, saying the insurer sold customers an entirely new policy every 12 months, which classifies all conditions their pet has previously experienced as pre-existing, thus excluding them from cover.

Quest received an award for selling its “protein chips” at a huge mark-up compared with similar, non-protein options. For example, at more than $20 per 100g, the Quest nacho cheese protein chips were 886% more expensive than nacho cheese flavour Doritos, which cost $2.06 per 100g.

Although the Quest chips contain 56g of protein per 100g, compared with 7.5g for Doritos, Perry said there were much cheaper and simpler ways to get additional protein.

PlayStation owner Sony received a Shonky for revealing customers did not actually own the movies and games they’d paid for, and instead had only bought a licence to access the content, which can be revoked at any time without a refund.

Choice said the problems with this kind of licensing arrangement became apparent in June, when Sony announced an end to its licensing agreement with Studio Canal in the UK and Europe. This resulted in 500 movies paid for and stored in customer’s digital libraries being removed, without any kind of refund, Choice said.

Guardian Australia contacted Quest, Petcover and Sony Entertainment for comment.

This article was amended on 4 November 2026 to clarify that the $1650 John Farnham tickets were not subject to variable pricing.
