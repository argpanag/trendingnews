More drones bring huge manpower costs for infantry units, report finds

TheTools · 20.11.2026 13:10 · source · permalink

The drone revolution could require infantry battalions to devote nearly half their soldiers to operating and supporting unmanned systems, forcing armies to sacrifice some traditional combat jobs to accommodate new demands, according to a new assessment.

The findings, detailed in a report Thursday by Britain’s Royal United Services Institute, challenge the notion that increasingly sophisticated battlefield robots and unmanned systems will reduce the need for troops.

Researchers calculated that a battalion of roughly 560 soldiers operating drones at the intensity seen in Ukraine would need about 260 personnel dedicated to the effort.

“There is no discernible saving in personnel,” researchers Jack Watling and Robert Tollast wrote in the study.

A Marine controls an Overland ULTRA autonomous ground vehicle during a resupply exercise at Twentynine Palms, Calif., Aug. 14, 2026. (Alexis French/U.S. Marine Corps)

The researchers, who have spent years observing and interviewing combat units in Ukraine, used a British light infantry battalion as a point of comparison.

The findings also have implications for the U.S. Army and ground forces across NATO as they adapt to a modern battlefield dominated by drones, sensors and the threat of constant surveillance.

The report also challenges assumptions that cheap attack drones have made tanks easy targets and possibly obsolete.

The researchers found that properly protected armored vehicles can survive more than 15 drone hits, while some engagements have required 40 to 100 drones to destroy a single tank.

A soldier assembles a drone battery kit during a demonstration at Baumholder Training Area in Germany on Nov. 10, 2026. (Elijah Campbell/U.S. Army)

Such attacks can consume enormous quantities of ammunition and occupy drone crews that might otherwise be engaging a number of targets, the report said.

The findings point to how modern warfare requires armies to employ traditional weapons alongside unmanned systems. That runs counter to the conventional wisdom, which holds that such weapons are being rendered obsolete.

Still, the personnel demands of drones will require infantry units to reconsider how they are organized, the report said.

For a battalion of roughly 560 soldiers, the researchers estimated that 90 would be needed for first-person-view drone operations, while an additional 80 would handle unmanned logistics and maintenance.

Also, 60 soldiers would conduct reconnaissance and 30 would defend against enemy drones.

Marines watch a Bolt-M loitering munition hover over a range at Camp Lejeune, N.C., Aug. 28, 2026. (Jorge Borjas/U.S. Marine Corps)

The demands extend beyond operating the aircraft.

A typical Ukrainian attack-drone team of four or five people needs about 1.2 metric tons of drones and supporting equipment, including batteries, ammunition and generators, to sustain just three days of operations, according to the study.

An infantry battalion could expend 500 to 700 first-person-view attack drones each week, the researchers said.

Even reconnaissance drones, which can be recovered and reused, are routinely lost. A battalion can lose 20 to 60 multi-copter surveillance drones each week, according to the report.

Such demands create a substantial logistical burden for Army battalions that automation alone is unlikely to eliminate, the report said.

Some traditional infantry jobs could be redefined as a result.

For example, the researchers say drones could take over some traditional sniper missions. While snipers must position themselves to observe targets directly, attack drone operators can strike from greater distance and without exposing themselves to the same dangers.

The study suggests that military units could redirect some sniper training and personnel toward operating reconnaissance drones.

However, the researchers said traditional sniper skills will still be needed, particularly when bad weather or difficult terrain limits drone operations.

Soldiers launch a drone during an exercise at Yakima Training Center in Yakima, Wash., July 20, 2025. (Adeline Witherspoon/U.S. Army)

Mortar crews, meanwhile, retain advantages that drones cannot easily match. A mortar crew can sustain a rapid barrage directed at the enemy, potentially landing a round every five seconds for up to a minute.

By comparison, a drone team might require about two minutes between successive attacks. Yet the two systems are “devastating when used in combination,” the report said.

Despite the proliferation of robotic weapons, the researchers concluded that “infantry mass remains critical” because soldiers are still needed to take and hold ground.
