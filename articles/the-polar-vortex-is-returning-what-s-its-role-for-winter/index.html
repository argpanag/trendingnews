The polar vortex is returning. What's its role for winter?

TheTools · 23.09.2026 16:26 · source · permalink
  • The polar vortex, a circulation of cold air over the Arctic, is currently stronger than normal.
  • A strong and stable polar vortex helps keep the coldest Arctic air contained near the North Pole.
  • A very strong El Niño is also developing, which could influence the polar vortex later in the winter.
  • Long-range forecasts suggest the polar vortex could weaken in January and February, potentially allowing cold air to move south.

The polar vortex is taking shape over the North Pole, and it is starting the season stronger than normal, based on the latest sub-seasonal forecasts from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts.

That might sound like a recipe for a brutally cold winter. It isn't that simple.

The latest models show the zonal winds surrounding the polar vortex strengthening through October, while NOAA is forecasting a very strong El Niño developing in the tropical Pacific, and the two atmospheric patterns could play a role in how the winter unfolds.

The polar vortex is a large-scale circulation of cold air and strong winds that forms high in the atmosphere over the Arctic each winter. When it is strong and stable, it can help keep the coldest Arctic air bottled up near the pole. When it weakens or becomes disrupted, some of that cold air can move south into the United States and other parts of the Northern Hemisphere.

This year's vortex is strengthening in the stratosphere as temperatures fall over the North Pole. Forecasts show it becoming stronger than normal in late September before trending closer to typical levels in October.

The bigger question for winter is what happens after that.

What is the polar vortex?

The polar vortex is a large-scale circulation of cold air and strong winds that forms over the Arctic during the Northern Hemisphere's cold season, according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

Most of the attention this time of year is on the stratospheric polar vortex, which forms high above the surface as the Arctic stratosphere cools. The circulation is strongest when the temperature difference between the cold polar region and warmer areas farther south becomes large.

Think of the vortex as part of the atmosphere's system for containing Arctic air.

When the circulation is strong and relatively stable, its winds can help keep cold air concentrated near the pole. When the vortex becomes weaker, stretched or displaced, the atmosphere can become more favorable for Arctic air to move farther south.

The effects are not automatic. A disrupted polar vortex does not mean every part of the United States will turn colder, and a strong vortex does not mean the country will stay warm.

Polar vortex is stronger than normal. What does that mean for winter?

The polar vortex is getting stronger over the Arctic as the Northern Hemisphere heads into fall. For now, that's not necessarily bad news for the United States.

A stronger, more stable polar vortex can help keep the Arctic's coldest air bottled up near the pole instead of allowing it to spill south. The bigger winter question is whether that changes later in the season.

The stratospheric polar vortex is developing normally for this time of year and is currently somewhat stronger than average, according to recent atmospheric data. Long-range forecasts show the vortex could weaken later in the winter, particularly around January and February.

That potential shift is what meteorologists will be watching.

When the vortex is strong and stable, its winds help contain cold Arctic air. But when it weakens or becomes disrupted, Arctic air can have more opportunities to move into the midlatitudes, including the United States.

When Arctic air meets a moisture-rich storm system, the combination can produce snowstorms and blizzards. This year, El Niño can help supply that moisture by strengthening the southern jet stream and steering more Pacific moisture into the U.S.

Why is the polar vortex getting stronger now?

A stronger-than-normal polar vortex in September is not a warning that winter will be unusually cold. In fact, a strong, stable vortex can help keep the coldest Arctic air farther north.

The polar vortex naturally develops during late summer and fall as sunlight decreases over the Arctic and the stratosphere begins to cool.

As that happens, strong winds begin circulating around the polar region from west to east. These are called zonal winds, located about 10 to 30 miles above the North Pole, and they are one of the key measurements meteorologists use to track the strength and organization of the polar vortex.

Recent observations show the 2026-27 vortex strengthening as that seasonal cooling gets underway. NASA data analyzed in mid-September showed winds around 60 degrees north in the lower stratosphere running above their long-term average for the date. The return and strengthening of those west-to-east winds are part of the normal seasonal development of the vortex.

The latest European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts forecast shows the same general trend.

In the latest forecasts, the average winds strengthen through the fall, reaching roughly 30 meters per second around December, before weakening later in the winter, where the forecast becomes more uncertain.

By late January, the average falls to roughly 17 to 20 meters per second, and later in the winter it hovers around 10 to 15 meters per second. Some individual forecasts become much weaker and even dip below zero, which would indicate the winds reversing direction.

A reversal of those winds can be associated with a sudden stratospheric warming, a major disruption of the polar vortex.

For now, the important signal is that the vortex is strengthening as it normally does during the fall. The bigger question is whether it remains strong through winter or becomes significantly weaker or disrupted later in the season.

Could the polar vortex weaken this winter?

Yes. The latest ECMWF seasonal forecast shows the polar vortex potentially weakening as winter progresses, with the biggest change appearing in January and February.

The model's average forecast has the winds surrounding the vortex strengthening through December before weakening substantially in January. By late January, the average winds fall to roughly 17 to 20 meters per second and then generally remain around 10 to 15 meters per second through February.

Some individual forecasts show an even greater weakening, with winds approaching or dropping below zero. That could signal a more significant disruption of the polar vortex, but the wide range of forecasts also shows how uncertain the winter outlook becomes.

And even if the polar vortex is disrupted, that does not automatically mean Arctic air will reach the United States. The effects have to work their way through the atmosphere, and the resulting weather depends on where the larger atmospheric patterns set up.

What does a polar vortex collapse mean?

A Sudden Stratospheric Warming, or SSW, is one of the most significant ways the polar vortex can be disrupted.

During an SSW, temperatures high in the stratosphere can rise by several tens of degrees in just a few days. The warming can weaken the vortex, displace it or split it into separate pieces.

The changes do not immediately reach the surface.

Stratospheric disturbances can take days to weeks to propagate downward into the lower atmosphere. If the disturbance couples with the tropospheric circulation, it can help create a pattern that allows colder Arctic air to move farther south.

But an SSW does not automatically produce a major snowstorm or prolonged cold wave in the United States. The eventual weather depends on the larger atmospheric pattern already in place.

How does El Niño fit into the polar vortex?

This winter has another major atmospheric player: El Niño.

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center said Sept. 10 that El Niño is strengthening, with a greater than 90% chance of a very strong event during the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026-27. NOAA also said there is a 75% chance that the October-December period will reach the threshold for a historic-strength event based on records dating to 1950.

Strong El Niño events can alter atmospheric circulation and influence the jet stream. Research and seasonal analyses also show that El Niño can affect the stratospheric circulation through changes in large-scale atmospheric waves.

Severe Weather Europe's long-range analysis notes that the developing strong El Niño could help create a pattern favorable for a midwinter polar-vortex disruption.

Farmers' Almanac winter forecast by region

  • Southern Tier: Wetter than normal, with frequent storms possible from California through Texas, the Gulf Coast and the Southeast. Some storms could bring sleet, freezing rain or heavy, wet snow when cold air moves in.
  • West: Changeable conditions are expected, with the Pacific Northwest seeing a less predictable snow season. The southern Rockies could see above-average mountain snowfall.
  • Northern Plains, Upper Midwest and Great Lakes: Temperatures are expected to average near normal, but quick cold snaps, snow squalls, blustery winds and brief snowy periods are possible.
  • Middle of the country: Expect frequent swings between thawing and freezing conditions, creating rapidly changing road and field conditions.
  • Northeast: One of the most complicated winter patterns is expected, with coastal areas more likely to see rain or mixed precipitation while inland locations could see rounds of heavy, wet snow, especially in January and February.
  • Mid-Atlantic: A secondary storm track could bring opportunities for rain, wintry mixes and snow as storms move along the Eastern Seaboard.
