UN court delivering judgment over whether Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya

TheTools · 19.11.2026 11:28 · source · permalink

Summary

  • The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is delivering what is expected to be a landmark judgment on Myanmar, which is accused of committing genocide against its Rohingya minority

  • Delivering the judgement, Judge Yuji Iwasawa says there is a "reasonable inference that Myanmar security forces acted with genocidal intent", and Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya

  • The verdict hasn't been announced yet, with the judge going point-by-point through the ruling from The Hague - you can watch live here, external

  • In 2017, a military crackdown killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee Buddhist-majority Myanmar for neighbouring Bangladesh

  • Myanmar denies genocide in the case brought by The Gambia and says its forces were responding to attacks by armed groups

  • The ruling is being watched closely for any repercussions it might have on other genocide cases, including one brought by South Africa against Israel over the war in Gaza

  • No state has ever been found guilty by an international court of committing genocide

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) is delivering what is expected to be a landmark judgment on Myanmar, which is accused of committing genocide against its Rohingya minority

Delivering the judgement, Judge Yuji Iwasawa says there is a "reasonable inference that Myanmar security forces acted with genocidal intent", and Myanmar had the "intention to destroy" a substantial part of the Rohingya

The verdict hasn't been announced yet, with the judge going point-by-point through the ruling from The Hague - you can watch live here, external

In 2017, a military crackdown killed thousands of Rohingya and forced more than 700,000 to flee Buddhist-majority Myanmar for neighbouring Bangladesh

Myanmar denies genocide in the case brought by The Gambia and says its forces were responding to attacks by armed groups

The ruling is being watched closely for any repercussions it might have on other genocide cases, including one brought by South Africa against Israel over the war in Gaza

No state has ever been found guilty by an international court of committing genocide

    11:12 GMT

    Judge Iwasawa says Myanmar engaged in "extensive destruction of evidence" by burning bodies or disposing of them in other ways.

    It also burned and bulldozed sites where "clearance operations" took place, he says, obstructing any effective investigation into what happened in 2017.

  2. Court 'not fully convinced' Myanmar incited genocide on Facebookpublished at 11:05 GMT
    11:05 GMT

    The court considers that some Facebook military messages "sought to encourage the non-Rohingya population to engage in violence against the Rohingya as a group", the judge says.

    But the court does not think the posts "were made with a specific intent to destroy the group in whole or in part... or that the intended audience would have understood the messages as such".

    The court is therefore "not fully convinced" that Myanmar was "directly and publicly inciting genocide against the Rohingya by private actors".

  3. Myanmar has long said Rohingya aren't a distinct grouppublished at 11:00 GMT
    11:00 GMT

    Jonathan Head
    South East Asia correspondent

    Successive Myanmar governments have long insisted the Rohingya are not a distinct ethnic group, and therefore should be regarded as Bengali migrants from Bangladesh.

    The 1982 citizenship law recognised 135 ethnic groups as citizens but did not recognise Rohingyas as a distinct group, thus excluding them from citizenship.

    This resulted in them being subjected to a range of discriminations like freedom to travel, freedom to marry and freedom to register the birth of children.

    Rohingyas had enjoyed some recognition prior to the 1982 law. These days nearly all Rakhine Muslims self-identify as Rohingyas, and winning equal recognition to other Myanmar groups as citizens with equal rights has been a core Rohingya demand.

    Notably, ICJ judge Yuji Iwasawa identified the Rohingya as a distinct ethnic group when delivering his findings earlier today - noting that they have a distinct language, art and self-identify.

    There is evidence that they have been discriminated against by Myanmar "for decades", he added, and they qualify as a protected group.

  4. Myanmar intended to destroy substantial part of Rohingya group - judgepublished at 10:43 GMT
    10:43 GMT

    Judge Iwasawa says the scale and nature of Myanmar's actions and methods went "well beyond what would be required to punish the population and are instead characteristic of an intent to destroy a substantial part of the group".

    "The court is of the view that the only reasonable inference that can explain Myanmar's actions is an intention to destroy a substantial part" of northern Rakhine State's Rohingya population, he says.

  5. United Council of Rohingya 'very optimistic' about judge's commentspublished at 10:37 GMT
    10:37 GMT

    BBC Burmese Service

    Khin Maung, acting president of the United Council of Rohingya, says they are "very optimistic about what the judge has said so far".

    "The Court has been addressing the right issues and acknowledging what we have been suffering for such a long time," he says.

    "The judge’s statements have been very detailed and precise, reflecting the experiences and suffering we have endured," Maung adds. "We are now optimistic about the outcome of the case and believe that the Court will recognise the genocide committed against the Rohingya."

  6. Reasonable inference Myanmar forces acted with genocidal intent - judgepublished at 10:33 GMT
    10:33 GMT

    Judge Iwasawa Yuji says there is a "reasonable inference that Myanmar security forces acted with genocidal intent".

    "The context surrounding the 2017 clearance operations created an atmosphere that was conducive to the violence perpetrated during those clearance operations by Myanmar security forces, and formed the basis of Myanmar's intention to destroy part of the Rohingya group," he says.

    "The court concludes that all the evidence before it, taken together, supports a reasonable inference that Myanmar security forces acted with genocidal intent."

    Judge Iwasawa adds: "The court recalls that The Gambia bears the burden of establishing the alleged breaches of the genocide convention. This entails establishing that genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference that can be drawn from the evidence."

  8. A guilty ruling could have huge global repercussionspublished at 10:29 GMT
    10:29 GMT

    Anna Holligan
    Reporting from The Hague

    A judgment against Myanmar would clarify how the ICJ proves genocidal intent - the decisive legal element - especially where atrocities include sexual violence, destruction of homes and villages, and mass displacement rather than a single straightforward pattern of mass killing.

    Legal experts expect future parties to cite the court’s approach to circumstantial and indirect evidence.

    It would also reinforce the principle that any state that's bound by the Genocide Convention can bring a case against another state, even if its own nationals were not directly harmed.

    The most closely watched is South Africa’s case against Israel over the war in Gaza. Parties and judges there could draw on the Myanmar ruling’s treatment of intent, the weight given to UN fact-finding findings, and the relevance of sexual violence and attacks on women and children in establishing a genocidal campaign.

  10. Hate speech portrayed Rohingya as 'sub-human,' court hearspublished at 10:19 GMT
    10:19 GMT

    Judge Iwasawa is now speaking in more detail about anti-Rohingya hate-speech.

    The court found that the Myanmar military "actively disseminated and promoted hate speech in a systematic and coordinated way" during its 2017 clearance operations.

    The ICJ notes an "overlap" between an "anti-Rohingya hate speech campaign" and the clearance operations.

    Iwasawa says the hate speech portrayed the Rohingya as "sub-human" and "a violent threat" which contributed to a "climate conducive to targeting a group for destruction".

  11. Number of Rohingya targeted by Myanmar is 'significant'published at 10:10 GMT
    10:10 GMT

    The court finds that approximately 815,000 Rohingya who lived in the northern Rakhine state before the 2017 clearance operation were targeted by Myanmar.

    "This number is clearly significant," the judge says.

    Based on its size, the group targeted by Myanmar is considered "substantial" for the purposes of the Genocide Convention, he adds.

  12. Myanmar policies aimed to slow Rohingya birth rate, says judgepublished at 10:07 GMT
    10:07 GMT

    Judge Iwasawa has spoken of ways in which restrictions against the Rohingya group impacted birth rates.

    Iwasawa recalls that Myanmar imposed regional order which required "special marriage permission requirements for people described as Bengalis, and limited the number of children married couples were allowed to have" - to two children.

    While the policies didn't succeed in restricting Rohingya birth rate, Myanmar accepts that the measure's purpose was to slow the birth rate in this group, Iwasawa explains.

    "Under these circumstances, the court is convinced that the order was a coercive measure directed at the Rohingya group, which was intended at the very least to reduce birth within the group including at the time of the clearance operations", the judge adds.

    This, alongside other measures, are "indicative" of the state's intent to stop the Rohingya group from reproducing.

    He also cites the use of sexual violence during clearance operations, which he says were intended to "physically and mentally impair the willingness and ability of the population to procreate".

  13. A majority of judges is enough to make a decisionpublished at 09:57 GMT
    09:57 GMT

    Anna Holligan
    Reporting from The Hague

    We've been watching the judgment being announced by the ICJ's president, Yuji Iwasawa of Japan.

    He sits on a panel alongside 14 other elected judges from around the world. Because neither The Gambia nor Myanmar has a judge of its own nationality on the court, each has chosen an additional judge for this case: The Gambia selected Nobel laureate Navi Pillay, while Myanmar selected Claus Kress.

    Decisions are taken by majority vote; if the judges are evenly divided, the president has the casting vote.

  14. Judge outlines a state's obligation to prevent and punish genocidepublished at 09:54 GMT
    09:54 GMT

    ICJ Judge Iwasawa has turned to state parties' responsibilities to prevent and punish genocide.

    He says the court recognises that a state may be held responsible for genocide or other acts if such acts are committed "by its organs, or by persons or groups whose acts are attributable to it [the state]".

    While individuals face criminal responsibility, states face obligations, he points out.

    Judge Iwasawa says obligations are responsibilities under international law, but they are not of a criminal nature.

  17. Myanmar security forces killed 'thousands of Rohingya civilians', court findspublished at 09:40 GMT
    09:40 GMT

    Turning to events leading up to August 2017 "clearance operations", Judge Iwasawa says the ICJ "recognises Arsa's attacks on security outposts and civilians, including its burning of villages".

    As a reminder, Arsa is a Rohingya militant group.

    Iwasawa also says, however, that the evidence establishes "Myanmar's security forces engaged in indiscriminate killings, separation and execution of men, sexual violence, and the deliberate burning of Rohingya homes" in certain locations.

    The judge adds that the court finds Myanmar security forces "killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence, as well as acts of extreme violence against children and attacked fleeing Rohingya as they sought to flee in large numbers to Bangladesh".

  20. ICJ judge outlines how the court approached its verdictpublished at 09:21 GMT
    09:21 GMT

    Judge Iwasawa Yuji, President of the Court, is laying out the background to this case and how it has approached making a judgment.

    Judge Iwasawa explains that in this case the ICJ does not have comparable judicial decisions to support its findings, as it did in previous genocide rulings.

    Therefore, he says the ICJ "can only rely on the evidence provided by the parties to make its own determination of the facts".

    The court will consider witness testimonies and witness cases, the judge explains, including its form and the circumstances in which the statements were made.

    Fear for reprisals from those who gave evidence is another factor the court is taking into consideration, he adds.

    During oral proceedings, Iwasawa says the court heard from four witnesses who had experienced violence in the last week of August 2017.

    "The court found The Gambia's witnesses credible in their account of the particular events to which they testified," he says.

    "Many of the witnesses' key statements were unchallenged in cross-examination".
