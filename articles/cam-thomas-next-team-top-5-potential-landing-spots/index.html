Cam Thomas is currently without an NBA team despite averaging 24 points just two seasons ago. With minimal league interest, an overseas move to the EuroLeague or China could help rehabilitate his value. BasketNews explores five potential EuroLeague destinations for the 25-year-old scorer.

Only two seasons ago, Cam Thomas was averaging 24.0 points per game in the NBA. Now, at 25 years old, he is without a team.

Thomas' fall from Brooklyn Nets leading scorer to an unrestricted free agent with little apparent NBA interest has been one of the more overlooked stories of this year's free agency.

His talent with the ball has never really been questioned, as he has scored as many as 47 points in an NBA game and has recorded multiple 40-point performances.

During the 2024-25 season, he averaged 24.0 points, 3.8 assists, and 3.3 rebounds while shooting 43.8% from the field and 34.9% from deep.

But that season also revealed a key reason as to why his career has moved in the opposite direction since then: Thomas appeared in only 25 games because of hamstring problems.

A year later, his production dropped to 13.5 points across 42 games with Brooklyn and Milwaukee, before both teams eventually waived him.

More than seven months after his last NBA appearance, Thomas remains available.

On September 10, NBA reporter Evan Sidery reported that Thomas was receiving "little to no interest" as an unrestricted free agent and suggested that he might have to go overseas, potentially to the EuroLeague, to rehabilitate his NBA image.

If that happens, Thomas would immediately become one of the most fascinating players to cross the Atlantic in recent years. The question is: where could he realistically go?

At first glance, it is hard to understand how a 25-year-old who averaged 24 points per game just two seasons ago can find himself without an NBA contract.

The answer starts with what Thomas does beyond scoring.

There is little doubt about his ability to put the ball in the basket. Thomas can create his own shot, attack in isolation, and make difficult attempts late in possessions.

But he has never averaged more than 3.8 assists in an NBA season, is a career 34.0% three-point shooter, and has long faced questions about his defense and off-ball impact.

His size doesn't make things any easier. Thomas is listed at 6-foot-3 (1.91 m), compared to an NBA average of roughly 6-foot-7 (2.00 m) last season.

That matters in today's NBA. Teams value bigger, versatile guards who can survive defensive switches and guard multiple positions.

Being 6-foot-3 isn't a problem on its own, but smaller guards generally need elite playmaking, shooting, or scoring efficiency to compensate when opponents can target them defensively.

Thomas is an exceptional scorer, but the rest of his game hasn't consistently reached that level, and it became clear during his short stay with the Milwaukee Bucks.

After Brooklyn waived him in February, Milwaukee gave Thomas another opportunity. He averaged 10.7 points in 16.6 minutes over 18 games but shot only 27.5% from three.

When the Bucks needed to clear a roster spot, they waived Thomas and converted Pete Nance's two-way deal into a standard contract.

Nance couldn't match his shot-creation talent, but he could contribute without the ball and offered Milwaukee more size and defensive versatility.

Thomas had already made a major bet on himself before that season. In the summer of 2025, he reportedly turned down a two-year, $30 million proposal from Brooklyn, as well as a one-year offer worth $9.5 million with incentives that could have pushed it to $11 million.

Instead, Thomas accepted his $6 million qualifying offer and positioned himself to become an unrestricted free agent the following summer.

It clearly hasn't worked out. Brooklyn eventually waived him, while Milwaukee moved on less than two months after signing him.

Another league could make Thomas' biggest strength worth much more, and Europe has already provided a blueprint.

Carsen Edwards never came close to Thomas' NBA production. The former Celtics and Pistons guard played only 72 NBA games, averaging 3.7 points with a career-high of 18.

Thomas, meanwhile, has played 257 games, averaged 14.9 points, and has a career-high of 47.

Yet Edwards eventually crossed the Atlantic and found an environment where his aggressive scoring became an asset.

It didn't happen immediately. He averaged 8.0 points during his first EuroLeague season with Fenerbahce Istanbul before moving to FC Bayern Munich. But by his second year in Germany, everything had changed.

Edwards exploded for 20.4 points per game during the 2024-25 EuroLeague season and won the Alphonso Ford EuroLeague Top Scorer Trophy.

He followed that with 17.3 points for Virtus Bologna last season and is once again among the top 5 scorers after joining Zalgiris Kaunas.

Of course, this comparison isn't perfect.

Thomas is significantly bigger, has been much more productive in the NBA, and relies more on scoring inside the arc. But the career logic could become similar.

A player can be considered too one-dimensional for the NBA and still become an offensive superstar in Europe. In fact, Thomas' NBA resume suggests that his potential ceiling could be even higher.

If Edwards could go from averaging 3.7 points in the NBA to becoming a 20-point scorer in the EuroLeague, what could a player who has already averaged 24.0 points in the NBA do with similar freedom?

However, there is one major obstacle.

Having no NBA market doesn't automatically make Cam Thomas cheap. As previously mentioned, only a year ago, he turned down a potential $30 million commitment from Brooklyn, and he is still only 25 years old.

There is no reported asking price for him in Europe, so any figure would be an educated guess. Still, the current EuroLeague market provides some context.

The competition's biggest salaries have increased significantly in recent years.

Vasilije Micic earns around €5.6 million net per season, Nigel-Hayes Davis around €5.0 million, Kendrick Nunn around €4.5 million, and Guerschon Yabusele around €4 million.

Several other established EuroLeague stars are now around or above the €3 million mark.

Based on Thomas' age, NBA resume, and scoring ability, a reasonable estimate would put his potential asking price at least around €3 million net per season.

And money would only be part of the deal. If Europe is supposed to become his road back to the NBA, becoming the rotation player for a contender wouldn't make much sense.

He needs touches, he needs shots, and he needs a club willing to accept some of his weaknesses in exchange for taking a high-price, high-reward risk.

That salary and role combo reduces the number of realistic destinations. So, BasketNews identified five EuroLeague teams that could potentially make sense.

Eight EuroLeague games into the season, and Milan's expensive roster is already approaching the point where doing nothing might become more dangerous than taking another gamble.

They don't lack guards. Darius Thompson, Marko Guduric, Devon Hall, Leandro Bolmaro, R.J. Cole, and Garrison Mathews already give head coach Giuseppe Poeta plenty of perimeter options.

However, Milan has struggled to consistently find something different: individual creation when their offense stops functioning. That is essentially Cam Thomas' job description.

He is a top-level scorer, while Milan remains one of the EuroLeague organizations with the spending power and ambition to consider a player of Thomas' profile.

Simply put, Milan needs changes (probably big ones) to rescue the season after starting 1-7 in the EuroLeague.

This might be the most fascinating basketball experiment on the list.

Valencia doesn't desperately need another scorer, as the highest-scoring EuroLeague team with 97.5 points per game, but Cam Thomas could be the luxury that turns another strong season into something bigger.

After reaching the EuroLeague Final Four for the first time in club history last season, Valencia has started the new campaign with similar ambitions. They are 6-2 in the EuroLeague and once again look capable of competing near the top.

TJ Shorts has taken over as the main creator, while Armoni Brooks, Omari Moore, Kameron Taylor, Mario Saint-Supery, Alvaro Cardenas, and Josep Puerto give Xavi Albert considerable depth.

But competing deep into both the ACB and EuroLeague requires more than depth. It requires players who can decide games, and Thomas could become another solid option.

Valencia replaced Jean Montero with Shorts, and one can only imagine the fireworks a TJ-Cam duo could produce on the court.

Of course, it would be a major risk to disrupt the current offensive flow. Additionally, signing Thomas would likely require billionaire Juan Roig to approve another investment.

Real Madrid may not desperately need another guard, but Sergio Llull has yet to appear in EuroLeague action this season, Max Shulga has played just 5.5 minutes, and Nick Smith Jr. is still adapting to a completely different environment.

Thomas would give Pedro Martinez another proven shot creator alongside Facundo Campazzo and Theo Maledon.

Real Madrid also spent a long time monitoring Lonnie Walker IV, another former NBA scorer, before he eventually went elsewhere.

Thomas would offer a similar profile. He isn't as athletic as Walker, but he is arguably an even more gifted pure scorer and one-on-one isolation player.

His ability to attack quickly could also fit the faster-tempo basketball Martinez likes to play.

Another detail shouldn't be overlooked. Los Blancos need talent, but they also need well-known names and personalities who can change games and bring something different.

Maccabi might provide one of the clearest paths to immediate responsibility. And unlike several other teams on this list, they now have an additional reason to explore the market.

Iffe Lundberg recently suffered a partial tear in his quadriceps tendon and is expected to miss six to eight weeks.

That has already placed a heavier workload on Yam Madar and Jimmy Clark III, while Keaton Wallace and John DiBartolomeo remain part of the rotation.

Thomas would immediately give Maccabi another high-level scorer and reduce the burden on a backcourt currently being asked to absorb extra minutes.

There is also recent precedent: last season, Maccabi gave Lonnie Walker IV significant offensive freedom, and he responded with a strong EuroLeague campaign.

That performance helped Walker earn another NBA opportunity with the Denver Nuggets, even though the comeback ultimately did not work out.

Thomas could follow a similar path. He wouldn't have to arrive as a traditional point guard, so Maccabi could use him for what he does best: creating his own shot and scoring in bunches.

For a player trying to rebuild his NBA value, that combination of minutes, freedom, and visibility could make Maccabi one of the most logical destinations, especially since the team is 2-6 in the EuroLeague.

If the question is which EuroLeague club combines spending power and ambition, Dubai immediately rises to the top.

Their roster already shows how aggressively the organization is willing to invest, with Thomas Walkup being the latest example: Dubai reportedly paid around €2.3 million to buy him out from Olympiacos before signing him to a three-year deal worth roughly €3 million per season.

The team also has a strong NBA presence. Dwayne Bacon, Justin Anderson, and Davis Bertans have all spent significant time in the NBA, while several other players on the roster have experience on both sides of the Atlantic.

That could make Dubai a relatively comfortable landing spot for Thomas if he decides to leave the United States for the first time.

Availability is another factor. Dzanan Musa has just returned to the court, while Dubai have dealt with several other injuries.

For coach Xavi Pascual, Thomas would add yet another star to an already deep roster. Managing all those personalities and touches would not be simple, but his elite scoring could make the gamble well worth taking.

And financially, Dubai might be the easiest fit of all. Few EuroLeague teams could realistically meet Thomas' potential asking price, and Dubai is one of them.

The EuroLeague is not Thomas' only option, however. If his priorities are playing time, scoring volume, and money, the Chinese Basketball Association (CBA) could offer an easier path.

China has long been a welcoming destination for former NBA scorers, especially guards given the freedom to dominate the ball and take a high volume of shots.

Jimmer Fredette became one of the CBA's most prolific scorers after leaving the NBA, while Joe Young averaged more than 38 points per game in 2019-20.

More recently, Edmond Sumner averaged 36.0 points with Sichuan in 2024-25 after playing 161 NBA games with Indiana and Brooklyn.

Current Hapoel Tel Aviv guard Antonio Blakeney followed a similar path, averaging 32.7 points in 2022-23 and 30.5 the following season after previously playing for the Chicago Bulls.

This route is still relevant today. Former NBA All-Star D'Angelo Russell became the latest notable guard to move to China when he signed with the Shanghai Sharks in September.

For Thomas, that environment would make plenty of sense. He could get the ball, take a large number of shots, and immediately become one of the league's most dangerous scorers.

The downside is that dominating in China would not necessarily answer the questions NBA teams currently have about his game. EuroLeague dominance could certainly open more doors and put him in a better spotlight.
