Put $300,000 into the Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF (NYSEARCA:SCHD), and it is estimated to pay out $9,604 a year. Put the same money into the Schwab U.S. Large-Cap Growth ETF (NYSEARCA:SCHG) and the estimate falls to $1,220. The result is a gap of $8,384 in cash every year. Most income investors compare these two numbers and decide right there.

Use a different measuring stick, and you get the opposite answer. From October 13, 2021 to October 13, 2026, SCHG returned 98.84% on a dividend-adjusted basis. SCHD returned 58.24%. On the same $300,000, SCHG finished $121,800 ahead. The two comparisons point in opposite directions, with the same two funds and the same money.

How These Figures Were Calculated

The income figures are a forward estimate. They take each fund's current annualized distribution rate, $1.066 per share for SCHD and $0.1484 for SCHG, and apply it to share prices of $33.31 and $36.51. What the funds actually pay could differ. Both return figures are dividend-adjusted, so each fund's distributions are already inside its return number. Nothing is counted twice.

One-Year Returns Break the Usual Trade-Off

From October 10, 2025 to October 13, 2026, SCHD returned 29.85% on a dividend-adjusted basis. SCHG returned 16.67%. On $300,000, the dividend fund finished $39,540 ahead.

Costs That Return Charts Leave Out

Much of the gap comes down to what each fund has. As of May 31, 2026, NVIDIA (NASDAQ:NVDA) alone made up 11.01% of SCHG's net assets. SCHD's largest position, QUALCOMM (NASDAQ:QCOM), was 6.74%. A small group of technology giants drives much of SCHG's results.

Each fund also has a cost that never shows up on a return chart. A retiree who needs $9,604 a year from SCHG would have to sell shares to cover the $8,384 difference. Selling during a downturn means selling shares at low prices, and those sales can trigger capital gains if the account is taxable. SCHD's cost works the other way. For taxable accounts, its distributions are taxed in the year they are paid, whether the holder spends them or reinvests them.

Each Window Tells Only Part of the Story

Over five years, SCHG delivered far more total value while paying a much smaller distribution. Over one year, SCHD delivered more of both. Both windows are accurate, but each one is incomplete. If you chose between these funds based on whichever window you saw first, timing made the decision for you.

Pick Your Measuring Stick Before You Pick a Fund

Start with one question: do you need cash out of this portfolio? If you draw income to pay bills, you have a real reason to value the distribution, whatever either fund returned last year. An estimated $9,604 payout comes without selling a single share. That keeps your withdrawals separate from market timing (we laid out how to build a dividend ladder that funds retirement without touching the shares in a free guide here).

If you reinvest everything, you are choosing on total return alone, so check more than one window. On an adjusted-price basis from October 14, 2016 to October 12, 2026, SCHG rose 474.27%, and SCHD rose 236.34%. Put the one-year, five-year, and ten-year figures side by side. One window can mislead you, and several give you a fuller picture.

Determine the cash question first, then use return data to refine the choice. Investors who work in the opposite order let the time period they happen to review decide their fund. Whichever measuring stick you use decides the answer, so pick it on purpose before you compare the funds.
