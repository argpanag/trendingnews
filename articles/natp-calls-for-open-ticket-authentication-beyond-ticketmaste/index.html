NATP Calls for Open Ticket Authentication Beyond Ticketmaster’s Selected Partners

TheTools · 05.11.2026 17:33 · source · permalink

Home »

The National Association of Ticketing Professionals (NATP) is urging lawmakers to consider policies that would give qualified ticket resale marketplaces broader access to authentication technology, arguing that consumers should be able to verify and receive legitimate tickets regardless of where they buy them.

In a Nov. 3 commentary, Zach Anderson, writing for the professional ticket resale trade association, points to Ticketmaster’s partnerships with CashorTrade as evidence that independent platforms can securely authenticate tickets through their original issuers. NATP wants that capability extended beyond individually approved commercial arrangements.

The proposal combines three objectives: prompt delivery of tickets that are available for secure transfer, fewer unnecessary account and personal-data requirements, and interoperable systems that allow qualified marketplaces to verify a ticket’s authenticity and status. It is an industry advocacy proposal, not an announced bill or a new legal requirement.

Existing Integrations Show What Is Possible

In April, Ticketmaster announced an integration with CashorTrade that allows eligible concert tickets to be listed on the independent fan-to-fan marketplace at face value or below. Ticketmaster’s systems authenticate the tickets, and completed sales result in new ticket credentials being issued to buyers within the CashorTrade app.

The model expanded Oct. 30 when CashorTrade announced a similar integration with Front Gate Tickets, the Ticketmaster-owned festival ticketing provider. That arrangement verifies the original purchase price, including fees, and supports authenticated resale and ticket reissuance for eligible festival passes.

Ticketmaster has presented the arrangements as a way to add secure resale options while respecting artist pricing preferences. In its April announcement, David Marcus, the company’s executive vice president of music, said, “Fans deserve more ways to buy and sell tickets at the original price, with confidence that what they’re getting is legitimate.”

NATP welcomes the technology but questions why the same authentication capability should depend on a marketplace’s pricing policies or a separate agreement with the issuer. CashorTrade is restricted to face-value-or-below transactions, while other legitimate resale marketplaces permit higher prices where lawful.

“The price of a ticket has nothing to do with whether it is authentic,” Anderson wrote. NATP’s position is that determining whether a ticket is genuine should be separate from decisions about resale prices.

Ticketmaster also has experience supporting verification across multiple resale platforms in professional sports. Its new NFL agreement, announced Oct. 21, is designed to expand distribution of verified season-ticket resale inventory to participating marketplaces. That is an authorized, league-specific network rather than the broader open-access standard NATP is proposing, but it offers another example of issuer-backed resale verification beyond a single storefront.

A Policy Debate Over Transfer Rights and Marketplace Access

NATP’s proposed reforms also address the period between purchase and entry. The group argues that buyers should receive tickets promptly once those tickets are available for secure transfer, rather than face unexplained delays, and should not have to create unnecessary accounts with a different ticketing company simply to receive tickets they already purchased.

Delayed delivery and controlled transfers can serve legitimate security or event-management purposes. NATP’s proposal does not eliminate the need for safeguards. Instead, it asks policymakers to distinguish necessary controls from restrictions that burden consumers or give the original issuer leverage over transactions occurring on competing platforms.

Those concerns have become more prominent after Ticketmaster’s Oct. 2 transfer disruption. As TicketNews reported, the Ticket Policy Forum subsequently asked state attorneys general to investigate the impact on buyers and independent marketplaces. No evidence has established that Ticketmaster intentionally favored its own transactions during the incident, but it illustrated how resale fulfillment can depend on infrastructure controlled by a competing ticket seller.

The broader market-access question also appears in the Justice Department’s proposed Live Nation-Ticketmaster antitrust settlement. The proposal includes open-distribution provisions for qualifying primary ticketing providers, while TicketNetwork has challenged resale-related eligibility conditions. NATP’s request is different: it seeks nondiscriminatory authentication and transfer access for qualified resale platforms, not merely the ability to distribute newly issued primary tickets.

Making that vision operational would require more detail. NATP calls for fair, objective technical standards, but its commentary does not specify who would certify a qualified marketplace, how verification costs would be allocated or what access providers would have to sensitive ticket data. The association acknowledges that any system would need privacy protections, cybersecurity safeguards and controls against unauthorized transfers.

No specific legislation implementing NATP’s proposed framework was identified in the commentary. The immediate question for policymakers and ticket issuers is whether authentication can become a broadly available consumer-protection function without compromising security or giving the original issuer final discretion over which competing marketplaces may participate.
