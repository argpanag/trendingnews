Beverage container returns as a side hustle? It's a good thing

TheTools · 30.09.2026 06:30 · source · permalink
  • Report this article

The Straits Times

,

By Audrey Tan

After a few teething challenges, Singapore’s beverage container return scheme will be fully implemented from Oct 1. 

Under the scheme, consumers will pay an extra 10 cents for bottled and canned drinks ranging from 150ml to 3 litres.They will get the deposit back when they return empty beverage containers that carry the scheme’s deposit mark at designated return points across the island.  

The machines at which people can return eligible beverage containers have been operational in Singapore since April. But their use only became more prevalent recently, when more beverages carrying the requisite logos and barcodes started appearing on shelves here. 

The authorities had granted drink retailers and producers a six-month transition period between April and September for them to clear old stock that do not have the required logos. 

From Oct 1, it will be an offence for anyone, including supermarket operators, to sell beverages without the required deposit mark and barcode. Those found guilty can face fines of up to $10,000 and/or up to three months’ jail.

Now that more drinks are carrying the requisite logos, queues have been observed at some machines. I recently spotted a group of students carrying large bags of empty beverage containers, and returning them one by one at a machine opposite my office. 

News outlets have been profiling people who have made beverage container collection a side hustle. They go around collecting cans and bottles to “earn” some money after returning them in these machines. 

This has prompted waves of commentary on whether this is an unintended consequence of such a scheme, and if there are better ways to encourage recycling instead.

But is it so wrong for people to be financially motivated to participate in this scheme? 

Those who want their refunds can return the containers at the over 1,000 machines islandwide, and I find that they are quite a ubiquitous sight near supermarkets and hawker centres now. 

But, there are some who may find it a hassle to do so, and would willingly forgo the 10-cent deposit. So if there is someone else who is willing to pick up these drink containers that would otherwise be discarded, this actually achieves the desired outcomes of this policy. 

It forces people to engage in “clean stream recycling”, where materials are sorted and returned to designated points, with little contamination. 

Without this scheme, recyclables including these cans and bottles would usually be deposited in the commingled blue recycling bins. A huge problem with these bins is that they are often contaminated. Once someone throws in food waste, for instance, it contaminates the entire bin of recyclables, which then have to be incinerated. Official figures put contamination rates here at about 40%. 

This contamination issue becomes less of a challenge when bottles and cans are returned at the reverse vending machines. Shabana Begum D/O Nazeer explains the step-by-step process of what takes place at the sorting facility.

In August, it was revealed in Parliament that 5.5 million used bottles and cans had been dropped off at reverse vending machines islandwide. Within the BCRS’ first year, the aim is to have 60% of bottles and cans in the market returned for recycling. Within three years, the goal is to reach an 80% return rate. The scheme is expected to recover more than one billion beverage containers used each year and over 16,000 tonnes of material a year. 

Dangling financial incentives or penalties to change behaviour is often frowned upon. Yet, statistics show that they do work. This was the case when we implemented a charge for disposable bags, and when fines for not returning trays at hawker centres and coffee shops were imposed. 

Ultimately, Singapore should work toward instilling more environmentally-friendly behaviour in its citizens. But until then, true behavioural change may require other incentives.
