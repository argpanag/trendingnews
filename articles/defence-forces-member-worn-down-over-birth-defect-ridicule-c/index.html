Defence Forces member ‘worn down’ over birth-defect ridicule complaint, tribunal told

TheTools · 29.10.2026 21:44 · source · permalink

A member of the Defence Forces was grilled by his commanding officer until he dropped a complaint about being “ridiculed” by a training officer over a birth deformity, a tribunal has heard.

The tribunal was established following a 2021 RTÉ Radio documentary, Women of Honour, which alleged sexism, bullying and the inadequate investigation of sexual assaults in the Defence Forces.

Sgt Gerard Guinan, the general secretary of the representative body for enlisted members, PDFORRA, told the tribunal he was approached by a member of the Defence Forces in the early 2010s who had a birth deformity to one of his fingers.

The unnamed member alleged his training officer had forcibly bent his finger to “make it straight” and made “derogatory remarks” which Guinan was unwilling to repeat to the tribunal.

He “felt ridiculed” and sought assistance in submitting a complaint, which Guinan – who has legal qualifications – helped him to draft using “legalistic” language.

Guinan told the tribunal the individual was then questioned so closely by his commanding officer on the wording of the complaint that he had to repeatedly call PDFORRA for assistance in explaining what exactly he meant by “force” and other legal terms used.

His commanding officer “wore him down” to such a degree that the complainant “couldn’t take it any more” and took the issue to the Military Police, Guinan said.

The unnamed member was “warned” about “breaching the chain of command” and the complaint was abandoned.

The tribunal also heard there is pressure to handle complaints “informally”.

Col Derek Priestley, deputy general secretary of the Representative Association of Commissioned Officers (Raco), said there is “always pressure” on those handling complaints to “keep it informal [and] move it along as quickly as possible”.

He said “even in the area of abuse, there is a certain consideration [given] to try and resolve the matter informally, and that slows things down”.

Officers were often viewing procedures for the first time when charged with investigating complaints, which could involve sexual assault or abuse.

Priestley said the regulations outlining the complaints processes are “sterile” and “not very practical”, adding that investigators would approach the association for help.

He said there was no training in how to conduct investigations until a pilot programme began in 2024.

Raco also provides advice to alleged victims of abuse and discrimination. Priestley said the complaints processes can be “confusing” to those unfamiliar with them, which leads to a “lack of trust or willingness to engage”.

He said the biggest problem for those making complaints related to timelines, concerns over the impartiality of the process and a lack of feedback to the complainant.

“You would frequently hear ‘I don’t know where this complaint is, it has gone on for years’,” he said.

He said the opacity of the process meant that even the officer charged with investigating a complaint would not hear about the outcome and would not get feedback.

Priestley noted that in the last “five or six years”, there has been “significant developments” and “improvement” in the Defence Forces in advising members of appropriate behaviour and outlining the processes for making complaints.

The association was so impressed by the handling of a recent complaint externally that it now considers it a “benchmark” and is seeking to externalise the investigations into all serious complaints.

PDFORRA holds the same view that the complaints system needs to be given to an “independent body” to investigate complaints.

Guinan said there is a “commonly held opinion or perception” that there could be retribution for filing complaints. He noted, however, that the “culture in the Defence Forces is starting to change, that complaints will be taken seriously”.

The tribunal is examining whether the complaints processes in the Defence Forces are fit for purpose and whether victims of abuse who made complaints faced punishments or reprisals. Its inquiry covers a 41-year period ending in June 2024.
