It's costing you less to fill up your gas tank. What's behind falling pump prices? | CBC News

TheTools · 19.11.2026 20:14 · source · permalink

National average gas prices about 28 cents below the national average from 30 days ago

No, your eyes aren't deceiving you — those sky-high gas prices are coming down across Canada.

The Canadian national average for gas prices sat at $1.514 per litre for Thursday, according to price tracking site affordableenergy.ca. That's down about seven cents compared to a week ago, and about 28 cents below the national average from 30 days ago, according to the tracking site.

In some provinces like Manitoba and Saskatchewan, those average prices are down by more than 30 cents compared to last month's average, according to GasBuddy petroleum analyst Patrick De Haan.

"In fact, in some areas of Calgary, we are seeing gas stations … below a dollar a litre again," De Haan said. 

He says prices could still fall by another five or 10 cents a litre countrywide over the next few weeks — so long as there are no new developments with the U.S. war in Iran, which at some points all but shuttered traffic through the vital Strait of Hormuz, sending oil skyrocketing.

While gas prices are still higher than they were a year ago, they have recently been brought down largely due to more oil moving through the strait, as well as some seasonal factors, analysts say.

Tanker traffic through the strait has been ticking up in recent months, aided by U.S. military escorts through the waterway, according to Al Salazar, head of oil and gas research at Enverus. That has helped crude oil prices come down — a barrel of Brent crude oil is now priced just under $92 US, compared to about $116 US at a high point in mid-October.

Gas prices are pictured at 222.9¢ a litre at a gas station in New Westminster, B.C., on May 1. The oil shock brought on by the war in Iran has sent gas prices way up in recent months, in some cases far past $2 a litre. (Ben Nelms/CBC)

"The U.S. military, prior to the midterms, was really [helping] improve and defend the ships getting through the strait. So that's led to a bit of a glut," Salazar said.

The International Energy Agency also said last week that the world oil market could return to a surplus in the fourth quarter, as more oil is able to make it out of the Middle East.

Meanwhile, U.S. President Donald Trump himself has taken a de-escalatory tone on the war. And expectations that Democrats could use their newfound power picked up in the midterms to block new strikes on Iran, encouraging oil prices to fall lower, Salazar said.

Seasonal factors are also giving the industry a lot of "breathing room," according to De Haan.

The seasonal switch to the cheaper "winter blend" gas is in effect, and refinery maintenance associated with the swap is wrapping up. Maintenance at the Irving Oil plant in New Brunswick was slated to finish yesterday, while De Haan adds that more production at refineries in the prairies is contributing to the big pump price drops in Manitoba and Saskatchewan.

Here’s why you’re paying more for diesel

There's also the fact that Canadians tend to drive less in the winter as they hunker down in the colder months, helping with demand.

And while the cost of diesel — which had been pressuring industries like trucking, farming and fishing — has also eased, relief on that front might be more short-lived, said Dan McTeague, president of the Canadians for Affordable Energy lobby group.

Diesel is similar to jet fuel and home heating oil — and the latter will be in higher demand as temperatures drop heading into winter, McTeague said.

"[With] diesel, this is probably as good as it's going to get at the end of this week," he told CBC's Hanomansing Live earlier this week.

De Haan predicts lower gasoline prices will last into early 2027, but notes some of that "artificial drop" will disappear when the suspended fuel excise tax comes back. The tax will return at a 50 per cent rate at the end of January 2027, before returning to its full amount at the end of March.

And, De Haan and Salazar both say costs could always soar again if tensions flare with Iran. Especially since strikes on ships in the strait have already risen again over the past few days, Salazar expects the relief at the pump might not last long.

"It really is a war call. And for us, it just doesn't seem like there's a durable solution to preventing the Iranians from attacking ships," Salazar said.

"So take the swoon in prices to your advantage temporarily, but I suspect that we're going to have higher prices to come still."
