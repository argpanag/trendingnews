Satellite photos reveal Russia's "Satan II" nuke may have entered service

TheTools · 26.11.2026 04:52 · source · permalink

Reporter

Russia's long-awaited "Satan II" nuclear missile may finally be entering service, according to a new analysis by the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), which examined satellite images of Russian military facilities.

The RS-28 Sarmat, nicknamed the "Satan II," is a nuclear-armed intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM), designed to carry out devastating strikes across continents.

The weapon, which Russia has long touted as a powerful addition to its nuclear arsenal, has had its development plagued by delays and technical issues, with several tests ending in failure.

But recent satellite images, captured by satellite image company Vantor and published by the IISS, appear to show activity around a missile silo in Russia. This suggests the long-delayed missile may finally be entering service.

What Do the Satellite Images Show?

A satellite image taken in June shows equipment positioned around a silo at a missile base in Uzhur, Siberia, where the 62nd Missile Division of Russia’s Strategic Rocket Forces is based.

While the images do not directly capture the Sarmat, the IISS analyzed the types of equipment and activity seen, which it says suggest the missile may have been loaded into the silo.

The satellite imagery appears to show a transloader, which is a specialized vehicle used to transport missile sections into a silo. The vehicle was pictured with its loading platform raised vertically but no missile section attached, suggesting it may already have been loaded into the silo.

The imagery also shows a separate vehicle with a red circular structure, similar to protective covers previously seen on Russian missile sections during transport. Its removal could further suggest that a missile section has been loaded.

The report also points to a crane seen near the silo, a tool which has previously been used to install payloads into other Russian missiles. Its presence could suggest that a payload, potentially nuclear warheads, has been installed in the Sarmat.

The Sarmat has had two successful tests—in 2022 and May 2026—with multiple failed launches in between. Russia has previously put ICBMs into service after three successful test launches.

It is unclear whether Russia has conducted a third successful launch of the Sarmat or has moved ahead with deploying it without one.

The IISS report also examined satellite imagery from October, which shows renewed activity around a separate missile silo at Dombarovsky, in southern Russia, which was the site of Sarmat's second successful test.

The equipment could indicate that Russia has either already conducted, or is preparing to conduct, another test launch of the Sarmat, the IISS noted, although the images do not confirm whether a launch has taken place.

What To Know About Russia's Satan II Missile

The Sarmat missile has been in development for more than a decade, and has become a prominent feature of Russian propaganda as a symbol of the country’s military strength.

Russian President Vladimir Putin showcased the weapon in 2018 as one of several new weapons that he said could overcome even the most advanced Western missile defense systems.

While the exact capabilities of the Sarmat have not been demonstrated, Western analysts have estimated it could have a maximum range of about 11,000 miles—putting most of Europe and the United States within reach.

Russia has previously said the Sarmat can carry up to 15 independently targeted nuclear warheads, allowing one missile to strike multiple targets. State media said in 2016 that the weapon could wipe out targets "the size of Texas or France."

Bringing the Sarmat into service is particularly important for Russia now, as it grapples with an aging fleet of intercontinental ballistic missiles, the IISS report noted.

The Sarmat’s potential deployment comes as tensions remain high between Russia and the West over its war in Ukraine.

However, analysts have previously noted that the Sarmat will not substantially change Russia’s attack capabilities, as it already has had missiles that could travel similar distances.

John Erath, senior policy director for the Center for Arms Control and Non-Proliferation, previously told The Washington Post that the biggest concern was not the weapon’s capabilities, but Putin’s rhetoric.

"We should be very concerned about this practice of making threats as an instrument of Russian policy [that] is gaining currency," Erath told the outlet in 2022.
