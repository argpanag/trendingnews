Healey draws up ‘green tax’ on petrol

TheTools · 26.10.2026 03:55 · source · permalink

Fears of more pain at the pumps as Chancellor considers ‘carbon charge’ on imported fuel

John Healey has drawn up plans to announce a “green tax” on imported fuel in next week’s Budget, prompting fears that petrol prices could rise, The Telegraph can reveal.

The Chancellor is considering a so-called “carbon charge” on refined products, which could include petrol and diesel imports.

Under current rules, British refineries face millions of pounds in net-zero expenses that their competitors do not, allowing imported fuel to undercut local industry.

But the proposed “carbon border adjustment mechanism” (CBAM) would subject imported fuel to some or the same net zero costs as domestically refined fuel, and would aim to “level the playing field”.

Fuel duty has been frozen until Dec 31, 2026, meaning motorists would not be imminently affected by an expansion of CBAM. However, it is unclear whether the Government will extend the fuel duty freeze into next year.

Opponents worry the carbon charge would then trickle down to consumers and lead to higher prices at the pump.

John O’Connell, chief executive of the TaxPayers’ Alliance, said the carbon charges would “be a shameless tax raid disguised as environmental policy”.

He said: “Levying carbon charges on imported fuel is little more than a tariff that will inflate pump prices and drive up supply chain costs for ordinary families.

“Burnham must rule out introducing new punitive green taxes in the Budget.”

James Murray, the paymaster general, said in a response to a parliamentary written question on Friday that the Government was “considering the feasibility and impacts” of using CBAM on refined products “in the future”, the strongest public signal of the Government’s support for the policy.

He said: “The Government recognises the important role of the UK refining sector in supporting energy security and the UK’s industrial base.

“As confirmed at Budget 2025, the Government is considering the feasibility and impacts of including refined products within the scope of CBAM in the future.”

Miatta Fahnbulleh, the Energy Secretary, was thought to have been supportive of expanding CBAM and was preparing to make the case for the tax directly to Mr Healey earlier this week.

On Saturday, Andy Haldane, a former chief economist at the Bank of England, said he feared that the Prime Minister may not be able to make the “hard choices” needed in the Budget.

He told The Times: “My concern is will those hard choices actually be made? Will the political capital – which is sky high now for Andy – be used to make the tough choices around public finances?”

Mr Healey is facing significant financial headwinds. Government debt has exceeded £3tn, with the Treasury spending £8.8bn servicing interest on it in August alone.

The UK imported £13.1bn of refined oil in the 12 months to the end of August 2026. According to the Government’s website, refineries produce a range of products including petrol, diesel, jet fuel and fuel oil, for domestic demand and export.

Gas prices hit their highest level since December 2022 on Tuesday, and energy bills are expected to rise by hundreds of pounds this winter because of the war in Iran and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

Motorists are already in the crosshairs of the Chancellor, who is also considering a new tax on family SUVs in his maiden Budget next week.

Robert Jenrick, Reform UK’s treasury spokesman, described the potential expansion of CBAM in this context as “an act of self-harm” and “the last thing our country needs”.

He added: “Instead of scraping around for tax rises, Healey should grow a backbone and cut the ballooning benefits bill.

“Reform would cut £40bn pounds of waste so we can scrap the fuel duty rise and give families £1,300 in tax relief.”

However, Elizabeth de Jong, the chief executive of Fuels Industry UK, which represents companies involved in refining, renewable fuel production, terminal operations and petrol stations, said: “It’s so essential to our energy security that we make the things we need here.

“Without that level playing field there is both carbon leakage and investment leakage. We want to make sure investment is coming to the UK rather than going abroad.”

The Government had previously announced plans to introduce CBAM on imported fertiliser in January. Farmers warned the move will have a significant effect on the industry as they face the worst harvest on record after the summer drought.

On Wednesday, Ms Fahnbulleh granted approval for the Jackdaw gas field after months of delays, marking a strong shift in the government’s climate policy. Pressure is mounting on the Energy Secretary to also approve the Rosebank oil fields to shore up domestic supply and reduce costs.

An HM Treasury spokesman said: “As has always been the case, we do not comment on rumour, speculation or proposals ahead of the Budget.”
