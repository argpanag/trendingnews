BFU report: Lufthansa A380 touches down 19 meters short of the runway in Munich

TheTools · 23.10.2026 08:31 · source · permalink

The Federal Bureau of Aircraft Accident Investigation (BFU) has presented a first interim report on a serious incident in commercial scheduled air traffic at Munich Airport.

On August 17, 2026, a Lufthansa Airbus A380-800 wide-body aircraft, operating flight LH 459 from San Francisco, touched down 19 meters short of the threshold of the southern runway 26L. The hard impact, with a vertical acceleration of 2,1 G, damaged seven main landing gear tires and three runway approach lights. No one was injured in the incident, and the aircraft was subsequently able to taxi to the terminal. The investigation report by the Braunschweig investigating authority examines the meteorological conditions surrounding a summer thunderstorm, the failure of precision approach systems, and the cockpit crew's handling of barometrically referenced navigation aids.

Failure of the instrument landing system and instrument approach procedure under FLS leadership

The reconstruction of the flight path shows that the approach to runway 26L at Munich Airport was hampered by infrastructural limitations. According to the Notices to Airmen (NOTAMs) available to the pilots, the Instrument Landing System (ILS) glide path was not available on this runway. Under these conditions, the operating manual specifies the use of a Required Navigation Performance (RNP) approach procedure with the aid of the Flight Management System Landing System (FLS). The FLS system generates a virtual glide path in the onboard computer based on navigation data and barometric measurements.

For the approach under FLS guidance, a published decision measurement minimum of 1.810 feet above mean sea level (AMSL) was in effect that day, which corresponds to approximately 340 feet (around 103 meters) above the airfield elevation. The prescribed minimum visibility was 800 meters. During the downwind and crosswind legs, the parallel northern runway 26R was visually identifiable to the cockpit crew, whereas runway 26L was obscured by low-hanging clouds and the remnants of a thunderstorm. The crew only noticed the approach lights for runway 26L approximately 10 feet before reaching the decision height.

Manual control in the final phase and warning in the cockpit

After establishing visual contact, the first officer, as pilot in flight, continued the approach. Shortly after passing the decision point, he disengaged the autopilot to manually pilot the aircraft for landing. The crew estimated the prevailing visibility at that time to be approximately 500 meters or less, which was below the required guideline. During the final descent, the aircraft exhibited a steeper flare than anticipated.

Two seconds before physical contact with the ground, the captain realized the aircraft was too low above the terrain and verbally warned the co-pilot that the approach was too low. However, the rate of descent was not reduced in time to prevent touchdown before reaching the paved runway. The main landing gear struck the concrete barrier 19 meters short of the runway threshold, damaging parts of the runway end lights.

Influence of barometric pressure fluctuations on FLS profile calculation

A key technical aspect of the BFU investigation concerns the functionality of the FLS guidance system and its coupling to the barometric altimeter setting (QNH). Unlike a conventional ILS glide path, which is defined by fixed radio signals on the ground and operates unaffected by air pressure fluctuations, the FLS system calculates the vertical approach profile barometrically based on the air pressure values ​​entered in the cockpit.

The crew of D-AIML had set the approach to the meteorological reference value of 1.015 hPa used in the Automatic Information Service (ATIS). Immediately after the incident, the airport's weather station published an updated QNH value of 1.014 hPa. In aviation practice, a deviation of 1 hPa in the barometer setting translates to a shift of approximately 30 feet (around 9 meters) up or down in the calculated vertical profile. Therefore, passing through a local pressure change during thunderstorms can produce discrepancies between the indicated target altitude and the actual altitude above ground.

Airline operating instructions and safety recommendations

In direct response to the findings of the BFU interim report, Deutsche Lufthansa has made adjustments to its standard operating procedures for its pilots. A fleet directive for the Airbus A320, A350, and A380 aircraft mandates that, during approaches using FLS guidance, both the Autoflight System and Flight Director Guidance must be completely deactivated once the published approach minimum is reached. This is intended to prevent visual instructions from the onboard systems from causing pilots to descend too low in visual flight rules (VFR) situations due to barometric deviations.

This measure is in place as a precautionary measure until the system architecture and risk assessment have been finalized in coordination with the aircraft manufacturer, Airbus. The final report from the Federal Bureau of Aircraft Accident Investigation is still pending and will include a detailed analysis of the flight data recording devices as well as recommendations for international flight operations.
