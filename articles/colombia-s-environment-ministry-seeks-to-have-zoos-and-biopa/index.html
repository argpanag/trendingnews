Colombia’s Environment Ministry Seeks to Have Zoos and Bioparks Take in Hippos

TheTools · 24.11.2026 23:50 · source · permalink

-

One week after it was announced that euthanasia would begin to be applied to an initial group of ten hippos in Colombia, with the aim of starting to control the uncontrolled expansion of this invasive species, the Ministry of the Environment opened another option to avoid ending the lives of these animals.

The ministry, which oversees environmental management and renewable natural resources and is responsible for defining policies and regulations for the country’s sustainable development, invited 13 zoos and bioparks in the country to receive some specimens at their facilities and called a working group meeting for next November 27, with the purpose of identifying relocation sites.

The initiative, supported by recommendations from the Colombian Academy of Exact, Physical and Natural Sciences, seeks to address this problem with rigor, responsibility, and coordinated work among all sectors.

Native Species Threatened by Hippos

In this regard, the Ministry of the Environment reported that it will work hand in hand with the entities willing to participate in the relocation process to seek financial and logistical mechanisms that will make it possible to carry it out.

According to scientific projections, there are around 260 hippos in Colombia, whose presence is concentrated mainly in the Middle Magdalena region. If effective measures are not implemented to control their expansion, the population could exceed 1,200 specimens in less than 10 years, which would represent a growing threat to biodiversity, ecosystems, and communities.

At least seven native species, some of them endemic, face different levels of risk due to the presence of hippos:

1. Manatee (Trichechus manatus): endangered.

2. Neotropical otter (Lontra longicaudis): vulnerable.

3. Capybara (Hydrochoerus isthmius): vulnerable.

4. Deer (Cervidae family): under study.

5. Magdalena River turtle (Podocnemis lewyana): critically endangered.

6. Bocachico (Prochilodus magdalenae): vulnerable.

7. Striped catfish (Pseudoplatystoma magdaleniatum): critically endangered.

It is also estimated that around 200 species of aquatic plants could be affected by the deterioration of water quality associated with the accumulation of organic matter and the proliferation of bacteria derived from the feces and other activities of hippos in the region’s aquatic ecosystems.

Given this scenario, the Ministry is moving forward with the implementation of the national plan, which includes different management and control measures. The Colombian Academy of Exact, Physical and Natural Sciences recommended strengthening it as a long-term program, with annual population control targets, a budget, and scientific monitoring.

“We are making decisions with scientific rigor, combining capabilities and calling on all sectors that can contribute solutions. Our responsibility is to implement all viable alternatives, monitor their results, and ensure that decisions are made on a technical basis,” said Fabio Arjona, Minister of the Environment and Sustainable Development, quoted in a statement from the ministry.

He added that the entire plan is part of the ABC Agenda: conserving water, which can be seriously affected by this invasive species; preserving native biodiversity, such as manatees, otters, and fish, some of these species critically endangered; and safeguarding the lives and peace of mind of communities that are also at risk.

Carriers and Transmitters of Diseases

Hippos are a species native to Africa. They are the third-largest land mammals in the world and have an average lifespan of 50 years. In Colombia, they are distributed across an estimated area of 43,342 square kilometers, especially in the Magdalena River basin and the swamp complexes of the Momposina Depression.

In addition to the damage to biodiversity, hippos are carriers and transmitters of tuberculosis, paratuberculosis, and brucellosis, among other diseases.

Additionally, they act as vectors for humans and domestic animals, as well as for native species whose distribution areas overlap with theirs, for example, through the contamination of aquatic ecosystems.

However, the issue that prompted the decision to begin applying euthanasia to some of them as part of a pilot plan is the situations of conflict with humans.

The presence of this invasive species has given rise to a new type of interaction for people, generating emotions and impressions of empathy or rejection, as well as incidents involving threats or attacks that put people’s peace of mind, lives, and livelihoods at risk.

In this regard, the coexistence of hippos with communities exacerbates preexisting environmental and social conflicts in the ecosystem and generates environmental, social, and economic vulnerability.
