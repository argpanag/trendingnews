How the Richest-Country Rankings Have Changed

Key Takeaways

  • Liechtenstein has had the world’s highest economic output per capita for decades, topping $195,000 in 2026 projections.
  • Europe is home to many of the countries with the highest gross domestic product (GDP) per capita.
  • Guyana’s GDP per capita soared to over $95,000, the highest in the Americas, following the exploitation of large oil reserves.

Measured by economic output per person, the world’s richest countries tend to be small, developed states found mostly in Europe. One South American country, though, has followed the Gulf state approach to greater riches.

This visualization ranks the countries with the highest GDP per capita in 2006, 2016, and 2026 using projections from the International Monetary Fund’s World Economic Outlook. Figures are in current prices and have been adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).

The Biggest Shifts in the Rankings

In 2006, Liechtenstein already stood atop global rankings, with the only GDP per capita ($112,499) to reach six figures. The small Alpine microstate was followed by nearby Luxembourg ($96,014) as well as Gulf petrostates like Qatar ($86,252) and the United Arab Emirates ($74,230).

Most of the world’s wealthiest countries by this measurement are small offshore financial hubs such as Singapore and Switzerland.

Different countries saw their economic fortunes grow through different means. Singapore, for example, developed internationally competitive industries, including semiconductors and pharmaceuticals. Off the strength of these industries the country eventually rose to have the second-highest GDP per capita ($173,708) worldwide in 2026.

Guyana’s transformation, by contrast, began with a resource discovery that changed the country’s economic profile altogether.

How Oil Transformed Guyana

Oil production in Guyana only began in December 2019. As offshore output expanded, the impact extended beyond petroleum. Oil revenues have helped finance infrastructure and human capital programs, while public investment has supported construction and other domestic activity.

For a small economy, developing a major export industry can rapidly change output per resident. The country’s GDP per capita has grown more than anywhere else since 1990. By 2026, Guyana was projected to have a higher GDP per capita ($95,477) than any other country in the Western Hemisphere, including the United States ($94,430).

The challenge for Guyana is turning that windfall into lasting gains. Guyana has accumulated oil revenue in a Natural Resource Fund, helping balance current development needs with future savings. Investing effectively matters because oil prices can fluctuate sharply, and a higher national average does not automatically translate into better services or higher incomes for every household.

Leprechaun Economics in the 21st Century

Ireland’s climb reflects a different kind of international connection: the role of multinational companies. Profits generated by foreign-owned multinationals contribute to domestic production even when they ultimately flow to owners abroad. This can create a substantial gap between the size of the economy and the income retained by residents.

Corporate restructuring can also produce unusually large jumps. In 2015, the relocation of company balance sheets to Ireland, including intellectual property, drove a dramatic increase in reported GDP. The Irish government has even set up a parallel measurement of economic output to account for these multinational firms’ activities.

By 2026 Ireland’s GDP per capita ($159,129) is forecast to be the second-highest in Europe and third-highest worldwide.

Ranked: The Most Valuable Company in Every European Country

Ranked: The Most Valuable Company in Every European Country

ASML is Europe’s most valuable listed company at $670 billion, but banks lead in 15 of the 42 countries covered.

Which Company Leads in Each European Country?

Key Takeaways

  • The Netherlands’ ASML is the largest company in Europe at $670 billion, nearly twice the value of the next-largest: the United Kingdom’s HSBC ($342 billion).
  • Finance and energy companies lead 28 of the 42 countries, with banks and other financial firms topping 18 and energy and utilities firms 10.

Europe’s most valuable listed company makes the machines behind advanced chips. But across the continent, banks are far more likely to be a country’s stock-market leader.

This graphic shows the largest publicly traded company in 42 European countries based on company headquarter location and ranking them by market capitalization. It uses data from StockAnalysis.com and local stock-exchanges as of September 29, 2026, or the latest available quote.

ASML Stands Far Above Europe’s Other Companies

ASML’s lead as Europe’s largest company reflects its unusual position in the booming chip supply chain. It is also the only technology company among the 42 national leaders.

At $670 billion, the Dutch company is worth almost twice as much as HSBC ($342.4 billion) or Roche ($341.5 billion), the largest companies in the UK and Switzerland.

As the only supplier of extreme ultraviolet lithography systems, which chipmakers use to manufacture advanced processors, ASML ranks as the world’s 20th largest company by market cap.

Novo Nordisk, Denmark’s largest company at $168.6 billion, was once Europe’s most valuable at more than $650 billion in 2024. The maker of Ozempic and Wegovy has since lost more than 70% of that value as Eli Lilly’s rival GLP-1 drugs, Mounjaro and Zepbound, took market share.

Banks and Energy Firms Lead Most of Europe’s Markets

Beyond ASML, finance and energy companies lead across Europe. Banks lead 15 countries, and including insurers and investment firms, finance leads 18. Energy and utilities companies lead another 10, so the two sectors account for 28 of the 42 national leaders.

Europe’s top companies are full of banks because of how its economy is built, with euro area firms having traditionally relied far more on bank loans than U.S. companies, which turn to capital markets.

Banks lead large markets as well as small ones, with Santander topping Spain at $205 billion and UniCredit leading Italy at $143 billion. In smaller markets, the leader is often a subsidiary of a Western European group: UniCredit controls Croatia’s Zagrebačka banka and the Bosnian bank on this list, and Germany’s Allianz owns Slovakia’s Slovenská poisťovňa.

Many energy leaders are state-controlled: the Norwegian state owns 67% of Equinor, and the Czech state holds nearly 70% of ČEZ.

Few newer companies have challenged these top-ranking European incumbents. A 2024 report by former ECB president Mario Draghi found that no EU company worth over €100 billion has been built from scratch in the last 50 years, while all six U.S. companies worth over €1 trillion were built in that time.

Ranked: The World’s Largest Vehicle Producers

Nearly 100 million car, buses, and trucks were built in 2025. Over a third of these came from just one country.

Which Countries Build the Most Vehicles?

Key Takeaways

  • China produced nearly 35 million vehicles in 2025, accounting for over a third of global production.
  • Vehicle production declined across the U.S., Mexico, and Canada, with Canada posting the steepest drop of the three.
  • India increased vehicle production by 7.9%, the second-fastest growth rate among the top 20 producers.

Factories around the world built nearly 100 million vehicles in 2025, led by major manufacturing hubs across Asia, North America, and Europe.

This graphic ranks 20 leading countries by reported vehicle production in 2025 using the latest figures from the International Organization of Motor Vehicle Manufacturers (OICA). Figures generally cover both passenger and commercial vehicles, subject to country coverage specifications.

China’s Lead Keeps Getting Bigger

China set a new vehicle-production record in 2025, extending its 17-year run as the world’s largest producer. The country manufactured 34.5 million vehicles, more than triple U.S. production.

Even at that scale, Chinese output grew by 10.4%. The additional 3.2 million vehicles exceeded Brazil’s entire annual production.

The table below ranks the world’s top 20 vehicle producers in 2025.

India produced nearly 6.5 million vehicles in 2025, reinforcing its position as the world’s fourth-largest producer.

The South Asian country’s domestic market helps explain its manufacturing scale: passenger-vehicle sales reached 4.49 million in 2025, compared with about 860,000 exported. That gives automakers a large home customer base alongside overseas demand.

China’s Cost Advantage Goes Global

China’s production lead rests partly on the sheer scale of its factories. Some of its largest electric-vehicle plants can produce more than one million cars annually, spreading factory costs across far more vehicles than smaller plants can.

Lower labor costs, government support, and a vast battery industry reinforce that advantage. For electric vehicles, years of manufacturing experience and widespread use of lower-cost lithium iron phosphate batteries help Chinese producers compete on price.

Those advantages are increasingly reaching overseas markets. China’s electric-car exports doubled to more than 2.5 million in 2025, while Chinese brands accounted for four in five Chinese-made electric cars sold abroad, up from fewer than two in five in 2021.

A Dip in North America’s Auto Industry

Production fell across all three major North American producers in 2025. Together, the U.S., Mexico, and Canada manufactured approximately 491,000 fewer vehicles than in 2024, a combined decline of 3.1% even as global output increased.

Behind those national totals is a shared manufacturing network. Parts can cross the region’s borders as many as eight times before final assembly, meaning a vehicle assembled in one country can contain work performed in all three.

That interdependence means a slowdown at an assembly plant can reduce orders for suppliers across the border. Meanwhile, new tariffs and reporting requirements in 2025 added costs and paperwork to a production system built around moving components between countries.
