SS United States live updates: How to watch intentional sinking of historic vessel off Florida coast

TheTools · 11.11.2026 18:27 · source · permalink

The SS United States, an ocean liner that was once the fastest on the seas, will soon hold a new title, becoming the world's largest artificial reef off the coast of Florida's panhandle.

It will be intentionally sunk on Wednesday, eventually coming to rest on the floor of the Gulf, about 25 miles southwest of Destin-Fort Walton Beach, between Panama City and Pensacola, in Okaloosa County.

Officials moved up the sinking date by one day because of weather conditions.

It's the end of an era for the iconic ship. After being retired for more than 50 years and several failed plans for restoration, officials in Okaloosa County bought the ship in 2024 in an effort to give it a new life.

The 990-foot-long vessel spent nearly three decades docked along Philadelphia's Delaware River waterfront before departing last year for Alabama, where crews have been preparing it for its next role as an artificial reef.

The $13.5 million project is designed to attract more divers and fishing enthusiasts to the area while creating a home for tuna, marlin and swordfish.

People from all over the country, including the Philadelphia region, have descended on Florida's Panhandle to be there for the historic sinking of the iconic SS United States, creating the world's largest artificial reef.

Despite the event being 22 miles offshore, many have gathered along the shoreline or embarked out on boats, just to be as close as they can.

Action News reporter John Paul, who is down in Florida for the historic event, says they are seeing decent weather, with a little overcast, making for a great day by the water.

The SS United States is being intentionally sunk Wednesday off Florida's Panhandle coast.

The SS United States has pipes that pulled seawater into tanks as part of the system that cooled its boilers, and those tanks have been removed so that once the valves are opened, water will flood the hull, according to Nick Tomecek, the spokesperson for Okaloosa County.

Workers also have cut 10-foot by 10-foot (3-meter by 3-meter) holes in the decks, and more than 400 portholes have been removed.

The boat was stripped down to make sure it is environmentally-friendly for its next life underwater as the world's largest artificial reef.

Officials say the ship will also be sunk without the use of explosive.

Federal environmental and explosives rules prevent Okaloosa County and its contractor from using explosives to scuttle the ship, Tomece explained.

The ship is to sink in about 180 feet (55 meters) of water, with its top deck about 60 feet (18 meters) below the surface — shallow enough that inexperienced divers can visit. It will settle on the floor of the waters about 25 miles (40 kilometers) southwest of Destin, between Panama City and Pensacola.

In preparation for the sinking, organizers also removed the 65-foot (20-meter) smokestacks and plan to have them anchor a new museum that celebrates the ship.

The ship has been undergoing remediation since it was towed from Philadelphia's Delaware River waterfront last year.

The SS United States is being sunk in an effort to created the world's largest artificial reef.

The long-planned $13.5 million project is aimed at attracting more divers and fishing enthusiasts to the Destin-Fort Walton Beach, while creating a home for tuna, marlin and swordfish.

The project will keep what had been a technological marvel when it made its maiden voyage in 1952 from becoming scrap metal.

"It was our goal to make sure that she wasn't lost forever," said David Lawrence, a member of the board for the SS United States Conservancy, which is dedicated to preserving the ship.

About 500 artificial reefs are located in the waters near where the SS United States will rest, said Nick Tomecek, the spokesperson for Okaloosa County.

"We're trying to be the best diving destination in the world," Tomecek said.

"This collaboration will foster amazing adventures for generations of visitors and create a tourism economy that will benefit the state and the entire Northwest Florida region," Okaloosa County Board Chairman Paul Mixon said in a statement.

The ship was decommissioned in 1969. Attempts by a hotel magnate and Norwegian Cruise Lines to use the ship never floated, and from 1996 until recently, it sat along a dock in Philadelphia.
