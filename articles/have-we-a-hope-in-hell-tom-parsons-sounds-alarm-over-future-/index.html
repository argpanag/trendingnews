‘Have we a hope in hell?’: Tom Parsons sounds alarm over future of GAA integration

TheTools · 10.09.2026 20:43 · source · permalink

Cahair O’Kane: It’s been evident for a while that the 2027 timetable was never realistic, was it?

Tom Parsons: Definitely there has been a feeling that the Special Congress this year wasn’t going to happen, especially after it wasn’t tabled at Central Council in August. We know there have been some challenges.

What has been frustrating is how this came to a point where it’s blown up that, ok, 2027 isn’t happening in its entirety, or there’s a change of direction and a pause, reflect, and a new five-year.

And the whole management of how a chair of a committee comes out in such frustration and explodes like that. Had this been brought up to Central Council democratically, I could shout and scream about it from the reps’ perspective and disagree or whatever.

County boards could challenge it. It’ll then get out publicly that the democratic structures have voted against a Special Congress. And I might have mandated to hold a Special Congress in 2027, so that 2027 isn’t going to wash away. As a Central Council delegate, that’s disappointing.

I’m not going to lie and say there hasn’t been rumblings of challenges but nothing insurmountable. One membership is agreed. The governance structures, it’s not a huge piece of work. Okay, not ready for this November, but could be ready for Congress in February.

I don’t think the script needed to be ripped up to say that we should move to an implementation group. It doesn’t come as a total surprise but it should have been handled much better than this.

COK: What would membership have looked like?

TP: I don’t think membership would have been a problem because you just sign up to be a member of the GAA. And, whatever levy comes centrally, it would be one injury fund as an example.

The membership fees for clubs differ. But it’s so you’re not paying a membership fee for your little girl and your little boy and they’re different because the LGFA is €90 and the GAA is €40.

I think that would have been straightforward enough. They came to the conclusion that you couldn’t just do that without also tabling the governance structures.

COK: What changes about the structures?

TP: The governance structures were presented to Central Council last February. There were moving parts at provincial, county and central level.

It was keeping a lot of what’s there, it wasn’t completely ripping up the script. You could burn the midnight oil in September and have three meetings a week and iron that out.

It’s not insurmountable that if you delayed it by six months or say that you’ll have it in February’s Congress in ‘27, you’re still making big ground next year.

COK: It was evident that Mary McAleese was frustrated, did it feel that her quitting was about not wanting it to drag on another year?

TP: Yeah, it could be. What she put out there factually was that the GAA had shared a position on integration and the language they used was they wanted to pause, reflect, reframe what it meant.

Then they wanted a phased plan over the next five years to achieve it. What she put out there probably seemed to be the GAA proposing a deviation from the work that the SGI had done. I’m sure she could have tried to iron that out but she made the decision.

She obviously made the conclusion that it’s a starkly different direction that the GAA wanted to go. But it’s positive that they’re still committed to integration.

COK: Jarlath Burns spoke to RTÉ Radio and said he would urge the GPA to work with the GAA and that some of the comments made over the last 24 hours have been “inaccurate”. You had quoted €5m as a minimum charter but the feeling is that it would be at least double that. Have you any response to Jarlath’s comments?

TP: Yeah, so there was a review group set up called the Player Charter Funding Work Group which was a member of the GAA, LGFA, Camogie and myself.

And we worked together to look at and get all the facts of what is the average cost of a senior inter-county team in the GAA? What’s the average cost currently of an LGFA or Camogie team? And then what’s the gap?

The average spend on a female team is €130,000 so there’s a lot being spent already. For example, the government funding that we lobbied for goes straight to paying female expenses, which we’ll get up to an average of 43c per mile this year.

Just to equalise expenses for female players alone, and this is all facts, about another €1m would nearly do it. But that report concludes that to bring it up to a minimum standard for every team, and that includes some of the lower tier male teams that wasn’t meeting the minimum standards, that the cost for a minimum standards team is about €280,000 per year.

And that total cost is [an extra] €10 million. When we looked at that, that was the cost for team management, player mileage, backroom team, meals, nutrition, travel, playing equipment, performance analysis, video analysis, hurls, footwear, facilities. So it wasn’t just player entitlements.

It was actually to know what’s the minimum standards that you have at an inter-county level. In the report we talked about phasing that [extra €10m] over two years or three years, and how do you split that between central funds or county boards, or do you go to government?

That’s all facts. The €44m being spent on male county teams includes all the development squads, the U17s, the U20s. It isn’t just senior teams - about €10 million of that that goes to all the underage development. What’s not factual in any sense is that the money that’s being spent on inter-county teams goes from €44m to €90m. There’s no factual report that demonstrates that.

COK: The feeling from people I’ve spoken to is money is the elephant in the room. If it becomes integrated, there are counties spending over €2m a year on their male teams. How do you prevent that becoming €4m? I know you’re saying it won’t be that but if you’re promising equality, then how does it not?

P: No, no, it can’t be equality. It needs to be equitable, but it needs to be on a performance tier.

For example, there’s a big difference in spend between the Mayo footballers and the Mayo hurlers because the Mayo footballers are just playing at a different level. With integration, there’d be a handful of female teams that would probably fall into that high performance category.

Take the likes of Cork and Galway, that All-Ireland [camogie] final - the skill level, the tempo, the training is probably right up there. They’d be one example of a team that should probably be treated equally to a good men’s team.

But you have to cut cloth to performance level, and that’s where the lowest tier teams might have to make do with one physio, might have to make do with no video analysis. The gear allocation might be given, but cut your cloth over two years. We need to be realistic.

It just wants to remove the male-female element out of it and have it equitable based on performance. Take Meath [ladies footballer]s when they won All-Ireland, arguably they were way ahead of the Meath hurlers and right up there with the Meath men’s footballers at that time.

So, it has to be equitable, of course, but based on performance tiers rather than men-women. We use the word equality, but we’re not naive enough to believe that everyone’s treated the same.

If Limerick hurlers win the All-Ireland, they’re going to dine in Adare Manor. The other three codes aren’t going to be dining in Adare Manor. If you’re filling out Croke Park, you’re going to need additional support.

If you’re playing at that level, the association and counties rightfully will need to inject a little bit of revenue to make sure that the octane here is really high. We need really good physios, S&Cs.

These guys are burning massive calories. They’re training at this level. The nutrition support might need to be just a level up. That’s what I mean. Maybe we’ve done a bad job at probably communicating that, but that’s why we did that piece of work to look at what the cost of the minimum standards is.

COK: I take the point but nobody on the men’s side is operating at ‘minimum standard’. You mentioned Galway and Cork camogs there. Galway were the second highest spenders on the GAA side at €2.3m and Cork were third at €2.2m. If you put those successful camogie teams on top of that and they cost an extra €1m, and those counties don’t have that extra €1m, and there aren’t black-and-white answers on how you deal with that, how do you?

TP: I think they’re forgetting that the female teams aren’t coming from nowhere. The average spend is €130,000 per team.

There are some male inter-county teams that aren’t spending that.

They actually have a fairly high performance environment than us and are actually way more efficient. There are efficiencies to be found. You look at the spend for the men’s team, it has went through the roof…

COK: But last year, the lowest spend on the men’s inter-county teams was Fermanagh at €682,000. That’s five times the average for a female team?

TP: Yes, but that’s €600,000, they would have reported the figures for all their inter-county teams that would have included development and underage. But you’re still talking about development squads and minor squads…

COK: From memory, you’re talking about €100,000 odd for those?

TP: Yes, you are right there. If you were to equalise, there is a big gap in the underage spend in those elite development squads. We didn’t look at that.

There are efficiencies to be found. What’s strange about it is that the player charter that we negotiated for the men hasn’t changed in 10 years. The three big costs that we negotiated in 2016 were a boots voucher, €375. That’s the same. The nutrition rate, which was €20 a week. That’s the same.

The mileage rate in 2016 was 65c. We moved it to 70c, but it drops to 40c. It’s the same cost.

We haven’t changed one of the player enticements in 10 years. What’s happened is that counties’ revenue has grown. They’ve had more money to spend. They’ve spent a fortune on paying managers and coaches and bringing in GPS and Hudl and all these other big performance-enhancing services.

The GPA never bargained for those. I’d nearly love to do away with all this video analysis and just have the game more free-flowing. That’s why the new football has been brilliant because there’s more chaos in the game. Again, back to the amateur status and the licencing.

This licencing framework was also meant to support integration, to put some manners on the whole system. To say, what? No, you can’t be training from September or you’ll lose your licence.

If you want to be an inter-county manager, you have to be contracted and we’re going to pay you €100. We don’t care, but these are the rules you follow. We needed to put manners on the system, but you’re right. There is inefficiencies. There are bigger problems in the GAA’s inefficiencies than there is…Currently, the female teams are very lean.

If you integrated tomorrow, you put a work group together and phase it over two years to try and equalise, you can find an awful lot of efficiencies.

The other one we looked at, the length of the season, as an example. If you’re competing at the lowest tier, why would you be training for nine months?

If the lowest tier had more condensed seasons where it was six months of back-to-back games and really good quality games. If the lowest tier can’t afford nine months of inter-county activity, you have to look at the competition structures.

There are different levers that you can pull, but it’s not black and white. We did a big piece of work trying to put some numbers to it. We came up with that number that to equalise minimum standards, it would need an injection of €10m.

At least that’s a figure we can stand over, but everything else is just opinion until we get facts. You’re not wrong with what you’re saying. It is a challenge, a huge challenge.

COK: When you take all of that, if you put your hand on your heart this morning, were the GAA wrong to say we should delay this? Regardless of the storm that has followed, was doing that and not rushing integration though when it’s not ready the right decision?

TP: They have to get it right, but they need to show urgency that it’s a priority. If you want to know what’s the priority of an association, show up at Congress and go to the workshops and listen to what’s been talked about.

Listen to what are the three pillars that the president of each association keeps talking about, because then that shows what’s the priority. That’s the frustrating part, that this was passed in 2022.

We trusted that this group was working in the background and we didn’t know as a fact that it wasn’t going to happen. If this happened two years ago, it would be more tolerable. It feels like is there four years wasted?

I burnt the midnight oil working on that piece of work on costings. A lot of other groups burnt the midnight oil looking at structures.

It nearly feels like there’s four years of work disbanded when you talk about we need to pause, reflect, change direction, change the group. That’s more frustration, but you’re right. I wouldn’t push ahead a half-baked proposal and try and get it over the line, but sometimes you need a deadline.

No-one ever achieves a goal if they don’t put an end date. It’s saying ’we’re building this house by September ‘27’. You can run over by a month or two months, but if you don’t put the date on it, it’ll be an endless project.

You have a rotation of presidents and there’s no urgency. No good project can be delivered without putting urgency and a date on it. Pulling ’27 - what’s the new date? If you said, look, we’re doubling down or tripling down on ‘28, that would be more tolerable than what came out.

COK: What is your solution on the finance? Does the spending on men’s teams need to come down to say €25m? How do you do that? And if you don’t, you’re creating a position of inequality and costs rise on the female side and suddenly you’re at €65m or €70m and then it’s ‘oh shit’?

TP: That €44m includes management teams and GPS and fairly high-performance operations for U20s, U17s. You have three inter-county teams on the men’s side on one code. That’s six in two codes.

Would a strategic position be that we move to one development team at U19 and then senior? So then you’re dealing with four teams instead of six, or you’re dealing with eight teams instead of 12 when you integrate if the female teams have the same structure.

COK: So you’re getting rid of development squads, basically getting rid of anything under 19?

TP: It’s ‘is there a different solution?’ On the men’s side, some teams are carrying a second squad. We’ve heard about a team going up to play a McKenna Cup game with one squad of 25 and then another squad goes down and plays a challenge game, so there’s nearly two senior teams, panels of 50.

We have to find efficiency.

Back to that inter-county licence, even for competitive balance, you need to put a kind of standard that.

Weaker, smaller counties are already struggling to keep up with the big counties who have sponsors who are giving €1m. For us to have a diversity of winners and different counties knocking at the door, we can’t have an unfair advantage. You need to find efficiencies. Where’s the urgency on the inter-county licence? That was voted and that was meant to look at cost savings and complement integration.

COK: Where is that process at currently?

TP: I got a promise from the GAA that the GPA would be on that committee. I have got no invitation to any committee. Where’s the urgency there? That was passed at Congress. And that was a great tool to try and manage costs and player welfare and put some sense on any silly stuff that’s happening.

COK: There has to be an end game solution in the sense of the financial problem. It can only be you reduce the spending on the male side or you increase the spending on the female side because it can’t do both. So which one is it?

TP: It’s probably a bit of both. It’s probably also a bit of additional investment. Government are really calling for this and calling for female participation, can they make an investment as well? Do you look at the length of the season?

I called for pre-season competitions to end and that would save every county four weeks of training. The cost of the additional training far outweighs what is taken in gate receipts, because these competitions are helter-skelter. We have Mayo having a potential challenge game in London in November. So find inefficiencies on the men’s side.

Gradually increase the investment on the female side. Centrally procure gear. You could save so much, or you might do a deal with these gear companies that they provide for free given the exposure. Just put a project management hat on it and bring in experts to help design it.

I think it’s too important. If the cost of the inter-county team is what stops… I’m a GAA man and I have a daughter playing and a son playing and I’m very proud of the GAA. And when you compare it to any other sport you’re like ‘Jesus, this is unreal’.

But I am uncomfortable that the playing members are boys and men. If the inter-county cost is a stumbling block throw block for something that’s much bigger, I think it’s a missed opportunity. And I’ll burn the midnight oil and help bring players with me to find a solution.

Let’s burn the midnight oil, have back-to-back Central Council meetings and clear the diary for days and solve it? So that’s my kind of thought on it. As Cillian O’Connor would say to me, there’s no big thing that wins a game, it’s the thousands of little things. And in this, there’s loads of little things, there’s loads of communication, efficiencies, the licence, look at competition structures, how do we find efficiencies?

COK: The fact that four years in, there are no answers to those questions, surely that means that not going ahead is the right thing to do?

TP: If the steering group on integration hasn’t done four years of work. We haven’t seen the big report that’s been done. If they’ve sat on their hands and done nothing, which I don’t know that answer to, then do you pause?

Or do you phase this? You’re not going to have everything solved in day one, but you have to bring motions to enable change.

And if you try and have too much detail in anything that’s changed, it’s like the detailed design of a project. You never go and say ‘we need to approve the metro’. You approve the conceptual design first, bang, approved.

Then you approve the preliminary design. And you allow changes in each design stage. But you have to get stuff approved. And that’s where I felt we’re at this stage of ok, membership system, let’s get that done. Now governance structures, let’s get that done. Ok, now we’re looking at the inter-county licences and costs, and so on.

COK: The structures and membership is the easy bit and four years in, they still haven’t got that nailed down?

TP: You’ve nailed it. If we can’t agree at the conceptual stage of membership and a governance structure, have we a hope in hell when we get into the detail?

Had we got past this stage, and got one membership voted on, and there was a big row because the GAA said the inter-county costs are a problem, that’s different. At least we’ve made ground on every boy and girl is playing one membership and they both play GAA.

At least you make ground on that. But the fact that we’ve had a fallout before that, you’ve nailed it. That is a big worrying sign, that we couldn’t agree over four years one membership and the high level governance structures.

Because the clubs are doing it themselves.

The clubs are figuring this shit out, whether they’re one club or they’re not. Over four years, we couldn’t figure out these two more simple pieces of the puzzle.

COK: Which means realistically, integration is at the very least another four years away?

TP: Maybe. If that’s how far off we are. But let that committee get some win. Don’t leave with nothing. If everything wasn’t right with that group, you need to at least let them get the one membership over the line.

Give them something. It’s so demoralising, whether they did great work or not, to not be able to get something over the line.
