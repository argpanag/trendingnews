Here’s the latest.

Candidates are making their last, best pitches in the final hours before Election Day, chasing votes in a midterm election that will determine control of Congress. The outcome is likely to hinge on a handful of issues, including how Americans feel about President Trump two years after his return to the White House.

About 50 million Americans have already cast ballots, a surge of early voting that is outpacing the last midterm cycle in every major Senate battleground as campaigning draws to a close. In several states, the share of the early vote from registered Democrats was higher than in 2024, an encouraging sign for the party as it seeks to flip control of Congress.

Interviews with more than 28,000 voters conducted by The New York Times and Siena University revealed consistent themes: economic anxiety, the importance of individual candidates over party affiliation and Mr. Trump’s unpopularity.

Mr. Trump, who has repeatedly urged supporters to pretend he is on the ballot, insisted beside the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool that Republicans would emerge victorious if his base turned out. But in remarks that began with a lengthy account of his various construction projects and touched on top soil, Bermuda grass and Corinthian columns, he also seemed prepared for the possibility of defeat. “For some reason the sitting president doesn’t win the midterms,” he said, “but we gave this a very strong shot.”

His appearance came just hours after his administration appealed to the Supreme Court in its latest effort to intervene in a state’s elections. In an emergency filing, the administration asked the justices to order California officials to set aside 20,000 mail-in ballots that the administration claims were provided to noncitizens.

Democrats, energized by positive polls in several close races, expressed hope that reclaiming control of the Senate — a goal that once seemed well out of reach — was possible. “I am hopeful that tomorrow we can celebrate,” the Democratic Senate nominee in Maine, Troy Jackson, said as he spent Monday rallying voters and canvassers in Portland, a Democratic stronghold.

For weeks, surveys have indicated that voters’ frustration with the high cost of living has turned even deeply conservative states competitive, as Mr. Trump’s popularity has deteriorated. The latest Times/Siena polls, released on Sunday night, showed Democratic Senate candidates leading their Republican rivals in seven major battleground states.

The Justice Department charges more people with voter fraud in California.

Federal prosecutors on Monday charged another six people in California with having illegally voted in the 2024 presidential election, the latest move in the Trump administration’s effort to identify and publicize instances of voter fraud.

The charges, announced on Monday by the Justice Department, come amid the administration’s efforts to segregate some 20,000 California ballots that it claims were cast by “likely noncitizens” — an assertion that state election officials have firmly rejected. Judges have rejected the federal government’s arguments in a series of rulings over the last 10 days, including on Monday at the Supreme Court in an unsigned order.

At a news conference on Monday, Bill Essayli, the top federal prosecutor in Los Angeles, said that the six charged, in addition to the 28 charged last month, were among the 20,000. More than 230 people across 28 states have been charged in connection with voting violations during President Trump’s second term.

“Voting is the essence of American citizenship,” Mr. Essayli said at the news conference. “Illegally attempting to infiltrate and influence our political determinations is not only wrong; it’s a crime.”

Also on Monday, a federal judge in Washington, D.C. granted a preliminary injunction that blocked the federal government from pressuring California officials to segregate the ballots, describing the lists as “flawed” and noting that the administration did not have the authority to do so.

“California may decide for itself what procedures to use for verifying the citizenship of registered voters and for deciding whether to count their mail-in ballots,” wrote Chief Judge James E. Boasberg, noting that the executive branch lacked the authority to “unilaterally impose its preferred procedures.”

Election officials in California and other states have condemned the federal government’s crackdown, saying its system for identifying suspected violators is flawed and based on incomplete information.

At the news conference on Monday, Mr. Essayli said that federal authorities planned to move forward with criminal prosecutions and were enlisting the help of some local California authorities to do so.

In addition to the federal cases, one person was charged with illegally registering to vote and voting in San Luis Obispo, Calif., the city’s district attorney, Dan Dow, said at the news conference.

He said his office was close to making decisions on 16 other similar cases.

“When a person who is not a citizen casts a ballot, a foreign national is making a decision that only belongs with Americans,” Mr. Dow said.

Reporting from the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool

Trump delivers a closing midterm message. Mostly about landscaping.

President Trump was standing in front of the Reflecting Pool on Monday afternoon, doing a little reflecting of his own.

“For some reason,” he was saying, the Washington Monument mirrored in the newly reopened pool behind him, “the sitting president doesn’t win the midterms. But we gave this a very strong shot.”

It was the day before the election, and the president had decided to come here, a place that excites him, to talk about many of the topics that make him happiest. Trouble is, almost none of those topics have anything to do with the issues that are driving this election.

At a time when voters have made clear they’re most concerned with the high prices of gas and groceries and a new war in the Middle East, Mr. Trump used his final in-person appearance before Americans head to the polls to talk about drain pipes, top soil, limestone, fountains and his favorite kind of columns.

“The pipes now are totally renovated,” Mr. Trump said. Propped up next to him was a poster board showing an aerial view of the pool, with this caption: “Back, and more beautiful than ever!”

The “brand-new pipes,” Mr. Trump said, are “very important in terms of the algae and other things. And it’s a complete job, and we’re very proud of it.”

The Lincoln Memorial in front of him, Mr. Trump addressed a small audience seated in folding chairs. There were a few dozen National Park Service employees, reporters, White House officials and Interior Secretary Doug Burgum. At one point early in his speech, Mr. Trump beckoned to a young man who bounded up onstage with a stack of large printouts under his arm.

“Great,” Mr. Trump said as he took the stack and began rifling through it. He held up one of the printouts. It was a photograph of what looked like a rusty drainpipe. Another photograph showed a dumpster filled with detritus.

“This is another example of what it looked like just at water level and below,” he explained as he waved another photograph aloft. Every so often, he would look over his shoulder to check on the body of water behind him. “It’s a massive piece of pool,” he said, sounding impressed. It looked clean, dark and choppy on this early November day.

The Reflecting Pool had been closed and fenced off since July. After a series of bungled repair jobs, it became a symbol of the Trump administration’s dysfunction.

After he finished with the pictures, the president turned to other matters of national interest. Matters including: the nature of Corinthian columns (“the most beautiful in the world”); the soul of grass generally, and Bermuda grass specifically (“like the human being, grass has a life also”); the endurance of granite (“it literally has thousands of years of life”); the renovation of a public golf course on the Potomac that he’s planning; statues; the White House ballroom; graffiti; and the new White House helipad.

“The helipad,” Mr. Trump said, “was desperately needed.”

In the history of American politics, the speech may go down as one of the more unusual closing messages on the eve of an election. But it was a pretty good crystallization of what matters to Mr. Trump as he heads into the lame duck phase of his second term — despite, or maybe because of, his depressed poll numbers and voter unhappiness over the economy, corruption and an unresolved war.

And now here he was, using his final in-person address before the election to talk more about his chryselephantine fantasies of ballrooms and arches and Corinthian columns.

“The president’s a builder,” Mr. Burgum said from the little stage on Monday. “You can’t keep him away!” Large signs that aides had duct-taped to the stage before the president’s arrival read: “RESTORING OUR CAPITAL.”

The president made some of his usual comments about the war with Iran, the U.S. economy and various culture war issues, but he did not even attempt to explain how any of the renovation projects that were the focus of the speech related to any of the issues animating the midterm elections.

“I’ve traveled all across the country, spoken at all of these rallies I’ve done over the last two and a half weeks,” he said. “America is ready to go. They’re ready to vote. I hope they’re ready to do the right thing.”

It seemed almost as though he had run out of things to say about politics and governance, and like he simply wanted to talk about what sparks joy for him.

“We have a lot of great things planned for Washington, and I could get into the nation,” Mr. Trump said, “but for that you don’t have enough time.”

The unsigned order, which gave no reason for the decision, came hours after administration lawyers asked the court to step in. That came after a divided federal appeals court had rejected the administration’s arguments that California officials should impound the ballots for further scrutiny.

Trump’s election fraud reward offer raises fears of vigilantism among local officials.

Local law enforcement and election officials are bracing for a tense Election Day as President Trump has kept up a sustained attack on the integrity of U.S. elections, including by offering a $1 million reward for information that leads to an election fraud conviction.

In response to Mr. Trump’s unsubstantiated claims about voting problems and his campaign to assert more federal control over state-run elections, officials have prepared for potential worst-case scenarios in which the federal government tries to intervene in voting.

Now some local authorities have said they also fear that the president’s offer of a large financial incentive could foster unrest or even vigilantism, with Americans eying one another with suspicion as they head to the polls.

“Don’t drag in the public and basically a vigilante-type system where we’re going to say, ‘Hey, you can make a million dollars here by going over here and accusing somebody of fraud,’” said Sheriff Chris Nanos of Pima County, Ariz., a Democrat. “Our elections are handled at the local level, but yet we have fire and brimstone, if you will, coming down from the federal level.”

Mr. Trump’s unfounded claims that the election could be rigged come at a time when violent threats against public officials and election workers have increasingly become a new normal. To prepare for Tuesday, election officials and local law enforcement agencies have held hundreds of exercises to anticipate a variety of possible threats and disruptions at polling sites.

Chief Chris Davis, who leads the police department in Green Bay, Wis., said the level of fear surrounding elections has increased in recent years, particularly in his battleground state.

“I worry about disturbances at polling places, and we’ve developed pretty comprehensive plans for that if it happens,” said Chief Davis, who added that he was concerned Mr. Trump’s reward offer could further heighten tensions at polling sites.

“If one voter challenges another voter, the potential for that to turn into a disturbance and a big problem for us is very real,” he said.

Deidre Henderson, the Republican lieutenant governor of Utah, has also warned in recent weeks that the federal government has contributed to a tense environment for the midterms.

Ms. Henderson told CBS News’s “60 Minutes” that in previous elections, “the threats were all external: nonstate actors, cybersecurity threats and other things. But now we also have threats coming from within our own government.”

The atmosphere is already fraught. The Justice Department has asked California to set aside 20,000 mail ballots, claiming they were provided to likely noncitizens. The administration has also sought to pre-emptively challenge the results of the Senate race in Alaska, where two candidates named Dan Sullivan are on the ballot. So far, the courts have blocked those efforts.

State and local officials have responded to Mr. Trump’s attacks by seeking to create guardrails to prevent potential disruptions.

Gov. Gavin Newsom of California, a Democrat, sent an extraordinary “alert” on Saturday to the four U.S. district courts that cover California, warning the judges that the Trump administration may try to seize ballots.

And in response to the Justice Department’s plan to dispatch 1,000 federal monitors across the country, some local officials have issued statements saying that monitors are prohibited from crossing into an established perimeter surrounding polling locations, citing state law.

Justin Elicker, the Democratic mayor of New Haven, Conn., said the Justice Department told local officials last month that it would be sending two teams of two people to polling sites in the city to ensure New Haven was complying with federal voting requirements on language assistance and disability access.

The city responded by sending the Justice Department a letter saying the monitors were not welcome to enter any polling locations.

“They intimidate people not to vote,” Mr. Elicker said in an interview. “We would prefer that they not be here.”

In discussions with local and state officials, the Justice Department committed to monitoring from outside a 75-foot perimeter, like other observers, and pledged not to interfere with voting, according to Stephanie Thomas, Connecticut’s Democratic secretary of the state.

A Justice Department spokeswoman said federal monitors do not require approval from state or local authorities but added that monitors are trained to follow local laws that govern access at polling sites.

In the final stretch of the midterm campaign, Mr. Trump has continued to make unsubstantiated claims that the country’s elections can be manipulated, falsely asserting that there is widespread fraud.

“I’m also going to be issuing a $1 million bonus or reward, or whatever you want to call it, for anybody that reports voter fraud, leading to the arrest and conviction of somebody,” Mr. Trump told reporters last week before flying to Mississippi for a campaign rally.

The president has said the offer applies to past elections, as well as the midterms.

Mr. Trump’s dangling of a $1 million reward caps a year in which he has trained the full arsenal of his administration on sowing doubt about the country’s election systems.

The White House declined to answer questions about what money would be used to pay for the reward or how Americans should report fraud, and did not address criticism that the financial incentive could spur people to vigilantism.

Lauren Bis, a White House spokeswoman, said that Democrats were the ones eroding election security. “They refused to enact common-sense election security measures like voter ID and citizenship verification,” she said in a statement. “President Trump is right: We have to have honest elections.”

Despite the inflamed rhetoric, local and state officials said that the authorities tasked with ensuring a safe election are prepared for this moment.

“A lot of what has been said has been designed to provoke and cause fear, so we’re paying attention to it but we’re not going to overreact,” said Aghogho Edevbie, the deputy secretary of state of Michigan, who said that his office has helped conduct about 125 security exercises this cycle.

“That is going to cause us to be ready for anything that comes along,” said Mr. Edevbie, a Democrat. “You could ask, ‘Was that more preparation because of what’s happening from D.C.?’ Absolutely.”

Gowri Ramachandran, the director of elections and security in the Brennan Center’s Elections and Government Program, said Americans should not lose faith in poll workers.

“Election officials just really have been working super hard to provide a free and fair election for voters,” Ms. Ramachandran said. “The rhetoric that we see coming out of the administration just does not square with the reality of what election officials and voters are doing. And I would say the only way all of that rhetoric works is if people don’t exercise their rights to vote.”

Nearly three-quarters of voters said they largely trusted that the results of Tuesday’s election would be accurate, including large majorities of Democrats, Republicans and independents, according to a recent New York Times/Siena poll. Still, half of likely voters were worried about Republicans trying to overturn the outcome of the election, while 31 percent of voters were worried about the possibility of Democrats taking such action.

Chuck Wexler, the executive director of the Police Executive Research Forum, which advises departments on best practices, said that current and former police chiefs discussed at a recent gathering how the political divisiveness at the national level had trickled down to communities trying to prepare for the election.

“There are some people who are willing to go beyond rhetoric to actual exercise violence, and that has not been lost on police chiefs,” Mr. Wexler said, noting the Jan. 6, 2021, assault on the U.S. Capitol by supporters of Mr. Trump who were angry about the outcome of the 2020 election. “What used to be a simple constitutional right of voting now has become an issue of considerable agitation.”

Georgia Democrats fear state races could be close enough to set off disruptive hand counts.

In Georgia, Democrats expecting Senator Jon Ossoff to win his re-election bid are also cautiously optimistic about their slate of down-ballot candidates. But some Democrats are also worried about potential delays, or even chaos, if races for state offices end up so close that they trigger automatic statewide hand recounts under a law signed earlier this year.

The law, drafted in a special session of the Republican-controlled State Legislature, requires Georgia’s secretary of state to direct a manual recount of all ballots in a race if the results of the initial tally are within 0.5 percent. The law applies to races for governor, lieutenant governor and a host of other state offices. It does not apply to races for the U.S. Senate or House.

On Monday, a Georgia state representative, Saira Draper, a Democrat, said that behind the scenes during the special session, Republican lawmakers had offered assurances that “a half-point race was a remote possibility — the vote was about placating election deniers, not about accuracy.”

Now, she said, the possibility did not seem so remote. “If a hand recount is triggered, Georgians will be waiting on a slower, messier process that Republicans chose for political reasons, and they’ll own every bit of the chaos that comes with it,” she said.

In 2020, Georgia carried out a manual recount of the close race between President Trump and Joseph R. Biden Jr. That recount was completed without major incident, and reduced Mr. Biden’s lead by 1,200 votes. Mr. Biden ended up narrowly winning the presidency, despite Mr. Trump’s unfounded allegations that the system was rigged against him.

Officials this week in the office of Brad Raffensperger, the Republican secretary of state since 2019, said they were confident that the system was ready for a hand recount. “Georgia is prepared,” Blake Evans, the state elections director, said in a statement.

We talked to 28,000 voters, and some clear themes emerged.

In the run-up to Election Day, people have looked to polls for indications of how the contest for control of Congress may play out. But beyond the horse race, polls this fall have sent some clear messages about what voters care about.

This year, the Times/Siena Poll made nearly four million phone calls to 1.6 million voters, completing more than 28,000 interviews. We will find out on Tuesday how accurate the polls were by our team and by other pollsters across the country, but there are issues on which voters’ opinions are consistent enough that the lead extends well beyond the margin of error. Those issues help shed light on the current political environment and the motivations behind various midterm election decisions.

It’s the economy …

Polls have consistently shown that the top issue for voters this year is the economy. Every Times/Siena poll conducted in 2026 has found voters listing it as their single biggest driver.

The economy was also a top issue in the 2024 presidential election, but in a departure from recent years, voters are placing their trust in Democrats this time. Nationally, they favor Democrats to handle the economy over Republicans by eight percentage points. The shift is a reversal from just two years ago, when Donald J. Trump was favored by six points over Kamala Harris on this issue.

Across the seven states in our final battleground poll, six of which Mr. Trump won in 2024, voters were nine percentage points more likely to say they trusted Democrats to handle the cost of living. In Kansas and Texas, both states that the president won by double digits in 2024, Democrats also held a nine-point advantage on the issue.

Trump is deeply unpopular, but he has a reliable base.

Regardless of the outcome of the elections on Tuesday, one thing is clear: Voters are frustrated with Mr. Trump. The president’s disapproval rating stands at 60 percent nationally, according to the New York Times presidential approval tracker.

Mr. Trump’s approval rating for his handling of the economy has fallen to 35 percent, a drop from 43 percent in April last year. It is down among men and women; white, Black and Hispanic voters; Democrats, independents and, to a lesser extent, Republicans.

A vast majority of Republicans, however, remain loyal supporters. More than 80 percent of Republicans in the latest Times/Siena poll said they approved of the president’s job performance. Signs of a mass Republican exodus are not there.
