How Wind Farms Affect Clouds — and Vice Versa - AIP Publishing LLC

TheTools · 17.11.2026 15:30 · source · permalink

The presence of clouds can significantly impact the power generated by wind farms and should not be ignored in simulations.

From the Journal: Journal of Renewable and Sustainable Energy

WASHINGTON, Nov. 17, 2026 — In the Netherlands and northern Europe, wind farms are often built in the water, where low-level clouds are common. Clouds can affect the power generation from wind farms, but the exact impacts haven’t been well studied.

It turns out that they both affect each other, something that surprised researchers from the University of Twente in the Netherlands. The results were published in the Journal of Renewable and Sustainable Energy.

“Clouds are extensive atmospheric phenomena that occur very frequently, especially in the North Sea,” said author Davide Selvatici. “They are so common that they may influence wind farms and their energy production.”

In general, many factors affect wind farm energy generation, including the presence of other turbines. The researchers focused on offshore wind farms and started small to simulate different atmospheric conditions, then subsequently scaled these up to full-scale wind farm simulations. When they included sets of smaller domains with different climates and simulated various atmospheric conditions, they found that interaction between wind farms and clouds depends on weather conditions, particularly cloud height.

“Many of these simulations [previously] only considered idealized conditions where [the clouds are] essentially not there,” said author Richard J.A.M. Stevens.

When clouds are present, however, the researchers found something different. Clouds — and atmospheric humidity in general — influence wind farm power production through their radiative properties, particularly their impact on longwave radiation. Vice versa, wind farms also influence clouds and the surrounding atmosphere by pushing the boundary layer upward in front of the wind farm and pulling it downward in its wake.

“It is important to include clouds in wind farm simulations,” said author Thijs van den Ham. “They do play a role, and they do matter.”

These are the first simulations of this kind. The authors have demonstrated their importance and highlighted the need for further research, including simulations under a wider range of meteorological conditions. They hope future computational advances will allow them to perform more complicated simulations, increasing wind farm size and distance, as well as introducing additional meteorological conditions.

“In these simulations, a possibility will be to really see the effect of meteorological conditions … When you plug in some realistic real time conditions, then it will be interesting to see exactly — and in more detail — the effect,” Selvatici said. “But this can only be done with more computational resources that will only be available in the future.”
