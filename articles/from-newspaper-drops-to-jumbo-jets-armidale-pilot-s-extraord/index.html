From newspaper drops to jumbo jets: Armidale pilot's extraordinary life in the skies

TheTools · 20.11.2026 00:15 · source · permalink

Long before Captain Garry Honour was flying Boeing 747s around the world, he was throwing bundles of newspapers out of a Cessna window over country towns in northern NSW.

It was the early 1960s, and the young pilot from Gravesend, west of Warialda, had landed his first commercial flying job with Tamworth-based charter company Tamair.

Every afternoon, Sydney newspapers would arrive at Tamworth Airport aboard a DC-3, where Mr Honour and his fellow pilots would load them into small Cessna 182 aircraft for distribution across the region.

One route took in Armidale, Glen Innes, Inverell, Bingara and Barraba before returning to Tamworth, with the pilots landing at each airport to deliver the day's papers.

The second route was considerably more adventurous.

"We had to do a paper drop," Mr Honour said.

"We'd taken the hinge off the window, made the window big enough to swing a bundle of newspapers, they all weighed probably 15 kilograms, tied up with string."

Flying low over towns without airstrips, the pilots would steer with their right hand while tossing bundles of newspapers out the window with their left.

At Edgeroi, near Moree, the drop zone was beside a general store, surrounded by powerlines and enormous wheat silos.

"We had to swing in low, avoid the powerlines and the silos and drop the newspaper, not on the highway, or traffic, and not on the roof of the general store, preferably in the parking area," he said.

On one memorable occasion, Mr Honour managed a perfect delivery.

"The newspaper had landed on the front step of the shop," he said.

"He bounced a couple of steps and there it was, he didn't have far to walk."

More than six decades later, the 86-year-old Armidale resident remembers those early adventures as vividly as the international flights that followed.

His career would eventually take him across the Pacific, through the mountains of Papua New Guinea and into the cockpits of some of the world's largest passenger aircraft.

But it began on his family's 4500-acre farming and grazing property, where a crop-dusting aircraft sparked an ambition that changed his life.

Mr Honour had originally hoped to become an agronomist, but a five-year waiting list for agricultural college left him working on the family farm.

"I got 1000 hours on John Deere tractors before I got near an aeroplane," he said.

Watching a crop-duster working dangerously close to the treetops one day, he realised there might be a more exciting way to earn a living.

"I sat on the back of Dad's Bedford truck, watching this guy work his magic fence-top height, in amongst the gullies, crop spraying, and I said, 'Jeez, that wouldn't be a bad occupation'."

In 1962, he secured an Australian Government flying scholarship after completing 30 hours of training at Inverell.

By 19, he had his commercial pilot's licence, and the newspaper deliveries provided the experience he needed to pursue bigger opportunities.

Those opportunities eventually took him to Papua New Guinea, where he flew supplies, medical personnel and passengers into remote communities surrounded by mountains reaching almost 15,000 feet.

The flying was dangerous, the weather unpredictable and the airstrips often little more than rough clearings in the jungle.

"You simply set your limits, and if the weather was below those standards, we simply said, sorry, we're not flying today. Wait for the fine weather," he said.

He later joined Trans Australia Airlines before becoming one of the founding captains of Air Niugini in 1973.

He went on to fly Boeing 707s and Airbus A300s, before joining Singapore Airlines, where he commanded Boeing 747s on international routes.

His recollections also challenge the popular perception of Qantas as an airline that has never experienced a fatal crash.

Mr Honour said Qantas had suffered fatal accidents during its earlier operations in Papua New Guinea, involving smaller aircraft such as Otters and Beavers.

"They've lost quite a few through Papua New Guinea bush flying," he said.

After retiring from Singapore Airlines, he continued flying commercially for other airlines, including Air Pacific and Airlines PNG.

He eventually settled in Armidale with his wife Katie, whom he met during an overnight stop in Auckland while flying for Air Niugini.

Their eldest son, Kent, followed him into aviation, training at Armidale Airport while attending The Armidale School before becoming a Boeing 787 captain with All Nippon Airways in Japan.

Mr Honour has now retired from the cockpit after a career spanning more than five decades, although aviation remains central to his life through his involvement with the Armidale Aero Club.

"I've given up flying now. I've had enough," he said.

"I've done my share commercially up the front end, the pointy end as we call it."

For a man whose career began delivering newspapers across New England, it has been an extraordinary journey.

"Still loving it, loving flying, but at the moment I take a back seat. I've done my share," he said.
