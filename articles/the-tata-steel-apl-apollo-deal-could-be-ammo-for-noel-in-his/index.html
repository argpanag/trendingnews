The Tata Steel-APL Apollo deal could be ammo for Noel in his fight against Chandra

TheTools · 25.11.2026 01:28 · source · permalink

Tata Steel is yet to live down the disaster that was the Corus acquisition. It doesn’t need another

Shareholders in Tata Steel and APL Apollo Tubes couldn’t be more annoyed by the firms’ complex, two-part deal. On Monday, over Rs 18,000 crore vaporised from Tata Steel’s market value and Rs 12,600 crore from APL’s. (The stock exchanges were closed on Tuesday for Guru Nanak Jayanti.)

The pummelling matters more to Tata Steel than to APL. It is, after all, the second oldest of the Tata firms that are publicly traded, and only Tata Consultancy Services and Titan Company are worth more within the conglomerate. 

This is the deal in the simplest terms: 

– Tata Steel will sell its steel-tubes and prepainted-steel businesses to APL for Rs 28,000 crore. APL will first borrow to fund the acquisition and then raise equity

– Tata Steel will then buy the APL promoters’ 26.6% stake and then launch an open offer to take its stake in the company to nearly 53%, spending almost Rs 32,300 crore. APL will then become a Tata Steel subsidiary

Brokers tracking the companies didn’t see this coming. JM Financial pointed to “concerns around funding requirements” for APL and “potential balance sheet implications”. Emkay called it a “surprising turn of events” and said Tata Steel’s divestment “raises questions about Tata’s strategic priorities”. It also said that:

While the transaction could reflect selective portfolio rationalization rather than a broader retreat from value-added products, the key question is whether these businesses offered suboptimal incremental returns or whether Tata is sacrificing profitable downstream platforms for near-term monetization.

Proxy advisers are as flummoxed by the deal as analysts. Shriram Subramanian of Ingovern Research Services told Business Standard that the two-way acquisition doesn’t benefit minority shareholders of either company. What makes observers even more circumspect is that a company owned by APL promoters will be a contract manufacturer of pre-painted steel for Tata Steel.

Amit Tandon, founder and managing director of Institutional Investor Advisory Services India, questioned the strategic rationale for Tata Steel. “If Tata Steel intends to become an integrated steel manufacturer, why would it choose to depend on a contract manufacturer for a significant portion of its colour-coated products? And it achieves this dependency after adding around ₹32,000 crore to its consolidated debt,” he said.

₹30,707 cr mcap erased as deal rattles Tata Steel, APL Apollo shareholders, Business Standard

Tata Steel is infamous for making one of the lousiest acquisitions in the history of corporate India. In 2007, it bought Corus Group, Europe’s second-largest steelmaker, for $12 billion. A bidding war with a Brazilian rival meant that Tata Steel paid a third more than its initial offer, and almost twice as much, relative to profits, as Mittal Steel had paid for Arcelor. 

The deal loaded Tata Steel with debt and the “significant synergies” it expected never came to pass. The fact that Tata bought Corus just before the financial crisis didn’t help at all.  

A decade later, JJ Irani, a former managing director at Tata Steel, called the acquisition an “aspirational mistake”.
