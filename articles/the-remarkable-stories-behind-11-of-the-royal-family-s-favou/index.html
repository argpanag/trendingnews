The remarkable stories behind 11 of the Royal family’s favourite jewels

TheTools · 06.10.2026 14:59 · source · permalink

They know the soft power of a strategically worn precious stone. Here are the most spectacular examples, and their most notable outings

Of the innumerable jewels in the royal vault there are only some that remain in popular rotation today, glittering pieces filled with history and meaning that have been re-worn for generations.

These beloved heirlooms offer us a rare insight into the personal styles of their famously private wearers; some are chosen for their timeless designs, others gently adapted over decades to suit the prevailing tastes.

From sparkling tiaras to romantic bracelets, these are the stories behind 11 of the Royal family’s favourite pieces.

Queen Mary’s Lover’s Knot tiara

One of the most recognisable royal jewels, the Lover’s Knot tiara, was commissioned by Queen Mary in 1913 from E Wolff & Co, who supplied Garrard. Its design replicates a 19th-century tiara owned by her grandmother, the Duchess of Cambridge, and inherited by her “beloved Aunt” Augusta, Grand Duchess of Mecklenburg-Strelitz.

The piece is topped with heart-shaped bow motifs and was originally set with 38 pearls (19 dangling, 19 upright). Queen Mary later had the upper pearls removed and replaced with enormous diamonds.

“It’s undoubtedly a grand piece, but because of its airy frame and feminine pearls it still has a lightness to it that makes it very wearable, especially for younger royals,” says historian and author Martin Williams.

After Queen Mary died in 1953, the tiara passed to her granddaughter, Queen Elizabeth II, and was worn by her throughout the 1950s. It was later loaned to Princess Diana and soon became a signature element of her iconic style. Since 2015, the tiara has been the go-to headpiece for Catherine, Princess of Wales.

The Girls of Great Britain and Ireland tiara

In 1893, a committee of women led by Lady Eve Greville raised over £5,000 to buy a wedding present for Princess Mary of Teck, the future Queen Mary. From Garrard they purchased a diadem with an ornate festoon-and-scroll design.

“What I love is that the committee knew Mary well enough to ask what she wanted, and she chose this convertible piece, which can be worn without its base,” notes Claire Scott, design director at Garrard. “Mary had a very clear vision when it came to jewellery, and this is a stately design that suited her style perfectly.”

In 1914, Queen Mary replaced the pearls with 13 large diamonds, and in 1947 she gave the top half of the tiara to her granddaughter, Princess Elizabeth, as a wedding present. Elizabeth adored “Granny’s tiara”, and in 1969, reunited both halves of the tiara and its distinctive design became synonymous with the rest of her reign. Since her death, Queen Camilla has worn this beloved headpiece, debuting it in 2023 on a visit to Mansion House.

The maple leaf brooch

Crafted by Asprey in 1939, the platinum and diamond maple leaf brooch was a gift from King George VI to his wife, Queen Elizabeth (the late Queen Mother), ahead of their state visit to Canada that year.

Modelled on Canada’s national emblem, the piece was intended as a gleaming tribute to the country and helped to bolster support for Britain, which was teetering on the brink of World War II. In 2002 the brooch was inherited by Queen Elizabeth II, who wore it to mark Canada Day throughout her reign.

She later lent it to both the Duchess of Cornwall (now Queen Camilla) and the current Princess of Wales for their own Canadian tours, showing her deep understanding of the soft power of a strategically worn jewel.

The Edinburgh wedding bracelet

When Prince Philip decided to propose to Princess Elizabeth in 1946, he needed a gift suitable for a future queen. His mother, Princess Alice of Battenberg, offered her own diamond tiara – a wedding gift from her uncle and aunt, Emperor Nicholas II and Empress Alexandra of Russia – so it could be dismantled to make an engagement ring and bracelet for her future daughter-in-law.

Philip took the tiara to London jewellers Antrobus, who transformed it into a three-carat diamond ring and an Art Deco-style bracelet. Elizabeth was clearly delighted – she wore it on her wedding day in 1947 and throughout her 73-year marriage to Philip.

“The bracelet became far more than a piece of jewellery; it became an enduring symbol of royal legacy,” says Charlie Pragnell, managing director of Pragnell, the company which acquired Antrobus in 1991. In 2015, Queen Elizabeth lent Catherine, then Duchess of Cambridge, her cherished bracelet to wear to a banquet at Buckingham Palace – perhaps a testament to the esteem in which she held her granddaughter-in-law.

The Greville chandelier earrings

These spectacular shoulder-dusters are named after Dame Margaret Greville, a renowned society hostess and philanthropist who, like her friend Queen Mary, was passionate about jewellery. In 1918, she commissioned Cartier to create a pair of fancy-cut brilliant drop earrings, which she revised four years later by adding on twelve more diamonds.

In 1929, 10 further diamonds were added to form the eye-catching earrings as they are today. When Margaret Greville died in 1942, she bequeathed an enormous trove of jewels to another dear friend, Queen Elizabeth (the late Queen Mother), who gifted these earrings to her daughter, Princess Elizabeth, as part of a wedding present in 1947.

In 1952, having finally had her ears pierced, the newly crowned Queen Elizabeth II wore the earrings for the first time to the Royal Variety performance. Today, the earrings are part of the Princess of Wales’ jewellery box. She wore them to the Baftas in 2026.

Queen Victoria’s sapphire brooch

On the eve of her wedding to Prince Albert in 1840, Queen Victoria recorded in her diary that her fiancé had given her “a splendid brooch…which is really quite beautiful”. Enamoured with her present, which featured a large deep-blue sapphire framed by 12 round diamonds, the young Queen wore it pinned to her wedding gown and had it designated an “heirloom of the Crown” in her will, reserving its use for future queens.

“Albert was a great designer of jewels and engaged closely with his wife’s tastes,” says Williams. “Their union was one of the all-time great royal love stories, so this is a jewel that speaks evocatively of that.”

Since Victoria’s death in 1901, every subsequent queen, both consort and regnant, has worn her sapphire brooch – its huge central stone makes it the perfect piece for grand state affairs.

Queen Victoria’s Oriental Circlet tiara

Crafted for Queen Victoria in 1853, the Oriental Circlet tiara was made by Garrard under the direction of Prince Albert, a keen scholar of the natural world. Named for its Mughal-inspired arches (which are set with 2,600 diamonds) and its intricate lotus flower motifs, the piece was originally set with a collection of opals, one of Albert’s favourite gemstones.

“The prince was a real renaissance man, extraordinarily cultivated and educated and fascinated by minerals,” says the historian and author Geoffrey Munn. Later, Queen Alexandra had the opals replaced with rubies, allegedly because she believed they were unlucky. Queen Elizabeth (the late Queen Mother) inherited the piece in 1936 and wore it on many occasions, including for her portrait by Cecil Beaton following King George VI’s coronation.

The tiara is now on loan to the Princess of Wales, who debuted it at a banquet for the German president in 2025.

Queen Elizabeth’s four-row pearl choker

To mark her first official visit to their country in 1975, the Japanese government presented Queen Elizabeth with a set of lustrous Akoya pearls, which she commissioned Garrard to fashion into a choker with a diamond-encrusted clasp. “Having the clasp at the front gives the necklace a beautiful focal point, without taking away from the natural beauty of the pearls, so it’s impactful but still elegant,” says Sara Prentice, Garrard’s chief creative officer.

Indeed, the choker became one of the late Queen’s hardest working jewels, accompanying her on trips to Bangladesh and Canada, and even to Margaret Thatcher’s 70th birthday dinner. In 1982, she lent the necklace to Princess Diana for her first state banquet, and later to Catherine, then still Duchess of Cambridge, for a party at Windsor Castle. Touchingly, Catherine wore the choker to the funerals of both Prince Philip and Queen Elizabeth.

The Cartier Halo tiara

Famously sported by Catherine, Princess of Wales at her 2011 wedding, the Halo tiara was initially created for a coronation. Crafted in platinum and set with 888 brilliant and baton-cut diamonds, it was made by Cartier London in 1936 and was part of a selection of jewels that Jacques Cartier had hoped would be purchased for Edward VIII’s impending coronation. Instead, it was bought by his brother, the Duke of York as a gift for his wife, Elizabeth. Less than a month later, Edward VIII abdicated, and the Duke of York ended up becoming George VI.

Elizabeth was photographed in the tiara at charity balls and at the theatre, but its modest size meant that it was more suitable for a princess than a queen. In 1944 she gave the Halo tiara to her daughter, Princess Elizabeth, as an 18th birthday present. Elizabeth loaned it to her younger sister, Margaret, who wore it to her sister’s coronation in 1953 (and, in doing so, fulfilled its destiny). After Margaret, the next person to wear the tiara was Princess Anne in 1967, then it remained unworn until Catherine and William’s big day.

Queen Alexandra’s wedding necklace

Composed of eight cluster-shaped diamond and pearl motifs and three huge pearl drops, Queen Alexandra’s wedding necklace was part of a magnificent parure given to her by Bertie, the future Edward VII, shortly before their marriage in 1863.

The suite was commissioned from Garrard by the Prince himself, and Alexandra wore the entire set (minus its tiara) on their wedding day. “When it came to jewels, Queen Alexandra was definitely a maximalist,” observes Williams. “She was very considered with her clothing choices – but she really loved to stack up her jewels.”

After Queen Alexandra’s death in 1925, the parure was inherited by her daughter-in-law, Queen Mary, who gave it to her own daughter-in-law, Queen Elizabeth (the late Queen Mother), who was renowned for her love of pearls and cherished it during her lifetime. More recently, the piece has passed to the Princess of Wales, who first wore it to a state banquet for the Netherlands in 2018.

Queen Mary’s Fringe tiara

Five generations of women, including three brides, have worn the Fringe tiara, immortalising it as one of the Royal family’s most familiar and romantic heirlooms. It was created by Garrard for Queen Mary in 1919 in the style of a Russian fringe tiara, which was a popular style at the time.

“The design had its origins in traditional kokoshnik headdresses of the imperial court and could look very imposing, like a sunburst or a wall of diamonds on the head,” says Munn. According to Garrard’s logbook, the piece is set with “633 brilliants and 271 rose [cut] diamonds” taken from existing jewels in Mary’s collection.

When Mary died, the diadem was inherited by Queen Elizabeth (the late Queen Mother), who loaned it to her daughter, Princess Elizabeth, for her wedding day in 1947. Famously, its antique frame snapped as it was being placed onto the princess’s head, and the piece had to be rushed to Garrard under police escort to be hastily repaired – a minute gap between the front two spikes is still visible in photos from the day.

Luckily, by the time Princess Anne borrowed the tiara from her grandmother for her 1973 nuptials, it had been fully restored. When the late Queen Mother died in 2002, the piece was inherited by Queen Elizabeth II, who lent it to her own granddaughter, Princess Beatrice, for her wedding to Edoardo Mapelli Mozzi in 2020.
