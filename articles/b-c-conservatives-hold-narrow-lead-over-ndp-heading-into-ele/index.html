B.C. Conservatives hold narrow lead over NDP heading into election day: polls

With the provincial election now just days away, polls are suggesting a tight race, one that could be determined by voters who have yet to decide which party they’ll mark on their ballots, according to a pair of political scientists. 

Data released by the Angus Reid Institute this week shows the Conservative Party of B.C. leading slightly. The Conservatives are sitting at 46 per cent, the NDP at 43, the Greens at eight and other parties and candidates at three.

Simon Fraser University political science lecturer Sanjay Jeram said that narrow lead is "effectively a tie," meaning there is still room for other parties to make gains.

About 75 per cent of Conservative voters polled by the Angus Reid institute from Oct. 15-19 said they were certain about who they’ll be voting for on Saturday. Meanwhile, only about 41 per cent of Green voters and 54 per cent of NDP voters were sure of where their vote would go.

"There's still a good number of NDP voters who are expressing that they're … uncertain whether they will change their vote or not," Jeram told CBC’s BC Today. 

Fewer people advance voting in B.C. election so far than in 2024

About 15 per cent of voters polled, from across all parties, said they hadn’t decided who they’d be voting for, and three per cent said they were unlikely to vote, period. 

It’s those voters who still have no idea who they’re voting for or if they plan to vote at all that could make all the difference, according to Daniel Reeve, political science instructor at Camosun College. 

"Those folks are often the deciders," Reeve told CBC’s On The Island.

"It's very difficult to predict what one issue is going to stir them to vote. Often it's kind of an emotional response to something they see in the last few days that might inspire them to vote, but might not."

And parties will be trying swing votes in their favour, making announcements and speaking to reporters to the bitter end, though Conservative Leader Lorne Doerkson did not take questions from media on Tuesday and did not schedule media availability Wednesday.

Strategic voting

Undecided voters may feel pressure not to vote for their first choice in a close race like this one, Reeve said.

B.C.'s current electoral system can sometimes force people to vote strategically — when someone who, instead of voting for the party or candidate they prefer, votes for another party or candidate, in order to block someone else from being elected.

How strategic voting may play a role in this election

For example, say you live in a riding like Courtenay-Comox. According to polling aggregator website 338canada.com, that riding is a toss-up between the Conservatives and the NDP. If you want to vote for a candidate with another party, but worry your vote could be the difference between an NDP or Conservative MLA, you might not vote for the person you want, but against the person you don't.

"Do you recognize that there're two major parties who are most likely to win that riding … and vote for your second choice?" Reeve said. 

"It's a question of whether your heart or your strategic self makes that choice."
