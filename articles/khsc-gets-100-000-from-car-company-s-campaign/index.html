KHSC gets $100,000 from car company's campaign

TheTools · 15.09.2026 22:06 · source · permalink

Grant from Hyundai Hope on Wheels will help pay for clinical trial staff and more, hospital says.

Kingston Health Sciences Centre is getting a $100,000 boost for pediatric cancer care from an automotive company’s national campaign, it was announced Tuesday at Kingston General Hospital.

It’s the second year KHSC has received a grant through the Hyundai Hope on Wheels charitable foundation, which is distributing $4.1 million to 16 pediatric hosptials this year, up $100,000 from its inaugural year in 2025.

At KHSC, the grant money has allowed it to hire a second full-time person to conduct clinical trials last year. Clinical trials are key in pediatric oncology, said Dr. Laura Wheaton of KHSC’s pediatrics department, who spoke at Tuesday’s event.

“We’ve made a lot of progress in the last 50 years, so they’re increasingly complicated, involve lots of expensive drugs, have lots of optional studies, and so the actual work of doing the clinical trials has really expanded.” Wheaton explained afterward.

“The biggest thing (this funding) has done has helped us to strengthen that infrastructure to actually provide the therapeutic clinical trials.”

While the additional staff is where the bulk of the $100,000 is directed, part of it is being used to look into supportive care trials and improve a child’s experience of going through cancer treatment, Wheaton said.

That comes with challenges, Wheaton said.

“For instance, this mucositis study, kids can get really bad mouth stores. They can’t eat. They can’t drink. They’re in a lot of pain with that. And we know that cryotherapy, which is cold therapy, really helps prevent it. Okay. But trying to get a small child to eat ice — it’s not very enticing. So, a family actually donated a slushie machine, which then they’re going to get up and running.”

It has always been a challenge to find funding, Wheaton said, and this donation goes a long way to offsetting that.

“In fact, the funding has really gone down as part of the clinical trials. Part of that comes from the issues with the government. We’re in a collaborative research group through North America, and a lot of that funding came from the NIH (National Institutes of Health) and the U.S.,” the KGH pediatric hematologist and oncologist continued.

“So, obviously, there’s been some big changes with funding there, but also just generally, there’s been less funding available. We usually get some money per patient who’s enrolled, but that is not enough to support the actual infrastructure of doing it. This is a huge boost.”

While Wheaton represents the hospital side of the funding announcement, the Thoman family, six-year-old Anderson along with parents Lindsay and Adam, represents the patient side.

We are so grateful to everyone that has helped create this initiative, and to all of those that participate, because they're not only bringing awareness to the presence of childhood cancer in Canada, they're actively providing funding and hope for the many families like ours that are fighting this battle.

It was in May of last year when Anderson, then five, was diagnosed with b-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma. He exhibited none of the typical symptoms until a lump appeared on his right cheek. Five days and one fire truck ride home from kindergarten later, Anderson began treatment.

“Anderson’s treatment journey, which is just over halfway complete, has been an emotional and physical roller-coaster for the strength and positive attitude that Anderson has sustained throughout this extremely difficult process, has been nothing short of heroic,” his mother, Lindsay, told those gathered just inside the doors of KHSC’s Watkins building Tuesday.

“Adam and I could not be more proud of how adaptable and easygoing Anderson is. He teaches us every single day to focus on the positive and to keep moving forward, which has strengthened the bond of our family.”

It was during a visit to the hospital last year that Anderson, who’s this year’s ambassador, took part in the inaugural Hyundai Hope on Wheels event, which is distributing $4 million in donations across the country this year.

“Anderson was so excited to put his painted handprints on the car, and he’s eagerly anticipating doing it again after these boring speeches, aren’t we?” Lindsay Thoman said, looking at her son sitting in the front row beside his father.

“Adam and I are blown away by this amazing program. We are so grateful to everyone that has helped create this initiative, and to all of those that participate, because they’re not only bringing awareness to the presence of childhood cancer in Canada, they’re actively providing funding and hope for the many families like ours that are fighting this battle, so we’re beyond grateful.”

After the speeches had wrapped, Anderson and his parents, along with a number of representatives from Hyundai, KHSC and the University Hospitals Kingston Foundation, moved outside, where a white Hyundai car dotted with magnets bearing handprints was parked on the building’s front lawn.

Anderson dipped his hands in the paint and planted his hand on the outside of the fuel door. A few other childhood cancer patients at the hospital left their painted handprints, too.

In the past 15 months, Anderson has had two surgeries, chemotherapy four days in a row for weeks on end, and inpatient stays, the Thomans said. He’s now receiving active oral treatment every day at home, and comes in for lumbar punctures every 12 weeks, they said, and an IV to deter pneumonia. If all goes smoothly, he’ll be receiving until around this time next year.

“Lindsey and I just kind of take our cues from Anderson,” father Adam said as Anderson was literally running circles around the handprint-peppered car parked on the lawn as he spoke.

“He’s been so brave. He’s so resilient. He’s a champion.”
