8 Reasons It's Hard to Watch Naruto: Shippuden Today

TheTools · 09.10.2026 17:15 · source · permalink

Naruto: Shippuden remains one of the defining shonen anime of its generation. Pain’s assault on Konoha, the Akatsuki’s rise, and Naruto’s long journey toward becoming the shinobi he always believed he could be have lost very little of their power. At its best, the anime makes its enormous runtime feel completely justified. Nevertheless, actually watching it today feels less thrilling than it did when the show first aired.

Anime has changed dramatically since Shippuden premiered in 2007. Filler has become much less common, and audiences are accustomed to stories moving through manga material considerably faster. Returning to a 500-episode weekly anime consequently exposes problems that were easier to tolerate years ago. New fans and returning viewers need considerably more patience than they would with many modern alternatives.

Naruto: Shippuden Is an Enormous Commitment

Starting Naruto: Shippuden means committing to 500 episodes. That number is even more intimidating because Shippuden is not actually the beginning. Anyone watching Naruto's entire journey properly should already have gone through the original Naruto series, which adds 220 more episodes. That was easier to accept when enormous weekly shonen were normal.

Watching anime today offers far more alternatives requiring dramatically less time. Someone can complete several highly acclaimed modern series before reaching the end of a single major Shippuden storyline. For longtime fans, the enormous journey is part of the appeal. For a newcomer, however, there are much easier commitments available.

Naruto: Shippuden Has an Extraordinary Amount of Filler

The episode count would be much easier to accept if all 500 episodes moved the central story forward, but they do not. About 200 of Shippuden's 500 episodes are pure filler. Some anime-original material is genuinely enjoyable. Certain anime-original stories give supporting characters more attention than the manga could provide.

The problem is placement and volume. There are entire stretches that can be removed without making the central story difficult to understand. That creates an unusual viewing experience for modern newcomers. A series this famous should ideally encourage viewers to watch more of it. Shippuden regularly makes them research which parts they are allowed to skip.

Naruto: Shippuden's Weekly-Era Pacing Shows Its Age

Filler is not the only reason Shippuden feels long. Canon episodes can also move remarkably slowly at times. A conversation that needs a few minutes can be surrounded by reaction shots, close-ups, and repeated reminders of information viewers already understand. Battles sometimes spend almost as much time explaining attacks as performing them.

This was partly a consequence of being a continuous weekly adaptation. The anime needed to avoid catching up to the manga, which encouraged stretching available material. Not every episode suffers equally. Some of Shippuden's greatest battles move brilliantly. The inconsistency is what makes the slower material difficult to get through.

Naruto: Shippuden Really Loves Flashbacks

Naruto had a lonely childhood, and anyone who watches Shippuden will know this. Unfortunately, they will be reminded constantly. Flashbacks are essential to the series because the past drives many of its strongest characters, and repeatedly reveals something new. Kakashi's past transforms the way viewers understand him. Itachi's history completely changes the meaning of several earlier events.

The issue is repetition. Some memories appear so frequently that their emotional power can diminish rather than increase. Weekly viewers originally had years separating certain repetitions. Streaming compresses that distance. Something the audience saw months earlier during broadcast may have appeared only two nights ago during a binge. That completely changes how some of the flashbacks feel.

Naruto: Shippuden's Visual Inconsistency Can Be Jarring

Naruto: Shippuden has some extraordinary animation. Its biggest scenes feature ambitious choreography, expressive movement and sequences that remain impressive years later. Sadly, all 500 episodes cannot look like that. Some episodes are noticeably stiffer, and background characters can look unfinished. Early Shippuden also unmistakably looks like a television anime from the late 2000s.

The inconsistency can be jarring during a binge. One episode might feature ambitious choreography and expressive character animation. The next can rely heavily on static images and basic movement. There is also an inevitable visual gap between Shippuden's earliest episodes and modern high-definition anime. Watching Shippuden means experiencing everything between its best and worst moments.

Naruto: Shippuden's Final War Lasts an Extremely Long Time

The Fourth Great Ninja War is supposed to be enormous. It resolves mysteries stretching back through the series and brings many of the shinobi world's biggest conflicts together. The anime certainly gives it enough time. Once Shippuden enters its war-heavy final stretch, the story remains there for a huge portion of the remaining series.

Individual moments can be exceptional. Some of Shippuden's greatest revelations and battles happen during the war arc. Unfortunately, experiencing the whole thing consecutively diminishes its greatness. The war can sometimes feel like several arcs stacked inside one gigantic arc. Filler and flashbacks make it even longer. Eventually, escalation itself becomes exhausting.

Too Many Great Supporting Characters Get Left Behind

One of the original Naruto's greatest strengths is how important its wider cast feels. The Chunin Exams make characters such as Neji, Rock Lee, Shikamaru, Hinata and Gaara feel capable of carrying entire storylines. Many of them gradually disappear into the background in Shippuden. Shikamaru receives exceptional material surrounding Asuma, and Hinata gets a few important moments.

Most of their peers are considerably less fortunate. Rock Lee never receives another good storyline. Tenten barely gets anything substantial to do. The problem is more frustrating because viewers already know these characters can be interesting. They are not random background ninja. The original series spent years convincing audiences to care about them only for Shippuden to squander all that potential.

Naruto: Shippuden Has Been Outpaced by the Anime It Helped Inspire

Naruto: Shippuden did not suddenly become worse. Everything around it just became better. Modern anime have learned from the strengths and weaknesses of long-running weekly shonen. These days, stories move faster, and filler arcs are less common. A single cour can cover developments that an older weekly anime might have stretched across much longer periods.

There is also something modern anime lost by moving away from that structure. Few newer shows can reproduce the feeling of growing alongside a cast for hundreds of episodes. Naruto: Shippuden helped establish the appetite that modern battle shonen benefit from. Those newer anime then adopted production models that make returning to Shippuden considerably harder.
