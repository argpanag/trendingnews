The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth review – Tarantino and Fincher join forces for Brad Pitt’s glorious return

TheTools · 19.11.2026 18:00 · source · permalink

David Fincher directs Quentin Tarantino’s script, picking up from Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, and its lead is more ineffably beguiling than ever

We got through to the very end of Quentin Tarantino’s previous film Once Upon a Time in Hollywood without finding out exactly how, why or if Brad Pitt’s stuntman character Cliff Booth murdered his wife. It was an issue only addressed in Tarantino’s novel based on the movie. And throughout the 140 minutes of this outrageous sugar-rush sequel – scripted by Tarantino and directed by David Fincher – this subject is tactfully passed over in silence, the one point that damages our perception of Cliff’s amiable-cowpoke good nature.

Cliff is the guy who climaxed the previous picture by helping with a dramatic rescue; after, that is, gallantly refusing an age-gap relationship with Margaret Qualley’s jailbait teen. Well, we may not get the complete picture of Cliff’s matrimonial circumstances until Tarantino’s forthcoming crossover Cliff Booth novel, with which this film is to form a kind of multimedia narrative event. But there is nonetheless such a huge amount of energy, fun, scabrous black comedy, provocative bad taste and film-making style to be had here.

Like Tarantino’s Kill Bill: Volume 2, The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth repeats the ideas and characters of the first film a little pedantically, certainly, and the ending is rather truncated – again, I think, a function of the story being spread across film and book. But it maintains a headlong craziness with a flair so few can come anywhere near. It is a movie that once again gives us the brutality and scuzz of Ed Bunker, the urban crime-pastoral of Elmore Leonard, and naturally the classic Tarantino trope of two tough guys rolling up in a car, a little before the appointed hour, but in time for some backtalk and bickering. (Here, some loathsome hitmen argue about the illogical house-numbering sequence on LA’s Mulholland Drive.) Fincher’s direction perhaps speeds things up in the scene transitions, quicker than the chapter-by-chapter prolixity that Tarantino would have favoured. Female characters also tend to keep their shoes and socks on.

The setting is now almost 10 years on from the first film, the Los Angeles of 1977, the time and place of Paul Thomas Anderson’s Boogie Nights, in fact, and also the presidency of Jimmy Carter, though it is only Nixon who gets a namecheck. Sharon Tate is on TV advertising cigarettes. Cliff has grown a moustache and quit the stunt business, having sustained an injury that gives him a slight limp; his old pal Rick Dalton is apparently making a TV show in Australia.

These days, Cliff has moved out of his trailer and lives with his cantankerous old pawnshop-proprietor dad Gus (Peter Weller) and presides over a portfolio of side-hustles. He’s a movie theatre projectionist (like Pitt’s character in Fight Club) at LA’s historic New Beverly Cinema, a coyly proprietorial touch from the screenwriter and the venue’s present-day owner. Cliff tends bar at various joints on various nights of the week, has a gig at a demolition derby, and maintains unofficial connections with the screen acting world, which causes people to recognise him all over the city.

Most importantly, he is a discreet fixer and intermediary for influential people who are being extorted. Cliff is bemused to be contacted out of the blue by smart, willowy talent agent Karen Crosby (Elizabeth Debicki), a former movie critic once mentored by Pauline Kael, and this would appear to be how Tarantino has taken his idea for a movie critic character that was going to be the subject of his “final” movie. (Personally I was disappointed that the LA Times’s Kevin Thomas isn’t the one to have taken Karen under his wing.)

Karen, with whom Cliff falls passionately in love, introduces him to her client Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), who is currently acting in a TV show with blaxploitation star “Hot” Rod Harrison (Yahya Abdul-Mateen II). We get a very amusing pastiche-montage of Charlie’s B-picture and TV credits, like the ones for Rick Dalton in the first picture. Charlie is fascinated by Cliff’s military record in the second world war, and Cliff agrees to take on a difficult job for him, his heart softened by the fact that he once had a tendresse for Charlie’s worldly wife Toni (Carla Cugino). So Cliff enters, or re-enters, or maybe just gets further into, a world of violent crime.

The film can only work with the drawlingly imperturbable good humour of Brad Pitt as Cliff. His performance is endlessly watchable and entertaining, but also enigmatic, in that Cliff’s battle-hardened experience of the world has left him with an easygoing carapace of calm. Cliff is essentially surprised by nothing, affected by nothing; this is a function of Cliff’s character and Pitt’s own almost sphinx-like address to the camera. It is Cliff’s inability to be seriously rattled by anything which provides the ironic torsion and charge to the movie. It is Pitt’s great comic creation.

The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is in UK and US cinemas from 25 November, and Australian cinemas from 26 November; it is on Netflix from 23 December.
