The Fed isn't riding to the rescue — Wall Street still sees a December rate hike after tech's worst week since 2020: Chart of the Day

TheTools · 23.11.2026 21:04 · source · permalink
  • ^GSPC +4.73%
  • ^NDX +6.86%

The S&P 500 just lost ground for seven straight days, its first streak that long since the pandemic crash. The Fed is still expected to raise rates in less than three weeks.

The S&P 500 (^GSPC) fell 3.7% Friday and closed in a correction — 10% or more below its record — with about $8 trillion gone since that Oct. 6 peak. The Nasdaq-100 (^NDX) sank 5.6% and is a hair's breadth from a bear market, defined as a 20% drop.

Bettors aren't counting on relief. Polymarket gives a hike at the Fed's Dec. 9 meeting about 91% odds, down from 98% on Nov. 12, and the bond market is pricing in a 92% chance. "Waiting longer to implement rate increases risks getting behind the curve," Fed governor Lisa Cook said Friday.

Wall Street has a case of the Mondays

"Anybody who has studied history knows that half the biggest market crashes in history happen on a Monday," Dan Niles of Niles Investment Management said on Yahoo Finance's Deirdre Bosa Live. "You have a bad week, you have a horrible Friday."

Seven of the S&P 500's 10 worst days since 1952 came on a Monday. Tuesday has none of the 20 worst.

After the nine past weeks like this one — a 5% loss capped by a 3% Friday drop — the next Monday fell seven times, typically about 3%. The rest of the week usually rose, and by Friday the S&P 500 was higher seven times. The big exception was October 1987, when the next Monday became infamously known as Black Monday.

This Monday is off to a better start. S&P 500 futures (ES=F) and Nasdaq 100 futures (NQ=F) gained each gained over 2%.

Inflation is the difference this time

Most corrections don't last. Since 1989, 12 of 16 were erased within a year.

But consumer prices rose about 4% in the year through October, by Yahoo Finance's estimate, up from about 2.8% a year earlier. Since 1950, all eight corrections from a record that hit with inflation running a point or more faster than a year before became bear markets.

The Jensen wild card

Former Nvidia (NVDA) CEO Jensen Huang faces closed-door House interviews Dec. 1 and a Senate request to testify publicly, in what could be one of Washington's biggest business hearings in years. The SEC is reportedly examining Nvidia's accounting as well as its disclosures, and the company has delayed its full quarterly filing.

The Fed isn't blinking yet

The last time stocks panicked, the Fed held off. "The Fed likely would have hiked in October without the market turmoil that preceded the meeting," Natixis' Chris Hodge said this month.

Billionaire investor Bill Ackman wants a repeat. "I think the Fed is about to make another mistake on December 9th," he wrote on X, calling its 2% inflation target "misguided."
