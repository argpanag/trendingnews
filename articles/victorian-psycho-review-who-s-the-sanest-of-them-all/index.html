‘Victorian Psycho’ Review: Who’s the Sanest of Them All?

Critic’s Pick

A deliciously deranged psychological horror thriller pokes at the patriarchy and spills a lot of blood.

A good rule of thumb is that if someone tells you they’re the sanest person they’ve ever met, they almost certainly are off their rocker. So when Winifred Notty (Maika Monroe) introduces herself to the audience that way, head cocked eerily over her shoulder, it’s instantly clear who the titular psycho in “Victorian Psycho” is going to be.

Directed by Zachary Wigon, “Victorian Psycho” — written by Virginia Feito and based on her 2025 novel — is a deliciously deranged, hilariously sinister thriller about a woman whose marbles rolled away a long time ago. Miss Notty (as everyone calls her) arrives at Ensor House in 1858, ready to assume a post as the governess of two wealthy children, Drusilla (Evie Templeton) and Andrew (Jacobi Jupe).

Their parents, Mr. and Mrs. Pounds (Jason Isaacs and Ruth Wilson), are nice enough, though Mr. Pounds seems a little lecherous and Mrs. Pounds a bit tightly wound. Still, the magnificent home feels like an oasis to the new governess, who has recently come off a string of appointments in which the children in her care came to tragic ends. Her bedroom in the servants’ quarters is clean, if spare. And if she might spot — or think she spots, anyhow — a single human ear underneath the bed, what’s the harm?

Weird stuff starts happening from the very beginning of “Victorian Psycho,” and the movie often plays coy over whether it’s real or just in Miss Notty’s head. Slowly we get a sense of her life story, which is horrifying. But Feito excels at writing unapologetically repugnant protagonists (her 2021 debut, “Mrs. March,” is astounding in this regard), and Winifred Notty is not someone we’re meant to love. Just when you start to feel bad for her, she does something that is, well, insane.

The movie remains claustrophobically at Ensor House, which the production designer Jeremy Reed turns into a twisty, vulgar, overly ornate home, the kind that status-conscious Victorians might design to reassure themselves about their place in the social register. Through the cinematographer Nico Aguilar’s camera, it is a haunted place, dark and spooky by night, when Miss Notty often runs into Miss Lamb (Thomasin McKenzie), the children’s nurse.
