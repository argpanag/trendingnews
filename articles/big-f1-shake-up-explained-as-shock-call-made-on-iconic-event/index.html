Big F1 shake-up explained as shock call made on iconic event... and why Australia could still host Rd 1

TheTools · 16.09.2026 22:46 · source · permalink

The 2027 Formula 1 calendar was finally announced overnight, and though the general shape of it is now very familiar, the schedule comprises some new announcements that will change the feel of the sport.

The boost to sprint races has long been telegraphed but is now finally confirmed. Almost half of next year’s calendar will be run to the sprint format, with less practice and more competitive sessions.

It’s a sign the sport is determined to move in this direction, and it’s hard to believe F1 will stop at 10 — though that’s a decision for another season.

But the calendar was fascinating as much for what it didn’t say — and what can be read into some of those omissions.

WHY ISN’T AUSTRALIA ROUND 1 NEXT YEAR?

There are benefits to the race being held later in the year from the perspective of the race promoter.

Sunday’s grand prix will take place after the clocks are wound backwards at the end of daylight saving time. Assuming a 3pm start, the race will take place at 7am in Europe, 6am in the UK and 10pm in Los Angeles, maximising television exposure for the city.

But even as it’s become less common, opening the season in Australia just feels right. Melbourne turns it on for Formula 1 in a way none of the other season-opening alternatives does. The festival atmosphere gets the season started with the right attitude.

However, there are two reasons Australia was never going to take top spot on the 2027 calendar.

The first is that Bahrain is the default season opener per its contract. The only reason Melbourne has hosted the last two opening races is because of the timing of the Muslim holy month of Ramadan, which prevented both Bahrain and Saudi Arabia from running their races in March.

Ramadan shifts backwards every year and will conclude in early March next year, freeing both countries to resume their usual position on the schedule.

Melbourne does have contractual rights to a certain number of round 1 slots through to the end of its deal in 2037, in part because of Ramadan. Albert Park is believed to have three more season-opening cards up its sleeve, though it’s unclear when they’ll be used.

The second reason is equally pertinent. Right now there’s no pit building at Albert Park with which to host the race.

As part of a two-season renovation of the track’s permanent infrastructure, the pit buildings were torn down shortly after this year’s race and will be rebuilt just in time for 2027. Committing early to hosting the first race in mid-March would have shaved weeks off the timeline — a meaningful amount of time considering all the other regular set-up work that has to happen in the months before the grand prix.

After the 2027 race, the race control building will be torn down and rebuilt, and a permanent second storey will be constructed on top of the new garages.

But — and there is a but — reports suggest that construction is moving at a good pace and that the building will be complete long before the race’s 4 April date.

That could be critical in answering the next question.

IS THERE ANY RISK BAHRAIN AND SAUDI ARE CALLED OFF AGAIN?

Despite returning to their usual positions at the top of the schedule, there is still significant uncertainty around the viability of both Bahrain and Saudi Arabia as grand prix hosts while the US-Israeli war with Iran continues.

Both races were called off this year shortly after the outbreak of hostilities, and though there was some optimism in June that a ceasefire agreement could allow Bahrain to be rescheduled, the situation subsequently deteriorated again, forcing plans to be shelved.

Instead Bahrain is bankrolling the Malaysian Grand Prix next month. The event will be known as the Bahrain Grand Prix in Malaysia.

With no clear sign that the conflict could be coming to an end, the same uncertainties exist for next year’s calendar.

Formula 1 is determined to host the full allocation of 24 grands prix next season, and the sport has reportedly prepared contingency plans to shuffle the calendar if the situation remains unstable in January.

One option is to shuffle forward the three races in Asia — Australia, Japan and China — to open the season and leave room for the Middle Eastern rounds to be rescheduled later in the year.

That could see Australia return to what Melburnians certainly consider to be its rightful place at the head of the calendar.

Decisive would be whether the new garages and other temporary infrastructure are finished in time to host a race two or three weeks earlier than planned.

If confidence is low that it can be achieved, reports late last month suggested that China will be the preferred season-opening race, with Japan and Australia following on consecutive weekends.

However, if China were to take Bahrain’s date, that would put Australia on the weekend of Easter, something local organisers are unlikely to accept. It might instead see Australia retain its date as a standalone race.

What would happen to Bahrain and Saudi Arabia?

Speculation that they could be rescheduled to the end of the season in a run of four Middle Eastern races to conclude the year appears to be well wide of the mark given the dates announced.

Placing one on either side of the season-ending Qatar-Abu Dhabi double-header would create five consecutive races starting in Las Vegas — completely impractical for anyone working in the paddock — and it would also mean the season would end on 19 December, which would leave too little time for factories to wind down before Christmas.

Instead, it may be more likely that new alternatives — perhaps in Malaysia or Europe, for example — are shuffled into April in lieu of the Asian races to make up the numbers and prevent the loss of momentum experienced earlier this year.

There’s also plenty of space for new races in May, with two weekends spare between Miami and Canada, and the Canadian, Monaco and Portuguese grands prix from late May to late June are all standalone events.

Time was against F1 in finding alternatives this season, but it’s clear that the sport has myriad options to keep the calendar at 24 rounds in 2027.

WHAT ABOUT QATAR AND ABU DHABI THIS SEASON?

There has been an expectation that the 2027 calendar would not be published before a decision was made on where the 2026 season would end, with F1 boss Stefano Domenicali having said earlier this year that the sport would make a call by the middle of September.

That was in part because of the complex web of logistics behind every grand prix.

Races outside Europe are supplied with infrastructure via sea freight. There are several duplicates of all non-essential racing items either at sea or in port around the world at any one time. Only performance-critical items — like the cars themselves — are sent by air.

Last year the sea freight for the Qatar Grand Prix came from Singapore, while the freight for the Abu Dhabi Grand Prix came from Azerbaijan.

The Azerbaijan Grand Prix is next weekend, with Singapore a fortnight later, on 4 October.

However, Racing Bulls principal Alan Permane said last week that sea freight was already “sorted and either there or on its way there, so that won’t be an issue”.

However, ports in both Qatar and Abu Dhabi are accessed via the Strait of Hormuz, through which shipping is severely constricted due to the war. There is also uncertainty around access to the Red Sea via Bab el-Mandeb, which makes accessing the entire Arabian Peninsula difficult.

Governments worldwide continue to warn against travelling to the region. The Australian government advises reconsidering the need to travel to Qatar, Abu Dhabi and Saudi Arabia and to not travel to Bahrain.

The World Endurance Championship has already cancelled races in Bahrain and Qatar, while MotoGP is reportedly set to imminently axe its Qatar Grand Prix, which is currently scheduled for 9 November.

Formula 1 will wait until the last possible moment to make a call, but if it cancels both, it is expected to reinstate the Emilia-Romagna Grand Prix in Imola as the season finale on December 6.

WHY ARE THERE SO MANY SPRINTS NEXT SEASON?

The number of sprints has ticked up gradually, with 10 of the 24 rounds scheduled to feature a short race next season.

That’s up from the original three trialled in 2021, when the sprint race was used as an alternative qualifying format.

Formula 1 believes it’s found a weekend format that works, with two qualifying sessions and two races supplemented by a single hour of practice, and with fans and race promoters enthusiastic, it is rolling sprints out to more venues.

Five of the venues hosting sprint races next season will do so for the first time, including Australia, Bahrain, Japan, Abu Dhabi and, most controversially, Monaco — more on that later.

Speaking to Italian media in Monza, Formula 1 CEO Stefano Domenicali said sprints had proved their worth — including to him, who had been sceptical of their value at first.

“I am the first who, as a Formula 1 dinosaur, had doubts when we proposed this,” he said, per Motorsport. “But the data did not contradict it: promoters, media and fans, in the vast majority, have digested the format and consider it dynamic.

“Young people increasingly appreciate a weekend in which there is already something that counts on Friday.

“Longstanding fans, and I include myself among them, may also be interested in a practice session, but for the general public having qualifying already on Friday means having more content.”

Australian Grand Prix chief Travis Auld said it was clear that having a sprint was a drawcard for fans.

“The introduction of the Formula 1 sprint is an exciting addition for fans and another milestone for our event,” he said.

“Sprint weekends have consistently proven popular with audiences around the world, attracting higher global viewership and delivering more on-track action, with over 80 per cent of attendees saying the format adds value to their ticket.”

In a statement, Formula 1 said its data makes an undeniable case that fans prefer sprint weekends.

“Since the sprint was introduced in 2021, the data shows that total viewership of sprint weekends is 12 per cent higher than non-sprint weekends,” the sport said in a statement.

“Sprint qualifying is up to three times more popular with fans than free practice, 82 per cent of attendees at sprint weekends say it adds value to their ticket, and 75 per cent of F1 fans believe sprint events add greater value to a season’s calendar.”

There is of course also another reason, though.

“The principle is very simple,” Domenicali told a Liberty Media investor call in August. “This is also an opportunity to increment the revenue stream, for sure.

“This is an opportunity for us to have new deals, as we have already seen, and moving in this direction will be our future.

“We want to do it the right way, because of course this will allow us also to make sure that the scarcity is a value.”

Let’s not beat around the bush: more sprints mean more money.

That’s also why the number has been restricted to 10. Maintaining a certain level of scarcity can drive up the cost of hosting a sprint race.

BUT WHY HAS MONACO GOT A SPRINT?

The most surprising announcement of the 2027 calendar confirmation is news that serpentine Monte Carlo will get a sprint race.

The Monaco Grand Prix is notorious for its lack of overtaking opportunities. This year’s race featured just 11 passes, the fewest of every grand prix bar Spain, held at the weekend in Madrid.

It follows in the footsteps of sprints in Zandvoort and Singapore this year, two other tracks that on the surface make little sense given the difficulty of overtaking.

The sprint at the Dutch Grand Prix this year was dire — though the grand prix itself was one of the best of the year. Singapore is scheduled for next month.

Monaco, on the surface, a baffling choice. But there is a certain logic to the decision.

The highlight of the Monaco Grand Prix weekend is qualifying. It’s by far the best qualifying session of the year and a genuine annual highlight.

In 2027 Monaco will feature two qualifying sessions.

Regardless of how much or little value drivers seem to place in sprint races — though more sprints mean they’ll have to be taken more seriously as a source of points — qualifying in Monaco is always spectacular, and having another session featuring drivers taking their cars to the limit can’t be a bad thing.

There’s also an argument to be made that having less practice in Monaco is more likely to result in some cars starting out of pace order, which could boost the admittedly slim odds of overtaking or strategic differences in the race.

At a minimum, the change shakes up the historic event without adulterating either main qualifying or the grand prix itself, which remains prestigious despite the almost total lack of action.

Formula 1 has been racking its brains to find a way to reinvigorate the Monte Carlo weekend. For all the handwringing that will undoubtedly be taking place among the traditionalists, at least the sport has kept its hands off the main event.
