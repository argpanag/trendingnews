Spain demands answers as rail disaster death toll rises

TheTools · 29.10.2026 03:09 · source · permalink

Getty

Spain began demanding answers after the toll from the country's second high-speed train collision this year rose to 69 people, with scores injured and distraught relatives desperately seeking news about their loved ones.

One of Europe's deadliest rail disasters this century struck south of Madrid yesterday morning when a service by state operator Renfe ploughed into the back of a stationary train belonging to private Spanish company Iryo.

Spain's Foreign Ministry said 42 foreign citizens from seven countries were involved in the crash, information available so far showed, of whom one was killed and at least seven injured.

Authorities said earlier that 57 of those injured remained in hospital.

Toledo has received 52 of the corpses, overwhelming its morgue's capacity of 30 and forcing the army to set up an improvised facility.

Forensics experts in green jackets, a black funeral car and army vehicles moved in and out of the site, equipped with red marquees and refrigerated containers.

Sniffer dogs and thermal drones searched for bodies at the crash site outside the central village of Urda, around 130km south of Madrid.

The death toll was 69, according to the transport ministry's website and regional authorities.

Among the victims was Fatima Banez, a former labour minister for the main conservative Popular Party (PP), Spain's CEOE business association confirmed.

Cranes were in place at the crash site to help shift the carriages as police and soldiers helped pick through the wreckage strewn down the gravel slope where at least three train carriages were thrown by the impact.

"Emergency workers and the military emergency brigade are still working with much care because we still can't be sure that there aren't more victims," a spokesperson for the Castilla-La Mancha said.

The crash is Spain's second high-speed rail disaster this year and has raised fresh concerns about the safety of the country's rail network.

Spain's network of high-speed rail has been considered one of the nation's crowning achievements. It ranks second to China in track mileage in the world, and until this year, it had not endured a deadly crash since coming into service more than three decades ago.

But the collision, which turned a sleepy stretch of railway lined by olive groves into a scene of carnage, came just four weeks before the country votes in a national election.

Unions blame lack of funding as train drivers strike

As shocked Spaniards searched for answers on why another crash had occurred, train drivers went ahead with a previously planned strike to press for more investment in railway safety.

"We will continue demanding the necessary measures to make this sector safe again ... It must have all the safety systems required to maintain the quality standards we have always had," said Diego Martín of the train drivers' union SEMAF.

"We cannot tolerate, under any circumstances, this type of incident happening again," he said at Madrid's Atocha railway station.

Renfe, the state-run rail operator, said the strike caused significant disruption to rail services and passengers could change or cancel tickets free of charge and would be notified of affected services.

The Spanish government has declared three days of national mourning for those killed in the crash, which Transport Minister Oscar Puente said happened after several trains stopped due to a signal failure.

A Renfe train was then authorised to proceed at normal speed, even though there was a stationary Iryo-operated train ahead, Mr Puente said.

Mr Puente said that the crash was caused by what he called the "inexplicable" order from a central command centre for Spain's trains after a security system had failed.

He said the train engineers were not to blame.

Mr Puente said that at 8.30am, the security system on a stretch of 63km stopped working due to a computer malfunction, causing all trains on that stretch to stop.

At that point the Iryo train was stopped near the town of Urda, while the Renfe train was 25km behind.

But a few minutes later the central command told the Renfe train to start running again without any speed restrictions, and it ended up colliding with the stationary Iryo train at 8.53am.

Mr Puente said that the train should never have been told to start moving again since there was still a "stop signal" active on that line, and that if it had, at least it should have been told to go at reduced speed.

Unions blamed a lack of funding and will to improve safety, saying talks after January's crash were abandoned by the government in April.

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez visited the crash site in the central province of Toledo.

Josimar Medina, a 31-year-old Peruvian who was holidaying in Spain with his girlfriend, said he was sleeping on the Renfe train when "I woke up and I was in the air".

Pablo Mejia, 42, escaped with injuries to his head, stating that a last-minute change of seat saved his life.

He recounted "a very strong hit ... a lot of people on the floor, on top of each other, seats ripped out, suitcases, a lot of chaos".

Only in January, 46 people died when one train jumped the track and slammed into the side of another going the opposite way in southern Spain.

Mr Sanchez pledged an investigation into the latest collision and improvements to prevent another tragedy like the two that have shaken trust in a rail system that carried 45 million travellers last year, including millions of tourists.

He said: "It is clear that we need to reinforce the safety of our train service to rebuild the trust in our high-speed rail. It is not acceptable for a country like Spain to endure two high-speed train accidents in the same year."

Both deadly crashes this year involved a train operated by the state-owned Renfe company and one from the privately owned Iryo.

Spain only liberalised its high-speed rail service in 2021, with the Italian Iryo starting service the following year.

The Civil Guard has set up four offices in the cities of Madrid, Toledo, Cordoba and Seville to enable relatives of the victims to provide DNA samples and help identify the missing.

Blood donation points have also been set up in Castilla-La Mancha and Madrid.

Jose Miguel Fernandez, a parish priest in Urda, joined the emergency services in offering support to the victims' families.

"What matters most is being with the victims' relatives, that they feel the closeness, because words at a time like this are pointless, but a hug helps," he said.

Recalling how he rushed to the site of the crash, Fr Fernandez said he was confronted with "an image of desolation, despair, uncertainty, death".
