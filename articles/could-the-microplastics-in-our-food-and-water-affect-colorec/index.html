Could The Microplastics In Our Food And Water Affect Colorectal Cancer Risk?

TheTools · 23.11.2026 00:07 · source · permalink

Plastic particles are so common that people ingest and inhale them daily without realizing it. Now, researchers are working to determine whether long-term exposure damages the digestive system and increases the likelihood of developing colorectal cancer (CRC).

A study published in October 2026 in Molecular Cancer adds new urgency to that concern, according to The Washington Post. Researchers at Central South University in China found that mice exposed to polystyrene nanoplastics developed more colorectal tumors and experienced more aggressive tumor growth than mice that were never exposed.

While findings in mice do not prove that microplastics cause cancer in humans, the researchers note that their results build on earlier studies detecting plastic particles in human colorectal tumors and raise new questions about how these particles affect the gut. Understanding every potential risk factor is especially critical as colorectal cancer rates continue to climb among younger adults.

What are microplastics, and how do they enter the human body?

Microplastics are tiny plastic fragments measuring less than 5 millimeters across, while nanoplastics are even smaller—generally under 1 micrometer. Both form as larger plastic items break down in the environment due to sunlight, heat and friction.

Everyday sources such as food packaging, synthetic clothing, household dust and discarded waste shed these particles into the environment. According to the World Health Organization, food, drinking water, and airborne dust are the primary routes of human exposure.

Researchers have also detected microplastics in human blood and organ tissues, though scientists are still investigating how much accumulates in the body and what long-term effects it may have on the gut and immune system. Because colorectal cancer affects both men and women—with men facing a higher overall risk—clarifying these environmental triggers has significant public health implications.

What did the latest study reveal about microplastics and colon cancer?

Published on Oct. 29, 2026, the Central South University study examined how repeated oral exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics—the type of plastic commonly used in foam takeout containers and disposable cups—affects mice with chemically induced colorectal cancer.

Compared with unexposed animals, mice given nanoplastics developed more tumors with more aggressive growth. Tissue analysis showed that the particles accumulated primarily in the colon, damaging microscopic structures on the surface of intestinal cells. Exposure also disrupted the gut microbiome and local immune regulation, notably depleting levels of a beneficial bacterium, Faecalibaculum rodentium, as well as propionic acid, a protective compound involved in tumor control.

Alongside the animal experiments, the investigators detected distinct types of microplastics in the blood and tissues of patients with human colorectal cancer. Even so, animal models and controlled lab doses do not fully replicate everyday human exposure, meaning researchers still need to confirm whether these biological pathways drive cancer in people.

How could microplastics contribute to colorectal cancer development?

Scientists are investigating several ways plastic particles might disrupt the digestive tract:

  • Intestinal lining damage: In the Molecular Cancer study, nanoplastics harmed intestinal microvilli—the microscopic, finger-like projections on gut cells that absorb nutrients and help maintain a healthy barrier between intestinal contents and underlying tissue.

  • Microbiome disruption: Beneficial gut bacteria produce metabolites that calm inflammation and support immune surveillance against abnormal cells. When plastic particles upset that microbial balance, the body's natural defenses may weaken.

  • Mucus barrier penetration: The colon relies on a thick layer of protective mucus to shield its walls from harmful microbes and toxins. Researchers are exploring whether plastic particles can compromise or slip through this barrier.

"My own thoughts are that it's going to prove not to be the microplastics themselves — microplastics are going to prove to be a way of getting through the mucus layer," Frank Frizelle, a professor of surgery at the University of Otago in New Zealand, told The Washington Post in its coverage of the new mouse research. While Frizelle's view remains a working hypothesis, it highlights how plastics could act as a Trojan horse for other irritants in the gut.

Have researchers found microplastics in human colon cancer tumors?

Yes. In May 2025, a study published in Scientific Reports compared tumor tissue with nearby noncancerous tissue from CRC patients. The researchers identified multiple polymers—including polyethylene and polyvinyl chloride—and found a higher concentration and wider variety of plastic particles inside the tumors than in adjacent healthy tissue.

Other analyses have similarly documented microplastics in the blood and tissues of people diagnosed with the disease. Still, finding plastic inside a tumor does not establish cause and effect: researchers do not yet know whether the particles helped trigger tumor growth or whether rapidly growing tumors simply trap more circulating particles after they form. Larger, long-term studies will be necessary to clarify that timeline.

Are microplastics responsible for rising colon cancer rates among younger adults?

While microplastics are under active investigation, researchers say they do not explain the surge in early-onset colorectal cancer. According to American Cancer Society data, CRC incidence among U.S. adults aged 20 to 49 rose by roughly 3% per year between 2013 and 2022, compared with about 0.4% annually among adults aged 50 to 64.

Scientists are examining multiple drivers behind that trend, including diets heavy in ultra-processed foods, obesity, sedentary lifestyles, environmental chemicals and shifts in the gut microbiome. Because plastic production and early-onset cancer rates have risen over overlapping decades, it is easy to assume a direct link—but correlation alone is not proof. Researchers must compare lifetime plastic exposure between people who develop CRC and those who do not while accounting for diet, genetics and lifestyle.

How can people reduce their exposure to microplastics?

Because plastic particles are widespread in food, water and air, eliminating exposure entirely is unrealistic. However, simple everyday swaps can cut down on the particles and chemicals shedding from food containers:

  • Avoid microwaving food in plastic; transfer meals to glass or ceramic dishes before heating.

  • Store food and beverages in glass or stainless-steel containers whenever practical.

  • Keep hot foods and drinks out of single-use or non-heat-safe plastics.

  • Discard scratched, warped or heavily worn plastic containers, which shed particles more readily.

  • Follow manufacturer labels and use only plastics explicitly marked microwave-safe.

  • Cut back on unnecessary single-use plastic food packaging where possible.

While reducing plastic contact is a practical precaution, it is not a proven way to prevent colorectal cancer. Justus Rabach, MD, a physician specializing in clinical medicine and surgery, reminds Blavity Health readers, "As a practical suggestion, do not let concern about microplastics replace proven cancer prevention measures. Follow recommended colorectal cancer screening guidelines and talk to a healthcare professional about personal risk factors."

Why is early colon cancer screening still important?

Colorectal cancer frequently develops without noticeable symptoms in its earliest stages. Regular screening catches the disease early—when treatment is most effective—and can prevent cancer altogether by identifying and removing precancerous growths called polyps.

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends that most adults at average risk begin screening at age 45, using either stool-based tests or a colonoscopy, which allows doctors to examine the colon and remove polyps during the same procedure. People with elevated risk—such as a family history of CRC, certain inherited genetic syndromes, inflammatory bowel disease or past advanced polyps—may need to start earlier or screen more frequently.

Anyone experiencing blood in their stool, persistent changes in bowel habits, unexplained weight loss or ongoing abdominal pain should consult a doctor promptly. While scientists continue investigating how microplastics affect human health, routine screening remains the most reliable defense against colorectal cancer.

Bottom line

You cannot scrub microplastics out of modern life overnight, so focus on the levers you actually control. Swapping out heated plastics for glass or ceramic is an easy, low-effort way to trim daily exposure, while staying on schedule with colorectal cancer screenings—and flagging new digestive symptoms early—remains the most effective way to protect your long-term health.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can colorectal cancer develop without causing noticeable symptoms?

Yes, colorectal cancer can develop without early symptoms, which is why recommended screening is important even when a person feels healthy.

Does having a family history of colon cancer increase your risk of developing the disease?

Yes, having a close relative with colorectal cancer can increase your risk and may mean you need to begin screening earlier or undergo tests more often.
