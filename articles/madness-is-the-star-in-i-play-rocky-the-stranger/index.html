Madness Is the Star in 'I Play Rocky' - The Stranger

TheTools · 20.11.2026 00:17 · source · permalink

The determination to pursue your dreams to the very end is, in essence, an acute form of madness. That’s what I learned from watching I Play Rocky. The subject of the biopic, Sylvester Stallone, became a Hollywood star not because he had any real talent—he obviously couldn’t act, wasn’t eloquent, and didn’t even possess that film presence the French impressionists of the 1920s described as photogénie. According to the film, which I accept as an accurate description of Stallone’s rise to fame in the mid ’70s, the only thing Stallone had going for him was being mad enough to really believe that he should never ever give up on his dreams.

If you want to go to the moon, this kind of madman says, never give up at all costs. Same goes for those who want to be the next president, or the next Michael Jordan or Bill Gates or Princess Di. If a dream is to be realized, it demands nothing less than your total commitment. Make sure it’s there when you wake up, that it burns like a sun in the sky of your mind when you go to work, and, most important of all, it’s in your dreams when you fall asleep at night. There is no room for distractions. No halfstepping. You must never doubt yourself and the amazing you who you want to be. This is the entire substance of I Play Rocky. 

The film’s story: In 1975, Sylvester Stallone (played with eerie perfection by Anthony Ippolito) is young, dead broke, and married with a child on the way. He has only one goal in life: to become a movie star. One night, he chances to watch in an LA dive bar the boxing match between Chuck Wepner and Muhammad Ali. Now, this fight is supposed to be done in no time, but instead it drags on and on. Ali, whose last match was the legendary Rumble in the Jungle, even gets knocked out by Wepner, a white boxer. But by the 15th round, Ali defeats Wepner. Stallone then rushes home and, in three feverish days, writes a script about a white boxer, Rocky Balboa, who, against all odds, beats the heavyweight champion of the world, a Black boxer named Apollo. 

The bad script catches the attention of Hollywood producers and eventually obtains funding. But Stallone, who has only done bit parts in B-films, demands that he play the lead role. The producers offer him tons of dough to drop this ridiculous idea, but broke Stallone will have none of it. It’s either his way or the highway. The producers finally relent, the film is made on a relatively low budget and the suppression of unionized labor power, and Stallone’s dream comes true.

As you can see, this is the story of one madman who, despite writing a bone-dumb script and having no acting chops whatsoever (seriously, watch Rocky), found himself doing something that’s equivalent to walking on water. Miracles do sometimes happen in our otherwise hard-as-a-rock universe, but our market-motivated culture, American culture, packages them as inspirational movies for mass consumption. 

My advice? If you want to protect your mental health, to keep your feet on the ground, to stay woke and not be deranged by an excessive belief in yourself, then do the best you can to not play Rocky.
