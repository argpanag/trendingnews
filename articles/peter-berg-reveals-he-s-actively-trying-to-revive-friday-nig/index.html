Peter Berg Reveals He’s Actively Trying to Revive ‘Friday Night Lights’ | The Rich Eisen Show

TheTools · 26.11.2026 14:55 · source · permalink

Peter Berg came on to talk about The Mosquito Bowl, his World War II football film that spent close to a month in theaters and starts streaming on Netflix on November 25, the same day as the Packers and Rams game. Then he mentioned, almost in passing, that people are talking about bringing back Friday Night Lights, and Rich nearly came out of his chair.

The film follows four college All-American football players in 1941 who were "living in the apex of their glory" when Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. They dropped out of school, joined the Marine Corps and, with very little training, were sent to the Pacific to fight on Tarawa, which Berg called "some of the most barbaric, violent fighting that this country's ever experienced." Some of them later made the ultimate sacrifice on Okinawa.

In between, roughly 50 elite college and pro players, all Marines, played a football game on Guadalcanal, where for a moment they "just forget about the war, be happy, play a football game." Fifteen of those players lost their lives.

What stuck with Berg during his research was a number: 75 million people killed in World War II. That figure reshaped the project. "It really in many ways became a very, very strong anti-war film," he said. His summary of the whole thing was simple: he wants "a lot more football and a lot less war."

Rich pointed to Ray Nicholson in the cast, and Berg lit up. Being Jack Nicholson's son and saying you want to do this "is not an easy tool to pick up," Berg said, adding, "He's got the charisma and you can see the Nicholson gene in his eyes, but he's his own man."

The football scenes required outside help. "Whenever you meet an actor and you ask them if they can do anything, they lie, right? Actors are liars," he said. All his actors swore they could play football. They were lying.

So he called Christian McCaffrey, who brought in his own team of about eight movement, balance and "mental gymnast" coaches, then ran routes with the cast himself.

Rich asked how a star handles people who just don't get it. Berg said it never came to that. "The second they were around him, they realized they better not act like a fool," he said. "Christian just has the ability to command respect."

Rich couldn't resist pointing out that if actors are liars, Pat McAfee fits right in. Rich retold McAfee's story from a past visit: McAfee told the Colts he could hold, then had to hold for Adam Vinatieri, who sniffed him out immediately and sent him to what may or may not have been a real holding camp. "If you're not lying, you're not cheating, you're not trying," Berg said. "So God bless Pat."

Berg then made the case for McAfee's commitment. The two met on a Super Bowl commercial, McAfee asked to be in the film, and Berg saw a drill instructor in him. Asked whether he'd be dedicated, McAfee went to Parris Island with his best friend, a former drill instructor there, and spent 10 hours a day with recruits and drill instructors. McAfee, Berg said, was "straight up wanting to honor the United States Marine Corps. So I tip my hat to Pat."

Berg added that McAfee had been taking acting lessons from Bradley Cooper and Tom Cruise. "I could feel the ghost of Tom Cruise and Bradley Cooper right behind him with all the questions he was asking me," he said.

Berg directed the Netflix open for the NFL game in Melbourne, the one with Kyle Chandler channeling his Friday Night Lights role. Then he said this: "I miss Friday Night Lights. We've been talking about maybe figuring out a way to bring it back."

Rich stopped everything. Berg admitted he wasn't really supposed to talk about it, then talked anyway. The key, he said, is Buzz Bissinger, who lived in Odessa, Texas, for a year at Permian High School. Bissinger's reporting on people like Boobie Miles, Don Billingsley and Coach Gaines was "the foundation of the show," Berg said. "It wasn't the movie. It wasn't the TV show. It was an incredible sociological exploration of football, high school culture in America."

The plan is to send Bissinger back. "Buzz, I know you're watching. Get out of Philadelphia and get to Texas," Berg said. "We've got the schools. We want to embed with a school and come up with a whole new story for 2026, '27."

Why now? The transfer portal and name, image and likeness came up, along with the idea that Buddy Garrity today would either be in jail or president. The high school football world is "a radically different planet" than when Bissinger first went there, and Berg said any new version "would be a complete reinvention," with Bissinger as the key.

Movie or TV series? "Honestly, I can't talk about it," Berg said. So Rich pitched his own version: Coach Taylor, years later, either emeritus or coaching again because he can't quit it, with his daughter grown and his wife okay with it or not, depending on the better plotline. Berg's answer: "Sort of." There would be some old elements and some brand new ones. When Rich asked whether Brad Leland was still around, Berg said, "Everybody's around."

Rich admitted he was geeking out: the night Texas Stadium closed, the one person he left the NFL Network set to talk to was Brad Leland. Berg, for his part, said if he's part of bringing the show back, "the essence and the spirit of Mike Leach would live somewhere in that show."

Rich called Leach's cameo at the gas station spectacular, and Berg explained how it happened. Nobody on the crew knew who Leach was, and neither did Kyle Chandler. Berg told Leach to give Chandler's character a pep talk after a couple of losses and, if possible, mention swinging a sword. He told Chandler nothing.

Leach told Chandler he had a losing spirit and his sword was down, and he needed to get it up and swing it. "If you look at Chandler's face during that cameo, he has no idea what's happening," Berg said. It was one take. Berg called it one of his top moments on a set ever.

He shared one more Leach story. When Berg coached his son's 10-year-old flag football team, Leach was staying at his house, came to practice and drew up a playbook. Leach's theory was that 10-year-old brains can't cover crossing patterns. The team went undefeated and beat every opponent by at least 30 points. Does Berg still have the playbook? "Oh, I wish."

Chandler wasn't Berg's choice. He wanted Dwight Yoakam, who dropped out over a concert tour conflict, and thought Chandler was too soft from Early Edition.

Chandler showed up to the meeting hung over from a bachelor party in Vegas. "I'm like, I like this. I like the way you look right now. Can you do this every day?" Berg recalled. Chandler said he couldn't be hung over every day, but he could bring the energy. Within three days of shooting the pilot, Berg joked, "I'm a genius."

Jesse Plemons was "basically an extra" with one line in the first episode, Berg said, but he made something out of nothing in a moment with Sarah's mother, and the writers, including Jason Katims, kept giving him more. Michael B. Jordan was a different story. In his first audition, he pretended to be a linebacker and tackled Berg into a wall. "Tackled me pretty hard, which I liked," Berg said.

Rich added Aldis Hodge, and Connie Britton came up too. Rich called her spectacular and said she has to be in the mix for any reboot. Berg's response was to invite Rich into the writers room. "I'm in," Rich said.

Berg's NFL work continues on Netflix. He did the season opener with Chandler, ending in "clear eyes, full heart, can't lose." The new one for the Packers and Rams game carries a Thanksgiving tone, with Christmas and the playoffs still to come. One is left to shoot, and Chandler is currently on a research trip in the middle of the Atlantic studying deep water octopus. Berg is hoping he comes back.
