EasyJet flight forced to land after mid-air emergency at 15,000ft over Britain

TheTools · 18.11.2026 10:33 · source · permalink

GB NEWS

By Marcus Donaldson

An EasyJet flight was forced to land at Bristol Airport after experiencing an in-flight emergency.

The plane, travelling from Bristol to Edinburgh, transmitted an alert after a bird strike.

The aircraft sent a squawk 7700, a general emergency code used to alert air traffic control that an aircraft is experiencing an emergency.

According to tracking site Squawk7700, easyJet flight EZY897J issued the emergency code at 9.36am this morning while flying at 15,000ft.

The flight departed Bristol Airport at 9.24am, nine minutes behind its scheduled 9.15am departure, and was due to arrive in the Scottish capital at 10.35am.

An update on EasyJet’s website warns passengers of the “extraordinary circumstance” forcing the diversion.

“We're very sorry that your flight has now been diverted to Bristol,” it read.

“This is due to a bird strike on your aircraft. The disruption to your flight is outside of our control and is considered to be an extraordinary circumstance.”

The EasyJet flight landed safely at Bristol Airport and is now undergoing checks.

A bird strike occurs when a bird collides with an aircraft, potentially damaging its engines, windscreen, wings, or other components.

The impact can range from minor, with little or no damage to the aircraft, to more serious incidents requiring pilots to divert or return to the airport.

Birds can be sucked into jet engines, potentially damaging fan blades and disrupting the engine's ability to operate properly.

In more severe cases, multiple bird strikes can affect both engines, reducing or even causing a loss of thrust.

Bird strikes can also damage an aircraft's nose, cockpit windows, or flight control surfaces, potentially reducing visibility or compromising the plane's ability to fly safely.

Pilots are trained to respond to bird strikes, and aircraft are designed and tested to withstand certain levels of impact, although the severity depends on the size and number of birds involved and where they hit the aircraft.

The most famous example of a bird strike causing a major aviation emergency was the “Miracle on the Hudson” in January 2009.

An Airbus A320 struck a flock of birds shortly after takeoff from New York, and its captain heroically landed the plane in the Hudson River.

US Airways Flight 1549 hit a flock of Canada geese minutes after departing LaGuardia Airport, causing both engines to lose power.

The birds were sucked into the engines, damaging the internal components and leaving the pilots unable to generate sufficient thrust to continue flying normally.

With the aircraft losing power and unable to safely return to the airport, Captain Chesley “Sully” Sullenberger and First Officer Jeffrey Skiles decided to ditch the plane in the river.

The aircraft landed on the water, with all 155 passengers and crew surviving the emergency.

Passengers were evacuated onto the wings and inflatable slides as rescue boats rushed to the scene.
