Bruce Willis' '80s HBO Movie Deserves More Attention For Its Ambitious Premise - SlashFilm

TheTools · 11.10.2026 19:00 · source · permalink

Those of us who were alive in 1987 likely remember the phenomenon of Bruce Willis' "The Return of Bruno." Thanks to his stint on "Moonlighting," Willis was already something of a household name, although this came before the release of "Die Hard," so he wasn't yet known as a blockbuster action star. Heck, "The Return of Bruno" hit record store shelves a few months before the release of Blake Edwards' "Blind Date," so Willis wasn't even associated very strongly with movies yet. 

But everyone hooted at "The Return of Bruno," alternately mocking its ambition and its audacity. For the album, Willis semi-assumed an alter ego named Bruno Radolini, and released a hit single, a cover of the Staples Singers' "Respect Yourself," in December of 1986. The single was reviewed by Spin Magazine. The entire record was a collection of blues, soul, and R&B tracks, and was put out by Motown Records. 

I recall, through very hazy memory, that people hated "The Return of Bruno," wondering why an actor best known (at the time) for comedic chops bothered to do something so out-of-character. The project dripped of ego. Also, the title was confusing. Who was Bruno, and where did he return from?

The answers were provided by a near-forgotten 1987 HBO special, also called "The Return of Bruno," a mockumentary about the fictional life of Bruno Radolini, and his imaginary musical legacy. It was like "This is Spinal Tap" or the mockumentaries about The Rutles. It came with a lot of performances by notable soul singers and blues stars, lending it a little pop cred. Although, since no one liked the record, it's cred didn't stretch very far.

There was a tie-in TV special for Bruce Willis' 1987 album The Return of Bruno

The joke behind the "Return of Bruno" TV special was that this mysterious musician, Bruno Radolini, was actually the most influential musician of all time, beloved by everyone in the music business. Many, many game rock stars and celebrities appeared in the special to joke along with Willis. Ringo Starr notes that Bruno is the reason The Beatles existed at all. Michael J. Fox admits to being a huge fan ("There's about 20 of us"), giving a hilarious, earnest performance. Brian Wilson, Grace Slick, Elton John, Graham Nash, Phil Collins, Jon Bon Jovi, and many more give jokey testimonials about the influence Bruno had on the world.

The film contains many performances, as the back half is essentially a concert film. The entire special is only about an hour long. It's not available on any streaming services, but it doesn't take too long to find bootlegs and VHS rips online. 

It's hard to tell, but the central joke of "The Return of Bruno" might be that Bruce Willis is actually not a great musician. Some might call his style of music "white boy blues," what with his not-the-least-bit-cool sunglasses and fashions, his growly voice, and harmonica eating. He wears a ballcap all askew, and it's hard to tell if he's mocking the style or trying to make it happen. Musically, it's overproduced and sweaty. Is "The Return of Bruno" meant to be a spoof? Or is it just a winky way of aggrandizing a new record by over-inflating its importance? Whatever is happening here, "The Return of Bruno" was ambitious and startling. How did Willis, only a rising star at the time, manage to loop in so many famous people for this bonkers project? 

Even more bonkers: Bruce Willis turned Bruno into a bizarre animated TV show.

What did people think of The Return of Bruno?

Every shot in "The Return of Bruno" is hilarious, as everyone looks like they're about to crack up. You can tell that as soon as the cameras stopped rolling, everyone was chuckling up a storm. It's possible that Bruce Willis recruited so many people under the auspices that this was all just for yuks. 

A vintage review from People Magazine gave the TV special a B+, noting that it was funny, although it questioned Bruce Willis' future as a musician: 

"[I]n the last half, when he rocks for real before a live audience, Willis shows us that he can't shout songs quite as well as Don Johnson. So Springsteen he ain't. Funny he is. The special won't make Willis a rock star, but it does add to his portfolio as an entertainer."

The problem with reviews like that is that the TV special was meant to be a marketing gimmick for the record, and the record has none of the winking self-effacing humor of the special. It's just a straight blues record. And as a blues record — if I may offer my own editorial — it kind of sucks. A review on AllMusic remarked that Willis "doesn't have the conviction or skill of the Blues Brothers." Critic Stephen Thomas Erlewine added that "the album stands more as a testament to the excesses of Reagan-era celebrity and baby-boomer nostalgia than as a piece of music." This is apt. 

In 2026, some aging Gen-Xers might only listen to "The Return of Bruno" ironically. Bruce Willis went on to star in "Die Hard" (we ranked John McClane as one of the 15 best Bruce Willis roles) and now cassettes of "Bruno" can be found in the novelty bin at your local record store.
