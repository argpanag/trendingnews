Peter May, bestselling author of atmospheric crime novels set in France, China and the Isle of Lewis

TheTools · 29.09.2026 20:35 · source · permalink

His research for Lockdown warned him of the dangers ​of infectious disease and, years before Covid, he applied sanitiser after shaking hands

Peter May, who has died aged 74, was the bestselling author of more than 30 books, including the Lewis Trilogy in which he powerfully captures the island’s atmosphere, traditions and customs; in evocative descriptions unlikely to have pleased the Outer Hebrides tourist board, the bleakness of the island is matched only by the darkness of its inhabitants’ souls.

The series begins with The Blackhouse (2011), a gripping tale centred around Fin Macleod, a Lewis-born detective living in Edinburgh whose personal life is in turmoil. Macleod had been part of a team examining an as-yet unsolved killing, but when a gory murder on Lewis ​presents many similarities he returns to his former homeland to investigate, reviving disturbing memories and forcing him back into a world that he had been glad to leave.

Rejected by numerous British publishers, The Blackhouse was taken on by a French publisher, winning awards both there and in America, before being acquired by Quercus in this country and spending several weeks in the bestseller charts. It was followed by The Lewis Man (2012), which has Macleod investigating the death of a young man found in a peat bog, and The Chess Men (2013), in which our hero discovers the body of a musician friend who had vanished 17 years previously.

May wrote only of places he knew and his haunting depictions of Lewis were informed by Machair, the 1990s Gaelic-language soap opera he created for Scottish Television, which was filmed in the Outer Hebrides. He later returned to the islands for The Black Loch (2024), a sequel to the trilogy, in which Macleod is called in when the body of a teenage television personality is found on the west coast of Lewis – only to find that the victim is connected to his own family.

The seven books of his earlier China Thrillers series, which features a Beijing detective called Li Yan and​ an American forensic pathologist, Margaret Campbell, were inspired by a day trip in the 1980s across the border from Hong Kong to Shenzhen. An American criminologist who had trained Chinese police officers helped arrange for him to interview several of them.

On one occasion he visited the Shanghai mortuary and saw the body of a man who had been executed the previous day. “It makes you feel very human, very vulnerable, to see someone so young, so dead and so totally butchered,” he said.

He was flattered when the first book in the China Thrillers series, Firemaker (1999), was translated into Chinese, and even more so when he was invited to write a column for Contemporary World Police, a Chinese police magazine, comparing Chinese officers with their counterparts in the West. The Runner (2003), another book in the series, is set against the backdrop of the Beijing Olympics and involves the mysterious deaths of several Chinese athletes.

May’s other major series, the seven-book Enzo Files, was set in south-west France, where he lived for more than 25 years, and features the forensics expert Enzo MacLeod, who was in part based on himself. “At the opening of the series he was just turning 50, which was around my own age at the time,” May said. “He had long hair tied back in a ponytail, just like me. He even dressed like me, in cargo pants and baggy shirts and sneakers – an old hippy, as I had often been called. He is a lover of good wine … and loves to cook, as do I.”

Peter Marr May was born in Clarkston, south of Glasgow, on December 20 1951, the son of Leslie May, a teacher at Shawlands Academy, and his wife Mary, née Black, known as Molly. From an early age he wanted to write and at four penned his first book in longhand. His headmaster at Eastwood Senior Secondary School had little faith in his prospects, grabbing him by the collar one day and saying: “Take your long hair and your big furry coat and go home and don’t come back.” He did not.

His teenage band the Aristokrats often played Saturday-night dances at Clarkston tennis club, a period he drew from in Runaway (2015), in which five teenage musicians from Glasgow disappear to London in quest of stardom only to get caught up in a savage murder; 50 years later they return to uncover the truth.

In real life May and his bandmates ran out of money in London and made their way back to Glasgow, where he became a clerk with National Savings and then a trainee car salesman, before taking a journalism course at Edinburgh College of Commerce.

At 19 his first serious attempt at a novel, Portrait, was rejected by Philip Ziegler, then an editor at Collins, though with encouraging words. “We feel you ought to go on writing, and would like to see anything you write in future – which may not sound very much, but is, I can assure you, a great deal more than we say to 95 per cent of the people who send in their typescripts,” Ziegler wrote.

Joining the Paisley Daily Express, May was named Scotland’s Young Journalist of the Year in 1973. He then spent five years with The Scotsman and a year at the Glasgow Evening Times, recalling how he covered everything from youths tying dogs to railway lines so that their heads got cut off to old ladies desperately trying to get help from the council for their leaking roofs.

During that time his first novel, The Reporter (1978), written after hours in the newspaper office, was published. “They always say write about what you know, so I did,” he said. BBC Scotland asked him to adapt it for a 13-part television series, The Standard (1978).

This marked the launch of a screenwriting career that included more than 200 episodes for the ITV daytime soap opera Take the High Road set on the scenic banks of Loch Lomond. He also created The Squadron (1982), involving a fictional RAF rapid-deployment force, but quit television to concentrate on novel-writing.

May was a disciplined writer who wrote ​to a fixed schedule, producing 3,000 words a day. He published several standalone books including A Silent Death (2020), set in the region of southern Spain known as Costa del crime. While bringing his readers to warmer climes after the bone-freezing landscape of Lewis, it was in many respects a far darker place.

During the Covid-19 outbreak he dusted off a pandemic novel written 15 years earlier and forgotten about. Lockdown (2020), about a Metropolitan Police officer tracking killers while London is at the epicentre of a global pandemic, had been dismissed by publishers at the time as too far-fetched. His research made him keenly aware of the dangers ​of infectious disease and, years before Covid, he would privately apply sanitiser at the first opportunity after shaking hands.

Having announced his retirement, May recently delivered his final book, Island of the Dead, set on a fictional version of the abandoned St Kilda, in the north Atlantic, which will be published in January. Meanwhile, he had returned to music, and in 2025 released a CD, Towards the Light, its title track written from his father’s perspective as dementia robbed him of memories and lucidity.

May married Katrina Hutcheson in 1972. They had a daughter Carol who, like Enzo MacLeod, he lost custody of during their divorce. “My lifelong guilt about this became Enzo’s great emotional burden, and the series in many ways deals with his process of coming to terms with that,” he told a literary festival.

In 1990 he married Janice Hally, a fellow writer whom he met when they both worked in television. She survives him.
