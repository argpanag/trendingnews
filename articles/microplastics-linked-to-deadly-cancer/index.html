Microplastics Linked to Deadly Cancer

TheTools · 23.11.2026 00:07 · source · permalink

The more doctors learn about microplastics and their suspected effects on human health, the more alarmed they become.

Research has already suggested that the tiny plastic fragments can react chemically inside our bodies, accumulate in our brains, and possibly even degrade our bones. Now, a study by researchers at Central South University in China has linked microplastics to colon cancer, one of the deadliest types of the disease.

In experiments involving mice that were genetically modified to be more likely to develop colon cancer, the researchers found that long-term exposure to polystyrene made it more likely for the animals to develop more and more severe tumors.

While we’re still far from confirming any causal links, it certainly doesn’t bode well. The researchers found that the tiny particles accumulated in the guts of the mice, throwing off the balance of their gut biomes.

Researchers who were not involved in the research told the Washington Post that there may well be a connection. The grim context is that the prevalence of colon cancer has skyrocketed among young adults in recent years, becoming the second leading cause for cancer-related death in both men and women in the US. One in four men are likely to develop the cancer over their lifetime.

Scientists suggest that risk factors, that first came into play half a century ago could be to blame. One such risk factor could be the widespread prevalence of plastics, including polystyrene, in our daily lives.

“There’s been a rise in consumption of ultra-processed foods,” College of Pharmacy at University of South Carolina professor Lorne Hofseth told WaPo. “There’s been a rise in ‘forever chemicals.’ There has been a rise in the synthetic food dyes. But there’s also been a rise in microplastics, a significant rise.”

Researchers believe that microplastics could be breaking down the mucus layer, a barrier that protects the colon wall from toxins.

“My own thoughts are that it’s going to prove not to be the microplastics themselves — microplastics are going to prove to be a way of getting through the mucus layer,” University of Otago in New Zealand professor of surgery Frank Frizelle told the newspaper.

Fortunately, we’re not entirely helpless. Middle-aged men and women are being encouraged to be screened for colorectal cancer at a younger age, which can give them far better changes of a full recovery.

However confirmation that microplastics are playing a direct role would be a soul-searching moment about the effects of environmental degradation on human health.

“With food, I do wonder if we need to think about things a little differently,” Frizelle told the WaPo. “And just think about how we can reduce contamination in our food.”
