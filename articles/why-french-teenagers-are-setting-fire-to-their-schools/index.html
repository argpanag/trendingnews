Why French teenagers are setting fire to their schools

TheTools · 01.10.2026 16:08 · source · permalink

Pupils cite rat-infested classrooms, absent teachers and overcrowded lessons as hard-Left blamed for violent unrest

There are many reasons teenagers resent going to school. Early starts, no access to their phones, too much homework.

But in France, these frustrations have prompted a wave of violent youth protests, and the hard-Left, which needs the young to carry them in the next election, have been blamed for fanning the flames.

With the country bracing for fresh violence on Thursday, the protests have become a controversial political issue among presidential contenders eight months before Emmanuel Macron steps down.

The movement began on Sept 21 at a lycée in Créteil, a Paris suburb.

Far from dying down, it is “widening”, according to Laurent Nuñez, the interior minister, who warned on Wednesday of a “significant rise in violence”.

Some 356 schools were disrupted during the day, 625 people were arrested and 83 members of the security forces injured. Édouard Geffray, the education minister, said 119 pupils and staff had been injured since the unrest began.

Police have come under attack from firework-wielding teenagers, who have erected burning barricades outside their schools. A fireman was filmed engulfed in flames after an aerosol can exploded in a blaze outside a Bordeaux lycée.

At Jean-Pierre Timbaud lycée in Aubervilliers, a north Paris suburb, a headteacher was hit in the leg by a firework mortar. Another was struck over the head by a teenager not from his school, while one young protester lost the use of part of his hand after apparently mishandling a rocket.

Police have also been accused of brutality after a 14-year-old lost several teeth when an officer fired a tear-gas grenade launcher into his jaw at point-blank range during a blockade outside his high school near Paris.

The police watchdog has launched an investigation, while prosecutors maintain the boy had struck a policewoman beforehand.

With the unrest spreading far beyond the Paris suburbs, including Lille, Bordeaux and Lyon, Mr Macron intervened on Wednesday to call for calm.

“I can hear and understand the anxieties and anger that our youth can feel,” the French president said.

“However, none of these fears legitimises violence,” he added before accusing “many political leaders” of going to schools “to whip up the pupils ... I think that is serious”.

‘Clear desire for disorder’

An intelligence report warned that political groups on France’s hard-Left had been inflaming tensions in the run-up to the presidential election.

The note, obtained by BFMTV, a French news channel, said: “The origins of this movement lie in an instrumentalisation by student union structures and political movements close to the far-Left.”

It warned of a possible “strategy of conflagration” by some of the most radical political and union groups “to impose their ideas”, and described a “clear desire for disorder”.

Pupils and unions dispute this, saying their anger is rooted in genuine failings in French schools.

Back-to-school protests are an annual French ritual, usually involving union-led strikes over pay, staffing and class sizes. Barricades and clashes with riot police are not uncommon.

Yet this time, masked outsiders, fireworks, and lightning-fast social-media choreography have sent government alarm bells ringing. Intelligence officers say electric scooters are being used to ferry firework mortars between schools and help suspects escape.

Students bemoan homework and phone rules

At Auguste-Blanqui lycée in Saint-Ouen, Lokman, a 16-year-old student, said: “At first, it was a peaceful blockade, but it went too far when about 15 young people from outside the school, who had come from another town, took the opportunity to run riot.”

Slick AI-generated blockade posters are proliferating on TikTok and Snapchat, some announcing protests that never take place, disrupting police attempts to anticipate unrest.

The posters offer a catalogue of teenage discontent. One lycée’s pupils complained of finishing at 5.50pm and having “too many flights of stairs” to climb. Others cite too much homework, long canteen queues, hot classrooms, late buses and restrictions on mobile phones.

Outside Jean-Jaurès lycée in Montreuil, placards have read “Get Macron out”, “LFI 2027” and “Mélenchon 2027” referring to Jean-Luc Mélenchon, leader of the hard-Left France Unbowed.

Yet the revolt began with more genuine demands.

The first blockade was at Lycée Saint-Exupéry in Créteil, where pupils complained that the school lacked a deputy head, nurse and support worker for children with special needs.

It spread rapidly through poorer Paris suburbs amid anger over absent teachers, dilapidated buildings and overcrowded classes.

At one Saint-Ouen school, a 14-year-old complained of days running from 8am to 6pm, classes of 34 pupils, and even a lack of desks and chairs.

Pupils at Paul-Éluard lycée in Saint-Denis have complained of rats and cockroaches, and classrooms swinging between sweltering heat and bitter cold.

‘Nobody listens’

Parcoursup, France’s university admissions system, has become another lightning rod, with pupils accusing the platform of reinforcing social inequalities.

Ryad Rani, head of the Union Syndicale Lycéenne, a French high school union, said: “When we organise petitions and demonstrations, nobody listens to us. That’s why we’ve ended up here.”

The government has pointed the finger at Mélenchon’s France Unbowed, with Sébastien Lecornu, the prime minister, accusing its officials of an “irresponsible escalation”.

Valérie Pécresse, the centre-Right president of the Paris region, called them “arsonist firefighters”. She said: “It’s a ploy. [France Unbowed] is trying to reignite the Yellow Vest movement... it’s trying to organise another general strike.”

Rubens Strachan, of UNI, the Right-wing student union, said: “We’ve gone from ‘block your school’ to ‘vandalise your school’.”

Race for the presidency

The political battle comes as polls suggest Mr Mélenchon could well reach the presidential run-off against National Rally’s Marine Le Pen.

A Toluna Harris poll conducted from Sept 22-24 put Mr Mélenchon at 16 to 17 per cent, depending on the first-round line-up, and said he was “in a position to qualify for the second round” in some scenarios.

Young voters are crucial to his support. Le Figaro noted that some lycée pupils now demonstrating will be old enough to vote in spring, and described them as a potentially valuable pool for Mr Mélenchon.

Sensing the danger, presidential contenders joined the attack on Wednesday.

Gabriel Attal, a Centrist contender to replace Mr Macron, accused France Unbowed of using young people as “political cannon fodder”, saying: “They don’t give a damn about the substance and solving the problems. All they want is to create chaos.”

Édouard Philippe, his centre-Right rival, said a “politicised minority, instrumentalised by France Unbowed” was “wrecking schools”.

Mr Mélenchon hit back, accusing Mr Macron of approving “the police violence he ordered against young people”, while insisting his party “calls for non-violent methods of struggle”.

France Unbowed officials have been highly visible at blockades after Mr Mélenchon instructed them to “interpose” themselves between pupils and police.

In Lille on Wednesday, Aurélien Le Coq, a France Unbowed MP, wearing his tricolour sash, was filmed standing in front of riot-control officers during a police charge.

The highest-profile case has been in Saint-Denis, where Bally Bagayoko, the France Unbowed mayor, was carried shoulder-high by cheering pupils and sang the Marseillaise with them.

A short video appeared to depict him beside a burning bin, but later footage showed him moving it away from the school gates. Even so, Mr Bagayoko caused outrage by saying violence could sometimes “be legitimate” even if it was preferable to avoid it.

Mr Geffray responded: “Violence is not legitimate. What is legitimate are the demands ... they must be heard and listened to.”

In 2024, French lower-secondary school teachers earned 18 per cent less than other university-educated workers, while average teacher pay was around €3,090 (£2,650) net a month.

France’s Pisa 2025 results were among its lowest ever in mathematics, reading and science, although pupils remained close to the OECD average overall.

The school unrest comes at a perilous moment for the government. France’s public debt has climbed to a record €3.6tn (£3.1tn), or 119 per cent of GDP, as Mr Lecornu seeks tens of billions of euros in savings.

Organisers of the disturbances have called for an “Act Two” on Thursday, including university blockades, followed by a national mobilisation on Tuesday.

François Hollande, the former Socialist president, warned this week: “When young people are in the street, it’s like toothpaste coming out of the tube. Getting it back in is very complicated.”

He added: “The little spark can set the plain ablaze.”
