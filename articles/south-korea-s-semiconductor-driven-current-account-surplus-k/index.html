South Korea's Semiconductor-Driven Current Account Surplus: KIEP Says Weak Investment and Aging Population Also Widen the Gap — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 23.11.2026 07:35 · source · permalink

The semiconductor export boom, driven by expanded artificial intelligence (AI) investment, has been confirmed as the key engine behind South Korea's current account surplus this year. However, with analysts pointing to weak domestic investment and precautionary savings tied to population aging as structural factors inflating the surplus, questions are being raised about whether the widening surplus should be viewed purely as good news.

According to the Korea Institute for International Economic Policy (KIEP)'s report titled "Structural Changes in Global Imbalances and South Korea's Response," published on November 10, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimated that expanded semiconductor demand explains about 90% of the increase in South Korea's current account surplus as a share of gross domestic product (GDP) last year. Because the current account balance equals domestic savings minus domestic investment, a rise in savings or a decline in investment widens the surplus.

The problem is that the feedback loop returning this surplus to domestic investment and household income remains weak. KIEP emphasized that "structural savings-investment gaps — including savings set aside for aging and sluggish domestic investment — underlie the current account surplus," adding that "these must be addressed separately from the semiconductor export growth factor." The institute further noted that "semiconductor export income is first accumulated as corporate savings, and if it does not flow back into domestic investment and household income, the savings-investment gap could widen structurally."

The investment slump is most evident in construction. In its second-half economic outlook released on November 18, the Korea Development Institute (KDI) projected that facility investment would grow 8.7% this year while construction investment would remain flat. While non-residential construction such as semiconductor plants improved, residential construction continued to lag due to a slump in regional housing markets. The explanation is that although facility investment increased on the semiconductor boom, the contraction in construction investment was larger, resulting in an overall decline in total investment.

On the savings side, households increasing savings for retirement preparation — amid an insufficient social safety net — is cited as a structural backdrop for the surplus. KDI forecast that real GDP would grow 3.8% this year while private consumption would expand only 1.8%. The analysis suggests that the income gains from the semiconductor boom are spreading slowly across households broadly, and high inflation is constraining spending.

Growth is also heavily concentrated in semiconductors. According to KDI, the semiconductor sector accounts for about 50% of this year's GDP growth, and next year semiconductors are expected to account for about two-thirds of the projected 3.9% growth rate. KDI presented growth forecasts of 3.8% for this year and 3.9% for next year, while the IMF, in its annual consultation on November 17, projected 3.9% for this year and 3.3% for next year.

Note: KDI released its forecasts on November 18; the IMF on November 17.

Employment indicators show a divergence from the growth trend. KDI projected that employed persons would increase by 120,000 this year from a year earlier — the smallest gain since 2018 (97,000), excluding 2020 when employment fell by 219,000 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The added jobs were also concentrated in health and welfare services. According to Statistics Korea, employed persons rose by an average of 119,000 per month from January to October, but excluding health and social welfare services, other industries actually shed 108,000 jobs.

In manufacturing, too, production and employment moved in opposite directions. Based on Bank of Korea national accounts, manufacturing output grew 7.0% year-on-year in the third quarter, while manufacturing employment fell by an average of 31,000 per month over the same period. By age, total employment rose by 139,000 in October, with those aged 60 and older increasing by 196,000 while those under 60 fell by 57,000. Youth employment (ages 15-29) declined by 196,000, marking 48 consecutive months of decline, and the youth employment rate stood at 42.9%, down for the 30th straight month.

Kim Ji-yeon, head of economic outlook at KDI's Economic Outlook Office, said that "total consumption will expand, but whether this translates into improved conditions for small business owners will depend on how consumption is composed, leaving uncertainty." She added that "employment is showing a gradual improvement, but employment among those in their 20s remains weak," and assessed that "while the manufacturing recovery has eased the employment slump and widened the increase in employed persons, the employment rate for those in their 20s remains at a low level."

As for policy direction, KIEP recommended easing bottlenecks in productive investment — including semiconductors, AI data centers, and power grids — so that export income flows back into domestic investment. On fiscal management, it advised caution in using the temporary tax revenue increase from the semiconductor boom for stimulus spending.

The analysis shows that a widening current account surplus does not necessarily mean a recovery in domestic demand. Given that growth could falter quickly if the semiconductor cycle turns or global AI investment slows, broadening the channels that connect export income to facility and construction investment and household income is emerging as the key challenge for South Korea's economy going forward.
