This Week's Sky at a Glance, November 13 – 22

TheTools · 13.11.2026 16:47 · source · permalink

Celestial News & Events

FRIDAY, NOVEMBER 13

■ As November advances, Orion clears the eastern horizon as early as 8 or 9 p.m. (depending on how far east or west you live in your time zone). High over Orion shines orange Aldebaran. Above Aldebaran is the little Pleiades cluster, the size of your fingertip at arm's length. Two or three fists left of Aldebaran and the Pleiades, you'll hit bright Capella.

Down below Orion, Sirius rises around 10 or 11 p.m. Orion's three-star belt points down to it. No matter where they are in the sky, Sirius always follows along two hours behind Orion. Or equivalently, one month behind Orion.

■ Jupiter and Mars are poised together high in Leo before and during dawn all week. Tomorrow morning, as shown below, they're almost at their November 16th conjunction. They and Regulus now fit within a 5.3° circle.

P.S.: Today is Friday the 13th, of no astronomical significance. Heed the great Yankee catcher Yogi Berra, who supposedly said, "I'm not superstitious. It brings bad luck."

Which is true! It detaches your decisions from reality.

SATURDAY, NOVEMBER 14

■ Pull yourself out of bed in early dawn tomorrow and look east-southeast. It may be cold out, but the two hottest planets in the solar system await you there as shown below. And before the sky gets too bright, can you make out Spica, one of the hottest bright stars?

SUNDAY, NOVEMBER 15

■ Vega is the brightest star high in the west on November evenings. Its little constellation Lyra extends to its left, pointing as always more or less toward Altair, currently the brightest star in the southwest.

Three of Lyra's stars near Vega are interesting doubles. Barely above Vega is 4th-magnitude Epsilon Lyrae, the Double-Double. Epsilon forms one corner of a roughly equilateral triangle with Vega and Zeta Lyrae. The triangle is less than 2° on a side, hardly the width of your thumb at arm's length.

Binoculars easily resolve Epsilon. And a 4-inch telescope at 100× or more should, during good seeing, resolve each of Epsilon's wide components into a tight pair.

Zeta is also a double star for binoculars. It's closer and tougher, but is plainly resolved in a telescope.

And Delta Lyrae, upper left of Zeta by a similar distance, is a much wider and easier binocular pair. Its stars are pale orange and blue. It's set in a sparse grouping of a few fainter stars known as Stephenson 1.

MONDAY, NOVEMBER 16

■ The annual Leonid meteor shower should peak late tonight and late tomorrow night. The first-quarter Moon sets around 11 tonight, and midnight tomorrow night, leaving the early-morning Leonid-watching sky free of moonlight. The shower, however, has been weak for the last two decades. Expect maybe 10 visible per hour even under excellent conditions.

TUESDAY, NOVEMBER 17

■ First-quarter Moon (exact at 6:48 a.m. EST, 3:48 a.m. PST, on this date). You'll find the Moon hanging due south as twilight ends.

Look for Fomalhaut about two fists down from the Moon and one fist left in early evening. Spot Altair about four fists right or upper right of the Moon. Saturn glows about equally far left of the Moon. You can do this from inside a large city! All three points shine at between magnitude 0.6 and 1.2.

WEDNESDAY, NOVEMBER 18

■ This evening the Moon, a day and a half past first quarter, floats on an enormously wide bowl asterism: Saturn two fists to the Moon's left (magnitude o.6), Beta Ceti not quite two fists to Saturn's lower right (mag. 2.0), Fomalhaut two fists below the Moon (mag. 1.2), and fainter Delta Capricorni (2.8) forming a roughly equilateral triangle with the Moon and Fomalhaut, to their right.

The Moon Bowl, which hosts the Moon near its middle once a month, is temporary. In future years Saturn will wander away to the east.

THURSDAY, NOVEMBER 19

■ The Moon and Saturn now shine about a fist apart high in the evening, awaiting your telescope.

■ We're about two thirds of the way through fall, so Capella shines well up in the northeast as soon as the stars come out. Look to Capella's right by about three fists for the frosty Pleiades. With good (or well-corrected) distance vision, most people can count six Pleiads. With excellent vision some people can make out eight or nine. Binoculars easily show many more.

FRIDAY, NOVEMBER 20

■ The Moon and Saturn shine half as far apart this evening as they did yesterday evening (for the Americas). Try to picture that Saturn is 3,500 times farther away and 35 times wider in diameter than the Moon, not even counting Saturn's rings.

If the Moon were a bright marble just six feet in front of you, Saturn would be a large, sunlit beach ball four miles in the distance.

■ Orion clears the east horizon by about 8 p.m. now.

Orion's tilt while rising depends on your latitude. If you live north of 33° N (Los Angeles, Atlanta, the Nile delta, New Delhi, Shanghai), bright Betelgeuse in Orion's shoulder will be a little higher than bright Rigel marking his leading foot. If you're south of 33°, Rigel will be the higher of the two just after they rise; Orion will kick his brightest foot higher than his brightest shoulder.

As the night goes on, however, Betelgeuse always gains the upper position as seen from anywhere in the Northern Hemisphere.

■ In early dawn this week, toward the east-southeast, keep watch on the two hot inner planets plus Spica as shown below. They're all slowly coming apart now.

Mercury is currently as high as it will get for this apparition (for those of us at the world's mid-northern latitudes).

SATURDAY, NOVEMBER 21

■ Around 8 p.m., very high toward the south, the Great Square of Pegasus rests in its level position like a box. (It's straight overhead if you're as far south as Miami.) The Square's right (western) edge points about four fists far down toward Fomalhaut. Its eastern edge points down past Saturn toward Beta Ceti, less directly and less far.

Now descending farther: If you have a good view down to the south horizon — and if you're not much farther north than roughly New York, Denver, or Madrid — picture an equilateral triangle with Fomalhaut and Beta Ceti as its top two corners. Note where the third corner would be. Just a bit right of that point is Alpha Phoenicis or Ankaa, the brightest star of the southern constellation Phoenix. It's magnitude 2.4, not very bright but the brightest thing in its area. It has a yellow-orange tint; check with binoculars. You can now add Phoenix, or at least a bit of it, to your constellation life list.

SUNDAY, NOVEMBER 22

■ The early-morning trio of Jupiter, Mars, and Regulus has been grouping more together, as shown below. They now fit within a 3.6° circle. Watch their triangle morph rapidly from morning to morning as Mars speeds east.

This Week's Planet Roundup

Mercury is having a fine apparition low in the dawn, and brilliant Venus upper right of it helps show the way.

You can't miss Venus blazing in the east-southeast before and during early dawn. At the first sign of dawn (about 90 minutes before sunrise) watch for Mercury to peek above the horizon to Venus's lower left. They're a little more than fist at arm's length apart. By mid-dawn the two display themselves impressively as the inner solar system's hot couple.

Mercury brightens day by day. On the morning of November 14th it's magnitude +0.2. By a week later on the 21st it grows to magnitude –0.5, twice as bright. A telescope will show that Mercury's tiny disk (7 arcseconds tall) is about half sunlit, if the seeing so low isn't too bad.

Venus, meanwhile, holds steady at an impressive magnitude –4.6. A telescope or even good, steadily braced binoculars will show it to be a crescent 48 arcseconds from cusp to cusp. And if you have very sharp eyes, maybe 20/15 or better, can you detect Venus's crescent shape with no optical aid at all? One who did was apparently Edgar Allan Poe, an amateur astronomer since boyhood.1

Meanwhile Spica, magnitude +1.0, remains less than 2° from Venus all week.

Mars and Jupiter (magnitudes +0.7 and –2.1, respectively) rise together in the east-northeast around 11 p.m. Watch the horizon for them to come up about three fists below Castor and Pollux. Rising below the planets about 25 minutes later will be twinkly Regulus, magnitude +1.4, the leading light of Leo.

By the beginning of dawn they'll all be very high in the southeast. In a telescope, Mars is still just a very small 7 arcseconds wide. Jupiter is 36 arcseconds wide, small for Jupiter.

Saturn (magnitude +0.6, in Cetus) shines below or lower left the dimmer Great Square of Pegasus. As twilight fades, Saturn is the first point of light that comes into view high toward the southeast. Don't confuse it with fainter, twinklier Fomalhaut, magnitude +1.2, almost four fists down to Saturn's lower right.

Saturn after dark is nicely high in good atmospheric seeing. It transits the meridian (is highest in the south) around 9 p.m.

And a telescope will usually show at least one, or two or three or four, of Saturn's many moons. Use our Saturn's Moons Tool to identify them, or to determine what spots in Saturn's vicinity to examine for them, at any date and time (UT).

Uranus (magnitude 5.6, in Taurus) hides between the Pleiades and Aldebaran, which are well up in the east by mid-evening above rising Orion. Uranus is fairly plain to see in most binoculars. . . if you have a finder chart adequate to identify it among stars of similar brightnesses. Use the chart in the November Sky & Telescope, page 49. It shows Uranus's looping path southeast of the Pleiades for the rest of this observing season.

And with a 10- or 12-inch telescope at very high power, you can try for at least the two brightest moons of Uranus, Titania and Oberon, with our Moons of Uranus tool. They're about magnitude 13.5 and 13.7, respectively. Good luck.

Neptune (magnitude 7.8, near the Circlet of Pisces) hides 7° west of Saturn. You'll need an even more detailed finder chart to tell it from surrounding stars.

And with at least a 10 - to 12-inch telescope you can try to add Triton, Neptune's one big moon, to your life list. Use our Triton Tracker tool. Triton is magnitude 13.6. I've seen it fairly readily (and stars to magnitude 14.6 with much difficulty) with my 12.5-inch reflector at 450x or 800x even through my not-so-good suburban sky. See "Spotting Triton" by Michael Bakich in the October Sky & Telescope, page 56.

 

1. A Naked-Eye Venus Challenge this Week! Some people can resolve the crescent of Venus with their unaided eyes when it's as large and thin as it is now. Mere 20/20 vision probably isn't good enough; success may require 20/15, 20/12, 0r (very rare) 20/10 vision. Try early in dawn, after the sky has brightened somewhat and Venus is no longer too glary. Look long and carefully and please report your results to Sky & Telescope's Bob King, [email protected].

You may improve your chances by sighting through a clean, round hole in a thick piece of paper about 1 mm, 2mm, or 3mm in diameter held right next to your eye (try them all). This will mask out optical aberrations that are common away from the center of your eye's cornea and lens. Try each eye.

One person who apparently succeeded was Edgar Allan Poe. An amateur astronomer since boyhood, he used a naked-eye sighting of Venus's crescent as the central event in his poem "Ulalume" (1847) near the end of his life. Before dawn, a bereaved lover roams a misty October woodland accompanied by "Psyche, my soul." Ahead of them low in the east, where the constellation Leo ascends before dawn in mid-autumn, they witness the new-risen Venus, star of romantic love in Roman mythology, coming "up through the lair of the Lion." Poe refers to the planet as Astarte — the wilder, more wanton Greek version of the Romans' Venus goddess:

And now, as the night was senescent,
And star-dials pointed to morn —
As the star-dials hinted of morn —
At the end of our path a liquescent
And nebulous lustre was born,
Out of which a miraculous crescent
Arose with a duplicate horn—
Astarte's bediamonded crescent
Distinct with its duplicate horn.

Poe contrasts its passionate brilliance to cooler, more composed Dian, the horned crescent Moon, and urges Psyche forward:

Let us on by this tremulous light!
Let us bathe in this crystalline light!

But Psyche, who knows better, is terrified, and this being Poe, it doesn't end well.

Poe wrote "Ulalume" in the fall of 1847. On November 4, 1847, a crescent Venus and crescent Moon ("Dian") indeed hung near each other before dawn low in the east under Leo — in Poe's "lair of the Lion," the sky area from which the Leo figure stalks upward.

Astarte's bediamonded crescent was there a couple weeks earlier as well, during the mid- and late "lonesome October" of Poe's most immemorial year (his beloved wife had died). Venus was then a larger, thinner, more easily resolvable crescent, though Dian at that time was away.

 

All descriptions that relate to your horizon — including the words up, down, right, and left — are written for the world's mid-northern latitudes. Descriptions and graphics that also depend on longitude (mainly Moon positions) are for North America. Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) is Universal Time minus 4 hours. Eastern Standard Time (EST) is UT minus 5 hours. UT is also known as UTC, GMT, or Z time.

Want to become a better astronomer? Learn your way around the constellations. They're the key to locating everything fainter and deeper to hunt with binoculars or a telescope.

This is an outdoor nature hobby. For a more detailed constellation guide covering the whole evening sky, use the big monthly map in the center of each issue of Sky & Telescope, the essential magazine of astronomy.

For the attitude every amateur astronomer needs, read Jennifer Willis's Modest Expectations Give Rise to Delight.

Once you get a telescope, to put it to good use you'll want a much more detailed, large-scale sky atlas (set of charts). The basic standard is the Pocket Sky Atlas, in either the original or Jumbo Edition. Both show all 30,000 stars to magnitude 7.6, and 1,500 deep-sky targets — star clusters, nebulae, and galaxies — to search out among them.

Next up is the larger and deeper Sky Atlas 2000.0, plotting stars to magnitude 8.5; nearly three times as many, as well as many more deep-sky objects. It's currently out of print, but maybe you can find one used.

The next up, once you know your way around well, are the even larger Interstellarum Deep-Sky Atlas (with 201,000+ stars to magnitude 9.5 and 14,000 deep-sky objects selected to be detectable by eye in very large amateur telescopes), and Uranometria 2000.0 (332,000 stars to mag 9.75, and 10,300 deep-sky objects).

For the necessary tricks, read How to Use a Star Chart with a Telescope. It applies just as much to electronic charts on your phone or tablet — which many observers find handier and more versatile, if sometimes less well designed and contextualized.

You'll also want a good deep-sky guidebook. A beloved old classic is the three-volume Burnham's Celestial Handbook. It was my bedside reading for years. An impressive more modern one is the big Night Sky Observer's Guide set (2+ volumes) by Kepple and Sanner. The pinnacle for total astro-geeks is the new Annals of the Deep Sky series, currently at 13 volumes as it works its way forward through the constellations alphabetically. So far it's up to N and O.

Can computerized telescopes replace charts? Well, I used to say this:

"Not for beginners, I don't think, unless you prefer spending your time getting finicky technology to work rather than learning how to explore through the sky yourself. As Terence Dickinson and Alan Dyer say in their Backyard Astronomer's Guide, 'A full appreciation of the universe cannot come without developing the skills to find things in the sky and understanding how the sky works. This knowledge comes only by spending time under the stars with star maps in hand and a curious mind.' Without these, 'the sky never becomes a friendly place.' "

Well, that was then. The technology has continued to improve and become more user-friendly — particularly with "plate solving" software that can now recognize any star field to determine exactly where the telescope is pointed — finally bypassing all aiming imperfections in the mount, tripod, gears, bearings and other mechanics, or in the user's skill in setting up.

The latest revolution is the rise of small, photography-only "smartscopes." These take advantage of not only today's pointing technology, but also the vastly better capabilities of imaging chips and image processing compared to the human retina and the human brain's visual cortex. The most sophisticated image stacking and processing can come built right in. The result is decent deep-sky imaging from shockingly small units, relatively low priced. The image may be viewable on your phone or computer as it builds up in real time. Some can directly enable contributions to citizen-science projects.

Smartscopes are changing the hobby at the entry level. For more on this see Richard Wright's "The Rise of the Smart Telescopes" in the November 2025 Sky & Telescope. Read the magazine's review of this especially small one.

If you get a larger, more conventional computerized scope that you can look through, make sure that its drives can be disengaged so you can swing it around and point it readily by hand when you want to, rather than only slowly by the electric motors (which eat batteries).

About Alan MacRobert

Alan M. MacRobert became an avid Sky & Telescope subscriber in 1966 at age 14, joined the editorial staff in 1982, and is now a senior contributing editor, semi-retired.
