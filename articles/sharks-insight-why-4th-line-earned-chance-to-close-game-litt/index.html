Sharks Insight: Why 4th Line “Earned” Chance To Close Game, Little Things Smith & Wennberg Did in Big Win | San Jose Hockey Now

TheTools · 23.11.2026 01:16 · source · permalink

A hockey cliché is when a coach talks about a player doing the “little things”. What does that mean?

The San Jose Sharks did the little things in a 4-3 win over the Seattle Kraken on Saturday night. These are the things that they failed to do consistently in a listless 5-2 loss to the Calgary Flames in their previous game.

These are the little things that will lead to more big victories this season, if the Sharks can commit to doing them every night.

Here are some that I noticed last night.

Will Smith

When Will Smith is asked to be more physical, it’s not telling him to transform his game and blow up opposing defensemen a la Kiefer Sherwood. But it is telling him to change what might be his natural instincts, and initiate contact more, which maybe he didn’t have to do as much at USNTDP or Boston College.

This Nolan Allan (5) goal doesn’t happen if Smith (2) doesn’t initiate contact with Vince Dunn (29) off the draw, turning a Seattle puck into a 50-50. Ivar Stenberg (41) pounces on it and delivers to Allan.

Smith doesn’t get an assist, but that’s absolutely winning hockey that he played there. Do a lot more of this, mix in a brilliant game that only the uber-skilled forward can pull off here and there, and Smith is going to a $15 million dollar player sooner than later.

Alex Wennberg

Alex Wennberg is no stranger to doing the little things, which is a big reason why he’s a San Jose Sharks coaching staff favorite.

A couple things stood out from Wennberg last night.

Smith and Wennberg (21) don’t get assists on this highlight-reel Macklin Celebrini (71) goal, but their work is essential to the goal. Smith chases down the loose puck and doesn’t give in to two Kraken penalty killers. Wennberg helps dig it out to Dmitry Orlov (9).

“It’s great battle wins from Stenny, Wenny, and Smitty there. Puck came to me, so I have a time to make a couple steps and look where Mack is staying and where’s the other [Kraken] forward,” Orlov said.

Orlov gets the puck with plenty of time and space to judge where Ryan Winterton (26) is, and he completes an underrated pass into Celebrini’s wheelhouse.

“You make a hard, but not too hard flat pass,” Orlov said.

Orlov and Celebrini’s skill is set up by the little things.

“Wennberg does a really good job of getting in there, putting their guy up against the wall,” Warsofsky said. “Those little details, those things that we’ve talked about, the fundamentals of winning battles, not just hey go win a battle. There’s fundamentals to get in there and use your body and leverage. And I thought our guys did a good job, way better job of that tonight.”

Wennberg also did this well: He led the San Jose Sharks with three Rebound Shots, per Stathletes, an area where the team has really struggled this season. A focus of the Sharks’ recent hard practices was getting inside offensively more, and Wennberg did just that.

Barclay Goodrow-Zack Ostapchuk-Brett Leason

Warsofsky leaning so heavily on his fourth line to close the game was a surprise: They were sent out for three shifts with a one-goal lead in the last 3:18, with Celebrini replacing Brett Leason for the very last shift of the game with 5.9 seconds left.

It was a surprise because the fourth line’s struggles, mainly center Zack Ostapchuk and winger Barclay Goodrow, have been well-documented this season.

But Leason, making his San Jose Sharks debut, added a spark to the line. And 23-year-old Ostapchuk, who’s had an up-and-down campaign, found the form that made him look like a big part of the franchise’s future last year.

“That was the Zack Ostapchuk from last year,” Warsofsky said.

Let’s roll the film on Ostapchuk and Goodrow first, before we get to Leason.

Ostapchuk (63) is getting better and better on the draw, and won 3-of-5 in the third period, including this one to help seal the win. Those five faceoffs were second to only Celebrini’s six in the final frame.

You have to love Ostapchuk’s extra effort to win the faceoff, and Goodrow (23) not letting up and intercepting Dunn’s high-to-low pass to Matty Beniers (10), snuffing out a last-second desperation bid.

As for Leason?

“I thought [Leason] was really good, smart. Makes a really good read in the third period that us coaches really appreciate,” Warsofsky said. “The average fan may not see it but just works to get above and takes away time and space. So thought he came in and gave us some good legs.”

Maybe this was the play? Watch Leason (17) closely.

Leason deflects the Brandon Montour (62) shot away from Alex Nedeljkovic. As the San Jose Sharks are trying to break out, Leason hustles to make himself available as a center lane option for a Goodrow exit pass, and Leason gets it in deep. Leason is also F1 on the forecheck, getting to the puck before Montour and Winterton, to prevent a clean Kraken breakout. Then, he and Ostapchuk hustle back to be F3 high defensively, and force a Seattle hand pass, getting a whistle in the NZ.

That looked like “mature” hockey, in the eyes of Warsofsky.

“Making them come 200 feet, not giving them chances, not handing them chances. There’s a lot of different situations. There’s puck play through the neutral zone. There’s, you know you’re late in your shift and [don’t] try to make the extra play. That’s putting it behind their defense to make them turn,” Warsofsky said. “That’s just a simple brand of hockey. That’s winning hockey that every coach talks about and just having understanding of playing on the defensive side of things and you’ll get offense from that and you just got to believe in that.”

These are the definition of little things, and Leason’s work helped turn a Sharks’ DZ faceoff loss into an NZ faceoff, a territory win for the fourth line.

“They earned those minutes,” Warsofsky said of using his much-maligned line to close out the game. “They were playing really good hockey. They were forechecking. They were skating. They had great detail in their game.”

This is good news for the San Jose Sharks: While I don’t expect the fourth line to close every game and veteran Goodrow to turn back the clock all the way to his glory days, it’s great that the fourth line, after their struggles this season, showed some real signs of life.

A deeper, more detailed Sharks’ line-up is a good thing, even if it means some fan favorites like Collin Graf or Michael Kesselring or whomever sit from time to time.
