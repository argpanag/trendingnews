A legendary lust for women and booze: James Hunt’s world title 50 years on

TheTools · 24.10.2026 14:23 · source · permalink

Formula One changed forever after the hellraising but hugely talented driver’s triumph over Niki Lauda in Japan

Exactly 50 years ago today – October 24, 1976 – thousands of Britons woke up and did something highly unusual for that era.

They flicked on BBC Radio 2 to listen to live, uninterrupted coverage of the denouement of the Formula One world championship from a rain-soaked Fuji, Japan. James Hunt was about to be crowned world champion after a season of relentless drama, and Formula One was about to change forever.

The 1976 drivers’ championship remains arguably the most famous in F1 history. It has been immortalised in film (Ron Howard’s Rush in 2013), had books written about it, and created legends. Not least that of Hunt.

The Englishman, with his blond tousled hair, clipped public-school accent and legendary thirst for women and booze, captured the public’s imagination in a way no driver has before or since.

As Murray Walker, who was later to share a commentary booth with Hunt, once remarked: “For me, he was the most charismatic personality that’s ever been in Formula One.”

The ultimate maverick finds a kindred spirit

A relative latecomer to the sport, Hunt began his route into F1 at the age of 18, racing a restored Mini Cooper in club races.

In a tribute to their former driver a few years ago, McLaren described Hunt’s success in building that original car as “significant” considering he “barely knew a spanner from a sprocket”. But he could certainly drive.

By 1973, Hunt had made it on to the F1 grid with the privateer Hesketh team, owned by the young Lord Alexander Hesketh, who famously described F1 as like a “bottle of flat champagne in need of a vigorous shake”. It was a match made in heaven for a non-conformist like Hunt.

Hesketh would turn up to races in Rolls-Royces and helicopters, stay in five-star hotels, while the team’s pit-lane catering consisted of champagne, lobster and caviar. But while they were mocked as public-school amateurs by traditional teams, they proved to be highly competent on the track, with Hunt seriously quick when the mood grabbed him.

In a way Hunt’s enfant terrible reputation, which he more than lived up to, masked the fact that he was so good and so brave behind the wheel.

Hunt is best remembered these days for his smoking, his boozing and his “Sex: Breakfast of Champions” patch stitched into his overalls.

He is remembered for winning a dramatic British Grand Prix at Brands Hatch in 1976 (he was later disqualified) after the crowd turned mutinous, chanting “We want Hunt, We want Hunt” when stewards threatened not to let him compete following an opening-lap shunt which was not his fault.

He is remembered, too, for his pithy quote after that race, when asked what the win meant to him – “Nine points, $20,000 and a lot of happiness” – while cadging a cigarette off someone in the crowd.

He is remembered for turning up to official functions barefoot, or wearing ripped jeans and a T-shirt. And he is especially remembered for (allegedly) bedding 33 British Airways air stewardesses in a Tokyo hotel, where he was staying with his friend, the motorcycle world champion Barry Sheene, in the two weeks leading up to his 1976 title showdown with Niki Lauda.

But Hunt was a serious driver, too. Statistics can always be shaped to suit any narrative but a 2014 statistical analysis by the mathematician Dr Andrew Phillips ranked Hunt as the sixth greatest F1 driver of all time, behind Jim Clark, Jackie Stewart, Fernando Alonso, Michael Schumacher and Juan Manuel Fangio, while also ranking Hunt’s rookie 1973 season with Hesketh as the best of all time.

That might be stretching things in most F1 fans’ minds, but McLaren team principal Teddy Mayer was certainly in no doubt about his man’s talents behind the wheel. “In a straight fight I think James was more than Niki’s equal,” he once said.

Lauda, too, was impressed by his rival. Despite losing out to him in 1976, they were good friends. The Austrian particularly respected Hunt’s fairness on the track. “He was very good, correct… but f---ing quick. This was the problem.”

In a biography of Hunt by the journalist Maurice Hamilton, Lauda added: “James had this air of confidence and you couldn’t help but like him. That’s why I will always think of him as an open, honest-to-God pal. He was my kind of guy. We would become cast as rivals in 1976, but we would always be good friends.”

Commentary double-act and battling demons

Hunt’s confidence and swagger were in many ways a front. He would regularly be sick in a bucket not long before race starts because of nerves and anxiety. More fundamentally, he found fame difficult to cope with. He much preferred to be out walking Oscar, his beloved Alsatian, on the beach near his bolthole in Spain, than being a celebrity.

Hunt had his demons. Drink being the obvious one. Walker recalled when they were first paired together in commentary, at the 1980 Monaco Grand Prix, Hunt turned up in tattered jeans and a scruffy T-shirt, no shoes, his right leg in a plaster cast, with a half-finished bottle of rosé in his hand, and proceeded to put his plaster cast in Walker’s lap.

“To my horror, when his bottle of rosé ran out, a BBC bloke appeared and gives him another one,” Walker recalled later.

But he eventually came to love and respect his co-commentator, the pair of them forming one of the great double-acts, Walker with his excitable patter punctuated by Hunt’s piercing observations and insights. “James and I were about as different as it’s possible for two people to be. He could be very arrogant, he could be extremely objectionable. But I wouldn’t be saying all these less attractive things about James if also, James had not been immensely charismatic, enormous fun when he wanted to be, and an altogether really decent chap.”

Lauda, meanwhile, would later help Hunt after he had lost all of his money in poor investments post-racing and was drinking too much. The Austrian confronted his old rival at a lunch on the King’s Road (to which Hunt turned up dishevelled on a bicycle with flat tyres), gave him a loan and told him to sort himself out. “But do something,” Lauda recalled urging him. “Don’t drink. I give you the money, but come back. You have to come back. Then he was clean like you wouldn’t believe. He worked for the last three years of his life for the BBC, commentating the races, fit like you wouldn’t believe.”

Sadly, Hunt was to die of a heart attack in June 1993. He was 45.

‘The worst conditions I’d ever seen at the start of a motor race’

But back to October 24, 1976 and the culmination of one of the great Formula One seasons. Lauda had won four of the opening six rounds, and finished second in the other two, to establish a commanding lead. Hunt, meanwhile, won in Spain, but was later disqualified for being 1.8 centimetres too wide, only to have the win later reinstated on appeal.

Hunt’s aforementioned “win” at Brands Hatch – where the crowd, according to Hunt, “went completely hooligan” after it looked like he might not be allowed back into the race – looked like being another key turning point, only for the Briton to be disqualified two months later following an appeal by Ferrari.

The season hinged on Lauda’s horrific accident at the Nürburgring, after which the Austrian was given the last rites. By the time he made it back six weeks later, Hunt had closed to within two points.

It stayed nip and tuck until the final race in Fuji, with Hunt beginning the day three points behind his rival. A two-hour delay caused by a monsoon had pushed back the start time from the scheduled 5am, meaning more listeners waking up in Britain were able to tune in.

Just having live commentary was quite a thing in itself. Television exposure in those days was limited to the British and the Monaco grands prix, but such was the interest in that year’s title race, the BBC felt it needed to step up its efforts, flying out Simon Taylor, the former editor of Autosport, at the 11th hour to helm the radio coverage.

“The BBC didn’t really understand motor sport but they suddenly realised this was a major story,” Taylor recalled later. “The [delay] brought us into conflict with a religious programme at that hour – but the live motor racing coverage went ahead.”

It proved to be a turning point in F1 broadcasting. British fans listened intently as Lauda pulled out at the end of the second lap, deeming the conditions “lunacy” to drive in. Hunt, too, had queried whether to start the race, declaring he “would rather give Niki the title than race in these conditions”, while race winner Mario Andretti described them as “the worst conditions I’d ever seen at the start of a motor race.”

Hunt eventually won the title by a solitary point after battling back to third following a late pit stop, although for some minutes he would not allow himself to believe he had done it. “I was absolutely determined not to think I was world champion and then get disappointed,” he admitted. “After all the protests, disqualifications and bull---- that had gone on during the season, anything might still happen.”

He later softened and was generous with his praise for his rival. “Quite honestly, I wanted to win the championship and I felt I deserved it,” he remarked. “But I also felt Niki deserved to win the championship, and I just wish we could have shared it.”

Hunt, then aged 29, proceeded to drink the plane dry and returned to a hero’s welcome at Heathrow. And the BBC, realising it was sitting on a ratings monster, sent Taylor to all of the European races. Fifty years on, F1 enjoys wall-to-wall coverage on television and radio, but there will only ever be one James Hunt.
