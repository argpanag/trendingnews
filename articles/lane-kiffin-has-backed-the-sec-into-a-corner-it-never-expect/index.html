Lane Kiffin has backed the SEC into a corner it never expected

TheTools · 08.09.2026 17:31 · source · permalink

Lane Kiffin has spent most of his coaching career testing how far college football's rules can bend before somebody finally says enough. His first season at LSU may have produced the ultimate answer, with the SEC now considering whether one of its founding members should remain in the conference.

This started with LSU's pursuit of former NFL players Dae'Quan Wright and Zxavian Harris. Both previously played for Kiffin at Ole Miss before spending time with NFL teams, creating a fight over whether players who entered the professional ranks should be allowed back into college football.

LSU found some help in Louisiana.

A judge granted a preliminary injunction clearing a path for the players to join LSU's roster despite the SEC's professionalism rules. The conference responded through the federal courts, arguing that it retains the right to enforce its own membership standards regardless of the eligibility fight happening elsewhere.

LSU ultimately didn't put Wright or Harris on the roster for its season opener against Clemson.

That didn't end anything.

Instead, the SEC escalated the fight Tuesday by amending its federal lawsuit. Conference presidents are scheduled to meet Thursday to decide whether LSU's membership should be terminated. The SEC Constitution and Bylaws require a two thirds majority to remove a member.

Think about how quickly this got ridiculous.

A dispute over whether two former professional players can return to college has reached the point where the SEC could vote on booting out a school that helped establish the conference in 1933.

Kiffin found one heck of a loophole.

The SEC's problem extends well beyond LSU.

If the conference backs away now, every member school will know its rules can potentially be challenged through another state's court system. What happens the next time Alabama, Georgia, Texas or another program finds a competitive advantage that conflicts with conference policy?

Enforcement becomes considerably more difficult when the response is simply to find a judge who disagrees.

Going the other direction creates an even bigger mess.

Actually removing LSU would mean sacrificing one of the conference's biggest brands to prove a point about institutional authority. And LSU didn't even use the disputed players against Clemson. The Tigers kept them off the roster while the legal fight continued.

That is the corner Kiffin has helped create.

The SEC can soften its position and risk looking powerless, or it can demonstrate its authority through an action so extreme that almost nobody would have considered it realistic a few weeks ago.

Kiffin doesn't need to win the legal fight to have changed the conversation.

The SEC is already arguing in federal court for its right to remove LSU. Its university presidents are preparing to discuss whether they should actually do it.

College football has watched Kiffin push boundaries for years.

This time, he may have pushed hard enough to discover where the SEC's boundary actually is.
