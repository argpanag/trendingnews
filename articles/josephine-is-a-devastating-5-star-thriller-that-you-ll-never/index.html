Josephine is a devastating 5-star thriller that you'll never want to see again

TheTools · 16.10.2026 13:47 · source · permalink

You definitely need to see it once though.

How do you explain brutality to a child? That's the question posed in Josephine, the haunting new thriller that opened with a perfect rating at Sundance back in January. It's now had its UK premiere at the 70th BFI London Film Festival, ahead of its release in cinemas next month.

The movie stars Gemma Chan and Channing Tatum as Claire and Damien, a couple raising the titular character, a fun-loving eight year old played by first-time actor Mason Reeves. But an early morning run with dad Damien takes a turn when Josephine witnesses a brutal crime in Golden Gate Park, and the family struggle to navigate the aftershocks.

Inspired by an incident witnessed by writer-director Beth de Araújo as a child, Josephine is a harrowing exploration of a child losing her innocence. As Josephine acts out, confused and tormented by what she saw, Claire and Damien helplessly try to regain control. Like most parents, they mess up, make mistakes and disagree about the best way to handle the situation, unable to protect their little girl from her own mind.

It's an astounding debut performance from Reeves, who de Araújo discovered at a farmer's market. Shielded from the full script due to its heavy themes, Reeves manages to shift Josephine's face from sullen to scared and furious, as the camera locks in on her downcast gaze and the defiant set of her mouth.

We also see several scenes from Josephine's perspective, giving viewers a chance to get inside her terror in a way her parents can't. With this technique, it's easy to see where Claire and Damien go wrong, missing the signs that Josephine is still petrified after what she saw. To them, her behaviour is petulant and out of character; for the audience, we understand Josephine's childlike logic completely.

Chan and Tatum give affecting performances, their own fear palpable in every conversation. It's an almost impossible task to explain this particular brutality to a child, especially when you want to protect them from realising that the world isn't all sunshine and rainbows, but glossing over it leaves Josephine stuck at the scene of the crime.

The script expertly tackles this push-and-pull, weaving in gender dynamics and commentary on legal systems without feeling didactic or heavy-handed. In fact, Claire and Damien's arguments sometimes feel conflicting, prompting viewers to consider the contradictions and competing roles we hold as human beings; a woman is also a mother, a father can prioritise justice over everything, their own traumas and anxieties colouring their parenting.

The family all have their individual arcs, but Josephine's relationship with her father is perhaps the most interesting. Tatum is excellent as a father grappling with the reality of raising a daughter, a performance that Chan said - while at a recent Q&A - drew on his own experiences of fatherhood, while Chan's pain will be viscerally felt by any woman.

Where the movie shines is in portraying Josephine's hyper-vigilance after the incident. She sees the aggressor everywhere – often literally – hammering home the impact of this type of crime. There are moments every victim and survivor will feel deeply, worries that a child so young shouldn't have to contend with for years to come.

But don't mistake Josephine for a weepy emotional drama. This is a tense, claustrophobic thriller buoyed by an ominous soundtrack, shadowy characters at the side of the screen and hide-and-seek one-take scenes. De Araújo creates a visual tornado in one shot, with a rotating camera at the centre of a dining table. As Josephine runs around the table, characters appear in and out of frame, keeping audiences constantly on edge. It's a creeping dread – there's no need for jump scares when reality is terrifying enough.

While many movies have tried to handle themes of this particular brutality, few have done it as deftly and authentically as Josephine. De Araújo does not spoon-feed us catharsis in the film's closing moments, because that's not the way the world works. For some, a closing exchange will feel like justice enough. For others, the credits will roll on an unfinished narrative.

But this isn't a story about a case closed; Josephine is about a young girl losing her innocence but finding her resilience, and a family dealing with the unimaginable. This is no big-budget blockbuster, but it should be one of the most talked-about movies of 2026.

Josephine is released in UK and Irish cinemas on 20 November.
