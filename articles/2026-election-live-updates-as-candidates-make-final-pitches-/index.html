Appeals court halts ruling that blocked DOJ from attempting to "threaten" or "intimidate" election workers

A federal appeals court late Monday halted a lower court ruling that directed the Trump administration to stop ordering state election officials to separate out or not count the ballots of alleged non-citizens.

Last week, U.S. District Judge Trina Thompson ruled that the Justice Department's efforts to get officials in California and elsewhere to comply with its pre-Election Day demands — and its repeated threats to prosecute those who don't comply — likely violated the Voting Rights Act's prohibition on attempting to intimidate, threaten or coerce" people who help others vote. The ruling, which was set to last until Nov. 16, came as part of a lawsuit filed by several labor unions and civil rights groups.

The 9th Circuit Court of Appeals on Monday granted the Trump administration's request to stay that ruling pending appeal. In a 2-1 decision, the appellate judges wrote that the government's actions were "core Executive functions that did not actually disturb the states' regulation of their own elections."

Two George W. Bush-appointed judges signed on to the ruling. 

Obama-appointed Judge Mary Helen Murguia dissented, arguing the ruling "cannot be reconciled" with another case last week in which the court refused to order California to segregate 20,000 ballots.

"The Constitution's allocation of election procedures to the states is meaningless if Defendants can coerce states into foregoing their chosen procedures by abusing their prosecutorial powers," she wrote.

Judge rejects request from Democrats to block DOJ from ordering election workers to remove alleged noncitizens

A federal judge on Monday denied a request from several Democratic groups to bar the Justice Department from asking state and local election officials to remove alleged noncitizens from their voter rolls or segregate their ballots.

In his ruling, U.S. District Judge James Boasberg wrote that the Justice Department is "attempting to browbeat" states into removing people from voter rolls, and noted that states have raised concerns about the accuracy of the lists of alleged noncitizens assembled by the federal government. But he ultimately found that the plaintiffs did not have standing to sue.

"Even extraordinary merits questions cannot justify the extraordinary relief of a preliminary injunction or temporary restraining order if those seeking it lack standing," Boasberg wrote. He also noted that the government's "attempted interference has yet to alter a single rule or process governing the upcoming election," in part because state officials have resisted the Trump administration's efforts.

Earlier Monday, the Supreme Court rejected the Trump administration's request to force California to separate around 20,000 mail ballots from people it claims are noncitizens, after lower courts also refused to intervene. 

And in a separate lawsuit brought by labor groups, a California judge last week ordered the government to stop trying to "intimidate" and "threaten" election workers by ordering them to separate out ballots and dangling criminal charges if they don't comply.
