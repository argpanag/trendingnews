Kane Williamson on high-stakes cricket, philosophy and what comes next

TheTools · 19.11.2026 03:04 · source · permalink

RNZ / Life / People / Celebrity

Link: https://www.rnz.co.nz/life/people/celebrity%2Fkane-williamson-on-high-stakes-cricket-philosophy-and-what-comes-next

“Don’t let winning get in the way of playing,” the Kiwi cricket great tells RNZ in a wide-ranging interview.

One of the greatest batsmen of his generation, Kane Williamson enjoyed a remarkable career at the top of world cricket.

Having announced his retirement from international cricket in June, the former Black Caps captain is releasing his autobiography The Bare Margins.

He spoke to RNZ's Sunday Morning about the triumphs and disappointments that shaped him, his approach to leadership, and how an interest in Buddhism influenced his thinking on and off the field.

Kane Williamson: On His Autobiography 'The Bare Margins'

The bare margins refers to those bare margins of performance and mindset and everything you do in advance of walking out onto the field. Looking back, when were the stakes highest for you on the field?

"The 2019 World Cup final at Lord's, which ended in a tie after 100 overs and a super over, and it was decided only by a boundary.

"I mean, gosh, it was just a remarkable game of cricket that couldn't be scripted if both teams tried, it was outrageous and perfect in a lot of ways where, to have a tie and then have a super over that's another tie and then kind of expose this very odd rule that would then decide the final of the World Cup ... it was mad but so dramatic.

"I know hearing from friends, family, fans that watched it and followed that game, it was pretty riveting stuff. So, I don't think there's any bigger, more significant dramatic moments on the field than that."

Early in your career, you started keeping a small notebook to kind of ground yourself. Tell us about that.

"There wasn't a lot in the notebook, to be honest. In fact, that was kind of symbolic of its meaning, really.

"I was just sitting down and just wrote personal goals on one side and then career goals on the next page. Really, it's just a challenge to do more, but do it in a way that's authentic and natural to you. And that's what that sort of little notebook represented."

It was refusing to let a scorecard define your value as a human being. That's really interesting because, in a way, it is your take on the ephemeral criteria we apply to what we call a successful life.

"Yeah, that's basically right. You put it beautifully, really. I mean, just different moments, you know, that challenge your reasons for doing things and even just understanding what path you are on.

"Sometimes we just kind of blindly go about it and we think it needs to be a certain way. And it was just asking a whole bunch of questions of that, so then when you do walk out to bat, you can approach it with a bit of freedom and you can take your options.

"It's a natural feeling to what you're doing, and it's fascinating. It's almost a bit of a paradox because it can contradict a lot of ideas from people that look at high performance and all these things."

Kane Williamson says people should not let winning get in the way of playing.

PHOTOSPORT

Peace of mind, I think you're saying, can only be found on the quality of the preparation, not the guarantee of the outcome?

"I believe so ... sometimes we get in our own ways, and it might be the number or the striving to get it, and then it's like, well, why do you want it so bad? And what do you think it'll give you?

"Often you peel it back and realise that it won't probably give you everything you want.

"I got asked the other day by sort of a young player playing county cricket, sitting next to him, he said, I really want to average 50. I said, 'Oh, why do you want to do that?' And he kind of looked at me like sideways, thinking isn't that what we're all meant to do?

"I said just remember it probably won't give you what you think it will.

"The journey can be fun too, you know, rather than the stressful need to ... get 100.

"Don't let winning get in the way of playing. Because ultimately, playing and ... giving your attention in that moment and being really present is gonna give you the best opportunity to make really good decisions.

"Ultimately, the accumulation of those moments is what's gonna be meaningful and significant, versus this idea we need to suffocate ourselves across the line to have the W."

Brendan McCullum says in this book of yours, you had confidence but not ego when you came into the NZ side - kind of the Buddhist route. If you'd been the player always dying to average 50, would you have done as well in your career?

"It's hard to know exactly ... probably for 18 months before I retired, I'd constantly get asked about 10,000 runs and all these things, and there was a lot of noise around it.

"But I knew that if that was what motivated me, I just believed I wouldn't be anywhere near that number.

"It was quite ironic, you know, that it creeps in because everybody keeps talking about it.

"Don't get me wrong, it's an amazing achievement, but I think it needs to be achieved through other sort of motivations and focus in my personal view."

Don Bradman ended his test career with an average of just under 100. We'll remember you for your sudden exit mid-series at Lord's - only 500 runs short of 10,000. A lot of people will still be scratching their heads until they read your book about why you left like that.

"It was just important for me to make that decision based on the reasons of how I played the game and wanted to play the game.

"It's an amazing achievement and a big deal, but I've been retired five months or six months, and nobody's mentioned it.

"And that kind of speaks to what I had written on that first page, and you've mentioned it, [the] runs on your headstone thing which, yeah, they won't be there.

"But it is interesting how you can get a duck against Dale Steyn ane in a fierce South African side. And that leaves you entirely at peace.

"And we think, God, how does an ostensible failure leave you as serene as scoring a double century would?"

Kane Williamson says 2019 was painful - but they bounced back.

PHOTOSPORT

The 2019 World Cup final is one of the most agonising bare margins moments in sports history. How did it shape the way you led the Black Caps to NZ's first World Test Championship victory two years later?

"That was an experience. Maybe a scar for all of us, and we're all to sort of deal with it in their own ways.

"It was a mad time, but to fast forward and end up in that Test championship final, it is unique. There's just so many moments, uncontrollable moments, and that 2019 final was just full of them.

"Right at the end, even the rules and the deflection of the bat off Stokes, and they got the rules wrong for the boundary or the number of runs. It was just endless. And they were all at the end of the game, which made clawing it back quite challenging.

"In most games, you sort of walk off and you understand that that's how it plays out. But when it's a World Cup final, it seems to be a little bit more challenging to do.

"But to move into the Test Championship final fairly shortly after that ... the team picked themselves up and refocused because that was a part of it, your cricketing journey didn't end after that.

Were other cricketers you know playing for the enjoyment of a match irrespective of the result? How many are like you?

"It was not that you were just doing it for fun. I was very, very focused and driven in trying to get better as a player every day.

"But I just found before I started shaping the lens of how I was wanting to treat what I was doing, there was just a whole lot of stress.

"You only felt this relief in success or a good day. And I wanted there to be more. I wanted to bring those two pictures a little closer together so it wasn't just this thing that you were going out [to do] and it was just fun.

"It was just more that there was just a bit more of a sense of ease to it. There was some joy in it, joy in the development and the training and it was fun, but it wasn't just 'I'm going out and playing backyard cricket'.

"The balance had really shifted the priorities of my life that had changed quite a lot with a young family, and then I made the call."

With your family - Sarah, Maggie, Paddy and Stevie - what are you going to tell your children about following in footsteps like yours?

"Find their own interests. Paddy's very, very interested in dinosaurs, so he might pave his own way. Yeah - niche, but a real passion. He's young!

"I think it'd be great if they participated in sports for all the benefit that it brings, but they're gonna have their own interests and own ideas and that's cool.

HarperCollins

Does it feel strange to you that you're now outside the sporting world – and an author on a book tour?

"Yeah, it does. And do you know what? I much prefer it.

"The whole time when you're part of a team and you're doing a whole lot of media, you're so conscious of representing the side and the game that's played.

"Let's say you're in a press conference or you kind of know what's going to come and you know where the prods will be again, it's a game within a game really.

"You're quite guarded ... it's nice to not feel like that. You could kind of be yourself, which is nice."

What's next for you?

"I'm not 100 percent sure just yet, I'm going to do a couple of coaching opportunities, playing a little bit of golf, which I'm really enjoying.

"I didn't really play throughout my cricketing career, so I've just picked it up really, but yep, got the bug.

One last question - we once used to honour players past and present, and now it's a money-generating big industry, as with the All Blacks, and once you're gone, you're gone. Is the game still as special, and do we play it too much?

"I mean, I can speak for the players in the New Zealand team, and it definitely is, and especially Test cricket.

"There's a real soul to it, and we're probably one of the few smaller nations to hold on to that.

"It's tricky because it's not necessarily the thing that's supported [them] financially for the players, as compared to some of the bigger nations in the franchise competitions that exist. There is a real love for that format, but it's challenged hugely.

"We're sort of at a stage now where there is a lot of cricket and there's a land grab, really, between all these franchise competitions and how it kind of coexists with the international game, where we've never had that.

"Everybody's trying to get there a bit and it's tough, and it's going to have to land somewhere."

The Bare Margins: An Autobiography, by Kane Williamson, is out now.
