Japan nonlife agent fraud spreads, putting insurer duty in focus

TheTools · 17.11.2026 22:54 · source · permalink

Japan nonlife insurance agent fraud at a custom homebuilder has spread well beyond the single branch the company disclosed – and three major Tokyo-headquartered carriers are now demanding an independent review.

Tama Home Co. initially said the misconduct was limited to its branch in Takamatsu, Kagawa Prefecture, in western Japan. Nonlife insurers conducting their own review found otherwise. A Tama Home official, cited by Jiji Press in its November 12, 2026, report, confirmed that an internal investigation is under way across all 236 of the company’s branches, led by headquarters. Jiji Press reported the alleged fraud spans several hundred cases, with losses estimated in the tens of millions of yen – the company has not independently confirmed those figures in its public statements. Some claims also involved inflated amounts.

On November 14, 2026, Tama Home published a formal IR statement titled “Our Response to Certain Media Reports Regarding Fire Insurance Payouts,” apologising to customers, shareholders, and investors, announcing a nationwide investigation, and confirming it had proposed closing its insurance agency business to Tokio Marine & Nichido Fire Insurance Co., Sompo Japan Insurance Inc., and Mitsui Sumitomo Insurance Co., all Tokyo-headquartered. All three had already begun moving to end insurance sales through Tama Home before the announcement.

For brokers placing business into Japan or assessing Japanese carriers as counterparties, the case raises a direct question: when a licensed corporate agent also performs the work the policy covers, what oversight are the sponsoring nonlife insurers actually required to provide – and are they meeting it?

The fraud involved submitting photographs from past legitimate claims as documentary evidence for new, separate payouts. That technique exploits a verification gap at the claims stage that becomes structurally harder to close when the same entity manages both the physical work and the insurance placement.

Tama Home’s dual role sits at the centre of the case. Under Japan’s Insurance Business Act, non-insurance companies can register as agents and distribute cover on behalf of clients. Tama Home did exactly that, acting as an intermediary for fire and other policies while also carrying out the renovation work those policies were written to cover. That arrangement gave employees simultaneous access to both the claims documentation and the physical sites.

The nonlife insurers named in Jiji Press’s reporting have called for an independent, third-party body to conduct its own review. Their position is that an internal probe led by the same headquarters overseeing the implicated branches is not sufficient – a view that also implies the insurers have doubts about whether the company’s self-reporting can be relied upon.

Coverage of how Japan’s nonlife misconduct cases are reshaping the FSA’s supervisory agenda for the region provides further context on the reform trajectory now framing this case.

Bigmotor Co., a used car dealer, lost its insurance agent registration in late 2023 after the Financial Services Agency (FSA) found that employees had deliberately damaged customers’ vehicles to generate inflated repair claims. Bigmotor, like Tama Home, operated simultaneously as a service provider and a licensed insurance agent. The FSA subsequently issued business improvement orders against nonlife insurers that had continued routing business through Bigmotor despite evidence of fraud.

Those events drew direct comment from Katayama Satsuki, Japan’s Minister of Finance and Minister of State for Financial Services, at a press conference in Tokyo on July 3, 2026 – four months before the Tama Home disclosures but in remarks that directly anticipated the structural vulnerability the case has since exposed. “The actual state of insurance agents was also an issue in the BIGMOTOR case,” Katayama said, in a provisional English translation published by the FSA on its official website, adding that the regulator had begun surveying the actual situation of agents that also engage in other businesses.

Reporting on how the FSA’s conduct enforcement approach is shifting accountability up the corporate chain outlines the broader direction the regulator has been signalling across multiple cases since 2023.

The Insurance Business Act was amended by Japan’s Diet in May 2025 and entered into force in June 2026, specifically to address misconduct by agents operating in corporate settings. The FSA’s Annual Report on Insurance Monitoring 2026, published on August 6, 2026, notes that the nonlife sector is expected to continue advancing reforms and that the regulator will take supervisory action as necessary.

The channel at issue is far larger than the Tama Home case alone suggests. According to L.E.K. Consulting’s analysis of Japan’s insurance distribution market, published in May 2026 and drawing on an FSA working group survey, approximately 9,530 entities are recognised as in-house agencies by Japan’s four major nonlife insurers. The agency channel accounts for approximately 90% of nonlife insurance distribution in Japan, and in-house agencies are characterised by lean staffing and a frequent reliance on externally seconded resources, both of which can hinder independent compliance management.

The General Insurance Association of Japan (GIAJ) began full operation of its Agency Business Quality Evaluation System in April 2026. On August 5, 2026, GIAJ member companies endorsed the basic principles governing agency business quality, a formal step in building out the third-party evaluation framework that covers 31 companies and assesses operations independently of the sponsoring insurer. Direct premiums written through agents accounted for approximately 89.7% of total direct premiums in Japan in fiscal 2024, according to GIAJ.

Under Japan’s regulatory framework, the sponsoring insurer bears legal responsibility for the conduct of its appointed agents. The FSA’s supervisory guidelines, revised in parallel with the amended Insurance Business Act, require insurers to implement enhanced due diligence and periodic compliance reviews across their agent networks.

The Tama Home case will determine how thoroughly those obligations were being met. Brokers and risk managers assessing Japanese nonlife carriers should note that the FSA’s regulatory framework now gives it clear authority to pursue sponsoring insurers – not just the agent – when oversight is found to have fallen short.
