Nations League could be taste of silverware that England need

“You’ve got to get them used to ­champagne,” said Brian Clough after his first cup win at Nottingham Forest. “Then they’ll realise they like it.” Given that he was talking about not the European Cup, nor the league title or even the League Cup, but the Anglo-Scottish Cup (inaugurated 1975, discontinued 1981) which Forest won in 1977, it’s unclear quite what ­vintage his players were drinking but the point was well made. Clough wanted his team to experience being winners and enjoy the taste of even cheap champagne.

Fifty-two years on from the FA ignoring Clough’s claims as England manager after he had won the league title with Derby, and 60 years and counting since an England captain last lifted a trophy at Wembley, Harry Kane held the Ballon d’Or aloft on Thursday night, acknowledging the crowd honouring his ­record-breaking 126th cap. England history is being rewritten as Kane is beginning to nudge Bobby Charlton’s ribs in the race to be the national team’s greatest ever player but, appreciative though he was of the Kane-fest on Thursday, he knows Charlton won the ultimate prize. “The crowd will be going a bit more crazy if we’re lifting Euro 2028, that’s for sure,” Kane said.

England take on Spain in the Nations League on Sunday and while that may be the Watney Cup (founded 1970 when Clough won it with Derby, discontinued 1973) of international football, England have done well enough this autumn that, whatever happens in Madrid, they will be playing in the quarter-finals in March.

Somewhere, sometime in the future, England will win an international trophy. Most likely it will be this generation, albeit not playing like they did in the 2-1 win against Croatia on Thursday night, which was a ragged, disjointed display, not necessarily the sharpener you want before ­taking on the World Cup winners, a team unbeaten in a record 43 competitive matches. England remain perhaps the best international side in the world going forward but with ­considerable work to do at the back, which is often the foundation of trophy-winning sides. Ezri Konsa is expected to return at the Metropolitano on Sunday, which should make a difference.

And while it is by no means a must-win game – England and Spain have both qualified for the last eight – an unlikely victory would enable everyone finally to move on from a curious World Cup, which was both England’s best ever performance overseas and a source of anger and frustration. Thomas Tuchel was ­baffled that the media weren’t more supportive of his achievement in the wake of the 2-1 semi-final defeat to Argentina, ­unaware that in the ranks of the stadium in Atlanta, world-wearied English scribes and fans were watching with ever-increasing ­horror as the team retreated in the face of a merely capable Argentina side. 

Writer Simon Kuper’s line about the England team’s determination to turn every World Cup contest into the Siege of Mafeking, written 20 years ago, would have been lost on Tuchel but familiar to all England watchers and students of the national psyche. 

That hurt lingered even into this new season. Last month, after beating Croatia and Czech Republic with 10 goals scored and none conceded, Tuchel was receiving some softball questions about the post-World Cup scrutiny when he returned to his treatment after the Argentina game. “Why were you vexed by the reaction?” he was asked. “Were you in the press conference before the France game?” he replied. “Then you would know why.” He still bears the scar.

It was pointed out before this match that he has likened the England reboot since the World Cup to Tuchel 2.0. “No, you called it that,” he retorted. But the smile was back and a new style is emerging. England do press in a more aggressive and strategic way than at the World Cup. “This England team has everything modern football is asking for, it’s a complete team, completely different to the World Cup,” said Croatia coach Slaven Bilić, whose team once humiliated England in one of their many nadirs back in 2007. “I like this England team. It’s really cheeky, in a positive way. You play really aggressive football, all the players can move into different positions. It’s not easy to play against [Jude] Bellingham and Kane. Now the question is: can you play like this at the highest level and biggest championships?”

Even if Tuchel will never actually say the words, “I’m sorry I messed up in Atlanta,” it’s hard to imagine he will ever again revert to a back five if a trophy tantalising comes into view. If the 7-0 win against Croatia last month felt like us all kissing and making up with Tuchel, perhaps the Argentina defeat still lingers like that ominous marital argument that will resurface the next time there is tension. But if England reign in Spain, then another layer of healing would have surely been achieved. The Nations League may be small beer in the scheme of things, our very own Watney Cup, but the England national team can’t be choosy about which cups they win. 

Best to start with cheap champagne before trying the vintage stuff.
