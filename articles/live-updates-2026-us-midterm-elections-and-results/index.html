  3. Just 65 votes separates candidates in Mississippi's senate racepublished at 22:56 GMT
    22:56 GMT

    This is where the current race in Mississippi sits right now.

    Democratic candidate Scott Colom is slightly ahead in the race to unseat incumbent Senator Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith.

    Colom, with 462,857 votes, at the time of writing, is ahead by 65 votes between him and Hyde‑Smith's 462,792 votes.

    Independent candidate Ty Pinkins has almost 20,000 votes.

    The race is too close to call after almost 96% of the votes counted.

    Mississippi Secretary of State Michael Watson says in a statement that 70 of the 82 counties in the state have uploaded their unofficial result through a state system, and notes that counties get 10 days from election day to submit their certified results to his office.

    There are some 3,600 outstanding absentee-by-mail ballots yet to be received and an unknown number of affidavit ballots to be processed in that 10-day timeframe, he says.

  5. How long until we know the results in Mississippi?published at 22:18 GMT
    22:18 GMT

    Brandon Drenon
    US reporter

    We are still waiting to hear results for the Mississippi Senate race, and it could be a while before we know the winner in the race between Democrat Scott Colom and incumbent Repulican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith.

    Mississippi does not have automatic recounts for tight races or allow candidates to request recounts.

    The state also allows mail-in ballots to be counted if they are postmarked by Election Day and received within five business days later.

    That means the race is very likely not to be called until at least 10 November, says Julie Wronski, an associate professor of political science at the University of Mississippi.

    "Because the difference between the two candidates is so narrow, really, we're going to have to wait for every single vote received in any capacity to be counted," Wronski says.

    The vote spread between the two candidates is currently less than 100 ballots, according to data from the Associated Press.

  6. Here's a look at still-outstanding racespublished at 22:05 GMT
    22:05 GMT

    After the election night surge of results, ballots are still being counted for some gubernatorial, House of Representative and Senate races.

    Here's where things stand:

    Several House races were called earlier on Wednesday, though outstanding ballots remain for some 59 uncalled races.

    In the race for Kansas governor, Democrat Cindy Holscher is leading Republican Ty Masterson by 0.51 percentage points, with about 98% votes counted. There will be recount if the margin falls below 0.5 points.

    The Mississippi Senate race remains too close to call. Democrat Scott Colom leads Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by 65 votes, with ballots still outstanding.

    With about a quarter of ballots still left to count, Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Dan Sullivan by just over 600 votes in Alaska's Senate race.

    Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads three Republicans with 49.15% of the vote for Alaska governor, with a quarter of votes still to be counted.

  8. Oil falls, markets rise on Democratic wavepublished at 21:38 GMT
    21:38 GMT

    Rachel Clun and Priya Patel
    Business reporters

    Oil prices have fallen below $100 a barrel while stock markets closed higher following the Democrats' decisive midterm elections victory.

    Global oil benchmark Brent crude fell by more than 8% to about $95 a barrel on Wednesday afternoon, its lowest level in two months, after US Vice-President JD Vance said earlier that Republicans lost the elections due to the war in Iran.

    Vance's comments prompted investors to reassess the risk of a wider Middle East conflict.

    Markets seized on Vance's call to "end the war", focus on the cost of living and rethink America's relationship with Israel, interpreting the post-election political landscape as reducing the likelihood of a significant escalation involving Iran.

    Key US stock market indexes closed higher on Wednesday, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average jumping 3%. The S&P 500 closed up 1.6%, while the tech-heavy Nasdaq rose 1.4%.

  10. Here's how leadership could shape up in the US House of Representativespublished at 20:58 GMT
    20:58 GMT

    Sakshi Venkatraman
    Reporting from Washington DC

    Come January, there will be a new majority party in Congress.

    That means new leadership in both chambers - the Senate and House of Representatives.

    Let's look at the House.

    The top lawmaker right now is Speaker of the House Mike Johnson. When Democrats take over, that role could go to current House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, the top Democrat who is currently House minority leader.

    Jeffries announced his bid for the position on Wednesday.

    In a letter to his caucus "humbly" asking for their support, Jeffries says, "We must now deliver the dramatic change the American people are demanding in the majority".

    So what will happen to Johnson?

    There were questions at first that he may become the next House Minority Leader, but he has now said he will not run for that role.

    The new name being floated in another Louisiana Republican and current House Majority Leader Steve Scalise.

    He released a five page letter today saying he will go for the job.

    "Next Congress will be difficult," he writes. "But we are not powerless."

  12. How could Trump thwart a Democratically controlled Congress?published at 20:30 GMT
    20:30 GMT

    Trump has given mixed messages about how he'll navigate leading with Democratically led Congress.

    Before polls closed on Tuesday, he said he would become "Mr Veto" if the Democrats won, and exercise his constitutional right to refuse to sign any legislation passed by the Democrats.

    But after it became clear the Republicans had lost the House of Representatives and the Senate, Trump said he would be willing to collaborate with the the rival party.

    Here's how the presidential veto works

    As chief executive, Trump can use the power of the presidential veto to stop any bills passed by Congress from becoming law.

    But if two-thirds of members agree, the veto can be overridden by further congressional vote.

    It's all designed as a part of the checks and balances that allow each branch of the US government to hold a level of control over the others, which you can read more about below.

  17. Vance's candid comments distance him from Trump - and will spark debatepublished at 19:04 GMT
    19:04 GMT

    Daniel Bush
    Washington correspondent

    It's hard to recall any recent examples of a sitting vice-president speaking so candidly about their party’s midterm election defeat - and in ways that seem to create distance from the president.

    JD Vance said an "honest conversation" about the party's future had to start with acknowledging the Iran war cost Republicans' control of Congress.

    Trump conceded the Iran war and rising gas prices played a part in the defeat, but he said he didn't regret launching the conflict.

    Vance went one step further. The VP said Republicans could debate whether the war was worth the political costs.

    He stopped short of openly saying he opposed the war, which he has long backed in public.

    News reports indicate he has expressed opposition to the war behind closed doors.

    His statement will spark a broader debate on the right about the party’s foreign policy post-Trump.
