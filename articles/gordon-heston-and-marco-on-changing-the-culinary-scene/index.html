Gordon, Heston and Marco on changing the culinary scene

TheTools · 05.11.2026 21:29 · source · permalink

Gordon Ramsay, Heston Blumenthal and Marco Pierre White have all been awarded three Michelin stars - the highest number given by the guide

  • Published
    5 November 2026, 10:19 GMT

Iconic British chefs Gordon Ramsay, Marco Pierre White and Heston Blumenthal look back at 20-hour work days, falling out and chasing Michelin stars.

Gordon, Heston and Marco.

Three chefs that have made such an impact on the culinary world that food fans recognise them by their first names alone.

Before the trio arrived on the culinary scene, no British chef had been awarded three Michelin stars, the highest number given by the guide.

Looking back in an interview with the BBC, Ramsay remembers it was close to 11pm on a Friday night when he was told he had won his third star: "It felt like we were in the middle of Wembley Stadium because I just let out this incredible roar."

But the road there wasn't easy and the workload was brutal, the three chefs explain in new BBC documentary Rise of the Rockstar Chef, which charts their climb to the top of the industry,

"It's a very different world," says White, once described as the 'enfant terrible' of fine dining. "I don't think a lot of chefs today would survive the old world."

"We're talking 20 hours a day minimum," Blumenthal adds, recalling how he'd often sleep on laundry piles in his restaurant to save on time from driving home to sleep.

"I worked with delirium," he adds, remembering a particular day when, very tired, he tried to light a blow torch by running it under the hot water tap.

While he worked long days, Ramsay says: "Still to this day, I get excited every time I walk into a restaurant"

Time isn't all the chefs gave up.

"The sacrifice to open your own restaurant was immense," Ramsay says. Both Ramsay and Blumenthal sold their homes to fund their first restaurants.

Everything was on the line and emotions were heating up too.

Blumenthal explains that White and Ramsay had an "argument" at his restaurant in Bray one Sunday two decades ago and the three hadn't all been together since.

"Last time we all met wasn't as pleasant as this time," Ramsay adds.

Now, they're reuniting in the three-part BBC documentary. "The fact that the three of us sat down together was something magical," adds Blumenthal.

In 1987, aged 25, White (centre) opened his first restaurant, Harvey's, where he hired Ramsay

White, who moved from Leeds to London to pursue a career as a chef when he was just 16, says about the intense time: "The first thing you have to learn is how to absorb pressure. If you can't absorb pressure, how can you deliver?"

"I quite like having that jeopardy because it not only just kept me hungry, but there was so much at stake," Ramsay says about putting everything on the line to open his first restaurant in Chelsea.

White and Blumenthal met while the latter was staging, a term for unpaid work in a kitchen to gain experience, for the famous French chef Raymond Blanc's restaurant, Le Manoir.

Reflecting on those early days, with a little laughter in his voice, Heston says: "We're only cooking food but we think we're the fourth emergency service."

Blumenthal says he and White became close, with the latter lending him a car and staff when setting up his first restaurant

And finally, their hard work was successful. In 1995 when White became the first British chef and youngest chef ever, at the time, to be awarded three Michelin stars.

"Winning three stars is, without question, the most exciting journey of a young chef's life," White says in the documentary.

In 2001, Ramsay became the first Scottish chef to win three Michelin stars.

"It still makes me feel incredibly emotional now and I don't talk about it that often because it's happened once and once only," the chef says.

And in 2004, Heston Blumenthal's The Fat Duck won three stars - and was voted best restaurant in the world by hospitality guide The 50 the following year.

Blumenthal says winning his third star "was a real moment but then I spent at least a year being concerned that I'm going to lose the third star".

This fear reached boiling point, Heston recalls, when he was hosting a meeting with Michelin at The Fat Duck and staff wheeled bins through the restaurant.

For White the sheen faded fast: "Winning stars is exciting. Defending them is rather boring, because it becomes systematic. Because what you're doing is you're defending your reputation."

He famously returned three stars to Michelin and announced he would retire from his work as a chef in 1999, explaining this was because he felt the world of gastronomy was changing.

However, Ramsay sees great value in having kept his stars over the past 25 years, though notes that "maintaining them is bloody hard, arduous. Time-consuming perfection like no other."

"There's a responsibility on my shoulders to create future generations of this industry," Ramsay says

Reflecting on restaurants in the UK today, Ramsay says: "I think the industry now is one of the most exciting points in culinary history, I think [it's] this lovely multicultural melting pot."

However, Blumenthal notes that "financially it's a lot harder" to keep things afloat, "you need a millionaire to support your development team, or what I did was did TV," the chef says of the cost of running your own place.

White, meanwhile, feels modern restaurants have lost their excitement: "They're quite boring. People tend to whisper. There's no show."

In his final estimation, White says that "gastronomy is the greatest form of therapy any misfit could ever be exposed to".

And despite his success, Ramsay says he's just getting started: "Talk to me about legacy in 20 years time because I am fit as a fiddle, I'm hungry for success and I am not done yet."
