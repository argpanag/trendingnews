Goldman Sachs International CEO: Governments Must Cut Spending to Curb Soaring Borrowing Costs — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 05.10.2026 18:06 · source · permalink

Goldman Sachs International co-CEO Anthony Gutman called on Monday (5th) for Western governments to respond to bond market turmoil with a more stable policy environment, arguing that the core solution lies in reducing fiscal deficits and promoting more durable economic growth. Otherwise, borrowing costs will continue to climb, further squeezing the operating space of both businesses and governments.

In an interview with CNBC's Squawk Box Europe, Gutman noted that rising government bond yields have become a shared challenge across the Western world, with recent volatility in US Treasuries and French government bonds particularly concerning. He emphasized: "We all know what's driving this. We look at energy costs, we look at labor markets. But fundamentally, what do we need to solve this? We need lower fiscal deficits and more durable economic growth."

His remarks came as yields in major global government bond markets remain at elevated levels. The US 10-year Treasury yield stood at 5.2581% on Monday, down 1 basis point, while the French 10-year government bond yield rose more than 1 basis point to 4.8812%. Notably, US Treasury yields moved higher last Friday (2nd) even as September nonfarm payrolls data came in weaker than expected, signaling that market concerns over the fiscal outlook have outweighed the short-term reaction to a cooling labor market.

Gutman told program host Steve Sedgwick that governments "always face trade-offs," but in the current fiscal environment, resolving these issues has become "more challenging." He specifically pointed to Europe's election cycle as a source of additional policy uncertainty and instability for businesses. At the same time he was speaking, Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez announced plans to hold a snap election on November 29, underscoring the rising risk that Europe's political calendar poses to economic policy.

The combination Gutman hopes to see is one of "lower spending and higher growth," which he believes would bring greater confidence to markets and businesses. His remarks reflect deepening concerns among Wall Street executives about fiscal discipline in major economies, particularly against a backdrop of persistently high interest rates and expanding government debt-servicing burdens, where the importance of fiscal consolidation and structural reform can no longer be ignored.

Market participants interpreted Gutman's comments as not directly naming specific countries, but the trajectory of US and French government bond yields already illustrates the pervasiveness of the problem. The U.S. Treasury Department has continued to expand its debt issuance in recent months, heightening concerns over supply-demand imbalances in longer-dated bonds. France, meanwhile, has seen its bond yields approach the 5% threshold at one point due to an excessive budget deficit and political gridlock, reflecting investor doubts about the fiscal sustainability of the eurozone's second-largest economy.

For investors, persistently high government borrowing costs carry multiple knock-on effects: corporate financing costs rise in tandem, equity valuations come under pressure, and currency volatility intensifies. Gutman's warning also implies that if governments fail to demonstrate fiscal discipline, long-dated yields could remain elevated due to supply-side factors even after central banks complete their rate-hiking cycles, creating a structural risk of "higher-for-longer" interest rates.

Market focus has now shifted from inflation to fiscal deficits and debt supply. The International Monetary Fund and multiple investment banks have recently called on major economies to present credible medium-term fiscal consolidation paths. Gutman's remarks represent the latest pressure from Wall Street on policymakers, and set the tone for the upcoming series of European elections and the US mid-term fiscal debate.
