‘We just want to bring Brandy back:’ Dad pushes for search for daughter’s body under Stamp Falls

TheTools · 19.09.2026 17:05 · source · permalink

Chuck Nessel was at the ferry terminal on Quadra Island, heading back to Campbell River from his cabin, when his cell service came back on and he realized his son Mitchell had been frantically calling him.

That’s when Nessel first learned that his 20-year-old daughter Brandy was missing.

It was July 5, a Sunday evening, and Brandy had just moved to Port Alberni from Campbell River a month and a half earlier with her boyfriend, Colin.

That day, the two had gone to the Stamp River for the first time, with a couple of her girlfriends.

Nessel learned that Brandy — barefoot and in a swimsuit — had been wading through the water from a shady area to get to a sunny spot on the other side of the river when she lost her footing.

She was swept away, Nessel’s son Mitchell told him, and no one could find her.

Mitchell sent his father the location where Brandy was last seen, and they agreed to meet there.

In the hours it took to take the ferry, then drive home and on to Port Alberni, Nessel had nothing but time to think, and to speculate.

His daughter was an experienced swimmer who “lived in the water” and loved the outdoors, said Nessel. He had taken her camping, hunting and fishing her whole life.

Brandy was ridiculously strong, said Nessel. But the Stamp River has sections of dangerous turbulence leading to the Stamp Falls, a series of cascading waterfalls that cumulatively drop approximately seven metres, according to a world waterfall database.

Nessel had volunteered with Campbell River’s search and rescue team for 13 years, and knew from experience that the odds of a successful water rescue were slim. “The vast majority of times, it was a recovery. That’s the way it goes,” he said. “So then automatically, my brain’s going there, and then trying not to at the same time.”

That’s when Nessel decided to call in the expertise of his close family friend, swift­water rescue expert Jamie Turko.

Turko has 30 years of experience in search and rescue and has worked as an instructor all over Canada. “I said, ‘Hey, how bad is this?’ And he said it was bad,” Nessel recalled in an interview.

He picked Turko up en route to Stamp River Provincial Park, arriving at around 11 that night.

The Alberni Valley Rescue Squad, alongside Arrowsmith Search and Rescue and the RCMP dive team, among others, had turned up within an hour and a half of Brandy’s disappearance.

They called off the search at dark, with plans to come back in the morning, so Turko and the family continued their own search into the night.

“The thing about it is, I spent so much time on the other side of the caution tape, not understanding what the family goes through on the other side,” said Nessel. “And, you know, appreciating and being caring, but at the same time not really fully comprehending.”

A helicopter canvassed the river’s length, and divers scoured the river’s bottom. But after four days, the Alberni Valley Rescue Squad announced they were pausing their efforts.

Two and a half months later, Brandy’s body still hasn’t been found.“Nobody expected this to happen. We expected to find her within a day or two. Or if she was on the bottom, she would have surfaced, as the body changes its buoyancy over time,” said Nessel.

Though he knows it doesn’t make sense, he finds it hard to shake the idea that somehow, Brandy is still out there. With a broken leg, maybe. Or with amnesia. Cold and scared, on the bank of the river, but alive.

“But as time goes on, you realize that that becomes less and less of a possibility,” he said. “It’s still in the back of my head. Because there’s no definitive proof at this point, you know? So in my head, she’s still sitting on that bank.”

After extensive land and water searches, Turko and Nessel believe they know where Brandy’s body is — and they want to get her out.

There’s a camera mounted within about four metres of the falls, and Turko said he’s seen video of Brandy being carried toward the mouth of the falls.

They’re confident she went over the falls, but no remains have been found in the calmer pools below, farther downriver, or where the river meets the ocean. The only place no one has been able to search is right at the base of the falls, Nessel said.

Turko said she’s almost certainly stuck somewhere, most likely in an undercut beneath the falls. An undercut is a section of rock where fast-flowing water erodes the base underneath and creates a trap that can catch things like logs — or people.

There are three sections to the Stamp River’s waterfalls, and Turko thinks Brandy is trapped in the second section, a spot where the water splits and diverts around a large rock.

On the left side, looking downriver, is a fish ladder to help spawning salmon climb up through the waterfall.

Turko said he’s confident that with about five to eight large sandbags, in addition to the ones already in the river to divert water for the salmon, they could push the flow of water to the left side — enough to allow him or members of the Port Alberni search and rescue dive team to get in and recover Brandy’s body.

Turko said the recovery effort itself would likely take less than a day — but time is running out.

“We can have a crane, we have sandbags, we have all the materials — we just need to go and do it,” said Turko, noting time is of the essence before fall rains really hit. “We just want to bring Brandy back.”

A variety of agencies and government departments would need to sign off on the plan, from the RCMP to B.C. Hydro, the Department of Fisheries and Oceans, B.C. Parks and local First Nations, said Nessel.

A B.C. Hydro-controlled dam on the Ash River controls approximately 20 per cent of the Stamp River’s flow, but it’s a considerable distance from the falls, so its influence on the conditions there is limited, said Ted Olynyk, media spokesman for B.C. Hydro. However, “if we had a formal request, we would do whatever we could to accommodate,” Olynyk said. The utility had been contacted by the RCMP but has not yet received any formal requests.

Another factor to consider is that the river is already operating at its minimum flow because of drought conditions, he said.

Tseshaht First Nation has not been approached regarding water flows on the Stamp River and could not comment until they had more information, said communications liaison Melissa Bigmore.

In a statement, B.C. Parks representative Nicole Rogers said that “any proposal to temporarily dam or divert a river within a provincial park would require a park use permit under the Park Act.”

B.C. Parks has not yet received such a proposal, which would also depend on getting necessary authorizations from other regulatory agencies with jurisdiction over water management, dam safety, fisheries, fish habitat, and environmental protection, said Rogers.

The feasibility or safety of a recovery operation is not within the jurisdiction of Fisheries and Oceans Canada, said Ben Stanford, regional director of communications for the Pacific region.

The B.C. RCMP issued a statement saying a river diversion would be beyond its scope, but if another agency undertakes such an initiative, “the RCMP would continue to support efforts to recover the deceased.”

Nessel said though the Port Alberni RCMP have been “amazing,” he’s left feeling uncertain about next steps.

And without a body, the family is finding it hard to move on.

“I can’t go on to the next step in my grief, and my family can’t, without that, you know — having her,” he said. “You never know, right? Because you don’t have that final closure.”
