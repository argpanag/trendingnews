Binance's $2.5B+ Breach May Be Signing Flaw, Not Stolen Keys, Blockaid CEO Says — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 30.10.2026 01:05 · source · permalink

The multi-billion-dollar drain on Binance's hot wallets may not have required attackers to steal the exchange's private keys at all, according to the head of blockchain security firm Blockaid, who said the evidence points instead to a compromised transaction-signing system.

Ido Ben-Natan, Blockaid's co-founder and chief executive, told Cointelegraph that early findings suggest "key misuse" — a scenario in which attackers trick or subvert the infrastructure that authorizes transfers, rather than extracting the secret material itself. "It seems to be… a compromise of signing infrastructure. We don't think a key was leaked," he said, cautioning that the conclusion is preliminary and rests on the firm's prior experience with similar exploits plus its reading of onchain activity.

The distinction carries weight for how exchanges and custodians think about defense. If an attacker can manipulate the workflow that approves transactions, then safeguarding private keys alone may not be enough; controls around signer prompts, delegation logic and authorization pipelines become the critical layer.

Binance has not confirmed the mechanism behind the theft, which on some estimates ranks as the largest crypto heist on record. Arkham CEO Miguel Morel told the same outlet there is not yet enough information to say whether stolen keys or compromised infrastructure drove the breach, and added that from what his firm can observe, the incident appears to still be unfolding.

Onchain trail: 50 authorizations in one transaction

Blockaid's analysis, published on X, described attacker behavior in which 50 EIP-7702 authorizations were bundled into a single transaction aimed at a wallet labeled Binance 14. EIP-7702 is a protocol upgrade that lets an account hand execution rights to smart-contract code. According to Blockaid, one of the authorizations matched the account's expected nonce, a detail that would make it more likely to be accepted by the network.

The affected account was then delegated to an unverified "sweeper" contract, Blockaid said, a structure that gave a designated operator the ability to pull tokens and Ether out of the delegated context. In effect, a legitimate delegation feature was turned into a channel for draining assets.

Ben-Natan said the broader operation could have involved multiple compromised devices and movement across Binance's network, though he stressed that this remains unconfirmed. The picture that emerges is a possible blend of onchain execution tricks — delegation and operator permissions — with offchain access or operational compromise reaching the signing pathway.

Parallels to Bybit and North Korean tactics

Asked whether the suspected signing manipulation resembled the February 2025 Bybit breach, Ben-Natan said the two incidents were "very, very similar." In that case, Bybit's forensic update said attackers compromised credentials belonging to a Safe developer, reached parts of the wallet provider's infrastructure and then deceived signers into approving a malicious transaction. The common thread is a workflow in which authorization can be subverted, whether through stolen upstream credentials or manipulation of the signing process itself.

Ben-Natan also said the operation's sophistication, particularly the onchain preparation, resembled the methods of North Korean-linked cyber actors. That is a contextual assessment rather than a confirmed attribution, but it signals that analysts see structural similarities in how the attackers positioned authorization mechanisms and execution logic to move funds.

Scale of the loss remains disputed

Estimates of the damage have swung widely since the outflows began on October 29, and the final figure is the biggest open question. Onchain trackers have flagged billions of dollars leaving Binance-linked Ethereum wallets, with a large share moving inside roughly 40 minutes. Stablecoin issuers moved quickly to freeze a substantial portion of the assets involved.

Note: Gross outflow figures reflect wallet holdings reported by trackers rather than a confirmed stolen amount; Binance has not published an official loss figure.

Tether CEO Paolo Ardoino said his company had frozen more than 1.567 billion USDT, and separate tallies put USDC freezes at roughly 823 million, bringing combined stablecoin freezes to about $2.4 billion, or close to 40% of the assets involved. World Liberty Financial also froze around $27 million of its USD1 token. Native coins, however, sit outside that safety net: roughly 195,000 ETH and about 16,700 BTC were beyond the reach of issuer freezes.

Binance's public messaging shifted during the episode. An initial statement said teams were "aware of reports of a potential security issue and are actively investigating" and described user funds as "completely safe," according to BleepingComputer, with the wording reportedly revised within minutes. The exchange has not said whether it will draw on its SAFU emergency fund, established in 2018.

Binance founder Changpeng Zhao wrote on X that it "does look like a hack on Binance," thanked Tether and Circle for their freezes and said that, as a shareholder, he expects all user funds to be fully covered. Responding to an outside estimate of about $1 billion in net losses, he said the figure "may be slightly higher" but would be covered.

Market fallout and what comes next

Markets fell as the story spread, with about $628 million in positions liquidated within an hour. BNB posted the steepest decline among major tokens, while Bitcoin and Ether also slid. For investors, the episode is a reminder that assets held on any exchange carry custody risk, including the risk of hot-wallet exploits. Stablecoin issuers demonstrated that they can freeze part of a stolen haul, but native ETH and BTC have no equivalent backstop. Scams typically follow large breaches, and Binance itself warned users about fake support messages, refund offers and recovery services.

The key questions now are whether private keys were ever exposed, exactly how the signing and authorization pipeline was manipulated, and whether the incident is still generating fresh malicious authorizations. Until those points are settled, the focus for market participants will be real-time transaction monitoring, delegation patterns such as EIP-7702 usage, and where the unfrozen ETH and BTC move next.
