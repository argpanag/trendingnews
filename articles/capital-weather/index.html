Soaking rain into Wednesday; significant nor’easter develops offshore late week

A chilly, soaking rain has made for a raw day across the area, and periods of rain will continue into early Wednesday.

With rain chances remaining high through tomorrow morning, an additional 0.5 to 1 inch is possible. Added to what has already fallen, this will end up being a meaningful rain event for the area — and we could still use it given the long-term dryness and drought.

Not just rain

In addition to the rain, coastal flood advisories for DC and much of the bay. Gale warnings at the mouth of the bay and small craft elsewhere.

This is largely because of persistent and sometimes strong winds from the east and northeast. Minor coastal flooding is likely in places such as Alexandria and Annapolis, with water levels potentially increasing at high tide through at least Thursday. A full "harvest moon" on Saturday is also contributing to the elevated water levels.

It's also quite cool. Afternoon temperatures are running about 10 to 15 degrees below average. The high so far today — occurring near midnight last night — was about 65, making it the coolest day since May. Similar daytime temperatures are expected Wednesday and Thursday before some moderation late in the week.

Nor'easter update

Winds from the east and northeast are with us for a while, partly because low pressure will develop offshore over the next few days before meandering off the Mid-Atlantic and southern New England coasts into the weekend.

It's still uncertain how much impact we'll see here, with higher odds farther northeast.

Weather models are mixed. The latest European model and its AI version bring the low close enough to the Mid-Atlantic coast to deliver some rain to our region this weekend, especially toward the Chesapeake Bay and points east. Other models, such as the American GFS, track it toward Cape Cod and southeastern New England.

Either way, it will probably keep gusty breezes going through the weekend. The closer tracks would bring stronger winds, the potential for continued coastal flooding and erosion, and could threaten the Oceans Calling festival.

Oceans Calling weather forecast: watching a coastal storm

A strong storm spinning off the coast makes for a somewhat uncertain forecast that will have to be updated. Some models keep the storm far enough off the coast to limit the impacts for the Oceans Calling Festival in Ocean City, Maryland, starting Friday, while others bring it close enough for impactful rain and wind.

The way we see it now is partly sunny to mostly cloudy all three days (Friday, Saturday, and Sunday) with daytime highs in the mid-60s to low 70s and a significant wind from the northeast – sustained near 20 mph with gusts near 35 mph – perhaps diminishing Sunday. Evening readings drop back to the low 60s with overnight lows in the upper 50s to near 60.

As for rain chances, while some models currently keep Ocean City dry all three days, it's too early to rule out some showers on any of the days. Even if there isn't much or any rain, the persistent onshore winds could lead to periodic flooding of portions of Coastal Highway and other low-lying roads, especially on Friday.

This forecast accounts for the possibility that the storm track shifts a bit closer to the coast than most models currently project. An even bigger shift, however, could increase the wind and rain potential. We'll keep this post updated with the latest forecast as the week progresses.

‘Monster’ El Niño reaches record strength by key measure

By an important measure, the ongoing El Niño event — still strengthening — is the most intense ever recorded.

The climate pattern — characterized by warmer-than-normal sea surface temperatures in the tropical Pacific Ocean — is supercharged this year, pushing ocean temperatures beyond anything previously measured.

The average sea surface temperature in the central and eastern tropical Pacific is now about 3.1 degrees Celsius (5.6 degrees Fahrenheit) above normal, edging past the previous record just over 3.0 C set in 2015.

El Niño reached this level about two months earlier than it did in 2015. With ocean temperatures still rising, the current event is poised to push well beyond previous records.

El Niño typically doesn't peak until November or December and "there is every reason to think that this one will as well," climate scientist Zeke Hausfather wrote at Carbon Brief.

What is the fall equinox — and why it matters

Astronomical autumn begins with the fall equinox, the moment when Earth’s tilt relative to the sun places neither hemisphere in favor. This year, it will occur at 8:05 p.m. Eastern time on Sept. 22. Day and night are nearly equal in length, and the sun crosses the equator heading south, a sign that summer is giving way to fall.

The fall equinox typically occurs on September 22 or 23, though the exact date and time vary slightly from year to year. At that precise moment, the sun’s direct rays shine over the equator. Afterward, the Northern Hemisphere begins tilting progressively away from the sun, leading to shorter days and longer nights.

The word “equinox” comes from the Latin aequus (“equal”) and nox (“night”) , reflecting the roughly balanced split between daylight and darkness. Most locations receive a few more minutes of daylight than darkness on the equinox because of atmospheric refraction and the way sunrise and sunset are defined. Still, it’s one of the two times each year when daylight and night are closest to equal.
