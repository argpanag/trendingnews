Why in News?  

Both Houses of Parliament were adjourned sine die on 13th August 2026, at the conclusion of the Monsoon Session 2026, yet they remained unprorogued for over 100 days, marking the longest unprorogued period on record.  

  • The prolonged delay has raised uncertainty over the convening of the customary Winter Session and concerns regarding parliamentary accountability and legislative scrutiny. 

What is Prorogation? 

  • About: Prorogation in the Indian Parliament is the formal termination of a parliamentary session (not just a single sitting). It is executed by the President of India, acting on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. 
  • Constitutional Provisions: Article 85 governs the summoning, prorogation, and dissolution of Parliament. 
    • Article 85(1): Empowers the President to summon each House of Parliament from time to time, ensuring the gap between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next does not exceed six months (mandating at least two sessions a year, though three (Budget, Monsoon, and Winter) are held by convention). 
      • Because the Constitution only prescribes the six-month ceiling, a government can technically satisfy Article 85(1) even if it cancels a customary session (such as the Winter Session), provided the next sitting convenes within the six-month window.  
    • Article 85(2)(a): Empowers the President to prorogue both Houses or either House separately, formally ending a session without dissolving the House. 
    • Article 85(2)(b): Empowers the President to dissolve the House of the People (Lok Sabha), ending its life and requiring fresh general elections (the Rajya Sabha, being a permanent House, is not subject to dissolution). 
    • Article 174: Provides corresponding provisions for the summoning, prorogation and dissolution of State Legislatures. 
  • Timeline: Conventionally, prorogation takes place within a few days of the Houses being adjourned sine die.  
    • However, the Constitution prescribes no fixed time limit for prorogation, nor does it stipulate a minimum notice period for summoning a new session (though 14 days' notice is followed by convention). The President can also prorogue a House while it is sitting. 
  • Importance: 
    • Short-Notice Reconvening: Keeping a House unprorogued after adjournment sine die allows the Speaker (under Rule 15 of Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in Lok Sabha) or the Chairman (under Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in the Council of States (Rajya Sabha)) to reconvene it at very short notice (even two days) for urgent legislative business, bypassing the 14-day convention for summoning a new session. 
    • Legislative Continuity: Pending bills do not lapse upon prorogation, though pending notices (other than those for introducing bills) generally lapse and must be reissued. 
    • Uninterrupted Privileges & Committees: MPs and State Legislators continue to enjoy their parliamentary privileges during unprorogued periods, and Parliamentary Committees continue functioning normally. 
    • Executive Accountability: Prolonged intervals between parliamentary sittings restrict opportunities to scrutinise the executive through Question Hour, motions and debates, undermining the principle of collective responsibility under Article 75(3). 
      • The average annual sitting days of the Lok Sabha declined from 135 days during the 1st Lok Sabha (1952–57) to 55 days during the 17th Lok Sabha (2019–24). 
    • Constitutional Conventions: Although delayed prorogation is not inherently unconstitutional, prolonged parliamentary inactivity raises concerns regarding adherence to parliamentary conventions, deliberative democracy and effective legislative oversight. 
  • Historical Precedents: 
    • 2008: Both Houses of Parliament remained unprorogued for 87 days. 
    • 1963–1987: Parliament stayed unprorogued for periods ranging from 41 to 76 days on multiple occasions. 
    • State/UT Legislatures: During the 7th Legislative Assembly of the NCT of Delhi (2020–25), sessions were called annually, divided into parts, and adjourned without proroguing the House for extended durations. 
    • Skipping the Winter Session: Since 1952, the customary Winter Session has been skipped four times (in 1975, 1979, 1984 and 2020).

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) 

1. Which constitutional provision governs the summoning and prorogation of Parliament?
Article 85 governs the summoning, prorogation and dissolution of Parliament. Article 85(1) also ensures that the gap between two sessions does not exceed six months. 

2. Who has the authority to prorogue Parliament, and on whose advice?
The President of India prorogues either or both Houses under Article 85(2)(a), acting on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. 

3. What is the difference between adjournment sine die and prorogation?
Adjournment sine die terminates a sitting without specifying the next meeting date, whereas prorogation formally terminates the entire parliamentary session. 

4. Do pending Bills lapse upon prorogation of Parliament?
No. Pending Bills generally do not lapse upon prorogation. However, pending notices, except notices for introducing Bills, generally lapse and must be reissued. 

5. What is the significance of keeping Parliament unprorogued after adjournment sine die?
It enables the Speaker or Chairman to reconvene the House at short notice under the applicable parliamentary rules, facilitating urgent legislative business without summoning a new session.
