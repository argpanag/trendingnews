Why do Man City fans have inflatable bananas? Explaining retro fan craze for Liverpool match at Anfield | Sporting News

TheTools · 11.10.2026 13:21 · source · permalink

Dom Farrell

So much of the build-up to Sunday's blockbuster Premier League showdown between Liverpool and Manchester City has been dominated by discussions about the atmosphere.

The Anfield crowd is widely anticipated to bring an extreme state of vitriolic hostility and fury to the occasion, as City step out for the first time since the Premier League found them guilty of all charges related to serious financial breaches of competition rules between 2009/10 and 2017/18.

Just after the period that was examined by an independent commission and resulted in a shockingly damning verdict – where City were found to have concocted "sham" sponsorship agreements with companies from Abu Dhabi and put forward witnesses believed to have lied – Jurgen Klopp's Liverpool went blow-for-blow with Pep Guardiola's City in thrilling title races in 2018/19 and 2021/22. Twice Liverpool racked up in excess of 90 points; twice they lost the league by a point.

Andoni Iraola and Enzo Maresca will lead the two sides on Sunday, and few of the main characters from those incredible battles remain. Enmity on the Kop, understandably, does.

"Absolutely, [the City players] can face the pressure," a relaxed Enzo Maresca said at his pre-match press conference. "We have players who have experience of similar situations. Enzo Fernandez played in Argentina. In Argentina, almost every game is like you go to Anfield."

The response from City's travelling supporters, viewed by millions of television viewers across the world, might invite puzzlement. Tucked away in the left-hand corner of the Anfield Road End, the Mancunian contingent will brandish dozens upon dozens of large inflatable bananas.

Why do Man City fans have inflatable bananas?

The origin story of Manchester City's near 40-year association with inflatable bananas goes back to the 1987/88 season, the club's first back in Division Two after being relegated from Division One the previous year. Like a lot of the best stories, it features some palpably rubbish wordplay.

"It started because of [former City forward] Imre Varadi," said Dante Friend of 1894, the City fan group who have distributed upwards of 1,000 inflatable bananas among supporters traveling to Anfield.

"It was just one guy at the game who referred to him as "Imre Banana". A guy called Frank Newton took an inflatable banana to the next game, and then everyone started doing it."

There were all sorts of other inflatables, from paddling pools to fried eggs and Frankenstein's monster, but it was the bananas that endured. Sometimes in this job, you find yourself writing the strangest sentences imaginable.

Varadi enjoyed a nomadic career in English football, representing City, Everton, Sheffield Wednesday and Leeds among many others. He scored 31 times in 81 appearances for City across all competitions, a very solid return at a time when the club was not at its best on the field. His wider legacy has nothing to do with any of those goals, and it's one he continues to embrace.

"The bananas are taking over Anfield," Varadi posted on X on Sunday morning (account handle: @imre_banana). "City fans are ready to party at Liverpool."

Manchester also knew how to throw a party during the mid-to-late 1980s, as the acid house music scene that in turn spawned the 'Madchester' craze thrived. The famous Hacienda nightclub was the high church of the movement – a temple of optimism and love via chemical refreshment, that stood at odds with the often desolate state of hooligan-addled English football as Margaret Thatcher's conservative government oversaw a period of post-industrial decline in many of the country's biggest cities.

"The reason some people were doing it as a joke was because they were going out clubbing on a Friday night to places like the Hacienda and the Brickhouse and were absolutely off their heads on ecstasy, then falling into the match the next afternoon," Friend recalled.

"They weren't interested in fighting. Most of the fans were having a laugh. We were s***, we were in Division Two. I think, every now and then, when it's fan-led and not club-led, [bringing back the bananas] can be really good. It'll be a laugh."

And that's the spirit being tapped into as Manchester City, to many. begin life as pariahs of English football. The spirit of a club founded in 1894 that won its first trophy in 1904 and has seen all sorts of majestically brilliant, perplexingly bad and mind-numbingly average iterations throughout this long history.

Their trajectory was changed beyond all recognition by Sheikh Mansour's 2008 takeover. Pending an appeal, the wrongdoing of the Abu Dhabi royal's trusted executives will change that path once again. But nothing is permanent. Manchester City were here before their current ownership, and it would be a travesty for generation upon generation of fans if they weren't after them.

When City struggled in the 1980s and again, even more severely in the 1990s, inflatable bananas were there to celebrate every promotion back to the big time. They've featured in every pitch invasion after every Premier League success at the Etihad Stadium. Whatever those titles mean now, they promoted grown adults to wave ridiculous, oversized novelty fruit above their heads. Because sport is brilliant like that.

Sunday will be a show of defiance from those travelling City fans, who before the days of billion-dollar spending counted gallows humour and sarcasm as their most potent weapons. They're not scared to go back there if they have to. Don't threaten them with a good time.

"The message is obviously defiance," Friend added. "We don't give a f— who the owners are, we are the club. The fans are the club."
