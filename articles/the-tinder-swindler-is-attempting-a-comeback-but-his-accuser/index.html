The Tinder Swindler is attempting a 'comeback', but his accusers urge people to remember one thing

TheTools · 16.09.2026 11:06 · source · permalink

They urge people to remember what he is: a convicted fraudster

‘The Tinder Swindler was the best thing that ever happened to me,’ Simon Leviev recently told The Times, referring to the 2022 Netflix documentary that tracked his crimes as a con artist. Born Shimon Yehuda Hayut, he is estimated to have stolen around $10 million (£739k) from victims around the world. His scheme was to pretend to be the heir of a diamond empire, match with a woman on a dating app, lure them in with luxury hotel stays and private jets, feign an emergency, ask for a huge loan and then disappear.

He was first convicted of fraud in Finland in 2015 and sentenced to three years in prison. After two years, he returned to his birth country of Israel, where he was recharged and sentenced – before changing his name to Leviev. He then began tricking women he met on dating apps into lending him money that he never repaid. In 2019, he was arrested in Greece and sentenced to 15 months in prison in Israel, but released after five months due to the Covid pandemic – when he swiftly picked up where he’d left off.

Now, almost five years after the documentary exposed his crimes and topped the streamer’s most-viewed charts in 92 countries, Leviev claims to be a free man. For the first time in 15 years, he is not facing a court case (although one investigation involving his ex-girlfriend Kate Konlin is still ongoing). He boasts of a Hollywood manager and says he is developing his own dating app and launching a fragrance – while making a fortune from recorded birthday greetings, TV appearances and Bitcoin (his own cryptocurrency Tind failed to launch in 2022).

If it sounds too good to be true, it's because it most likely is. ‘This is the way fraudsters work,’ says Pernilla Sjøholm, one of Leviev’s accusers who featured in the film. ‘This is a tactic for him so he can commit more fraud.’

Sjøholm says Leviev swindled an estimated $45,000 (£33,000) from her after they met through Tinder in 2018. ‘Since Sweden doesn’t have an extradition order with Israel, they dropped [my case] – not for lack of evidence,’ she explains. ‘It feels like he is in a safe space to commit crimes as long as he involves multiple countries.’

It’s a similar story for Cecilie Fjellhøy, who met Leviev on Tinder in 2018. Over the course of their relationship, she claims to have lost more than £200k to his schemes. The Met Police dropped her case in 2023, having failed to extradite Leviev to the UK while he was living in Georgia. ‘I had to file for bankruptcy,’ she tells Grazia. ‘I was taken to court by four banks and had to go through two trial rounds where I was the perpetrator. No one cared about Simon. I had to sell my flat. This has had huge consequences for me.’

They urge anyone tempted to believe that Leviev is free, innocent or ‘laughing all the way to the bank’, as he told The Times, to remember that he is a convicted fraudster and professional liar. ‘He’s never going to change,’ says Sjøholm, who now works as an activist and keynote speaker on cybersecurity and fraud. ‘Don’t believe everything that you see on social media. A lot of the videos can be old. They can be fake. They can be staged.’

Fjellhøy agrees: ‘He’s a narcissist. He doesn’t have empathy. His career is just to continue to scam.’

One of the more galling claims in Leviev’s recent interview is that he and his victims are alike. ‘I’m happy to see them doing well,’ he said. ‘They’re cashing out. I’m cashing out as well.’ Unsurprisingly, the women at the centre of this story do not see it that way.

Although they have built careers off the back of The Tinder Swindler – Fjellhøy is also a public speaker and hosts Netflix’s true crime investigative series, Love Con Revenge – they are still dealing with immense trauma. ‘I would love to give it an American happy ending, but I have been really, really struggling,’ says Sjøholm. ‘He took my home fund. I have two kids. I am still fighting to get my life back.’

Leviev’s so-called ‘comeback’ exposes the ethical risk of true-crime documentaries making stars of their protagonists. ‘I find it disturbing seeing Anna Delvey [the Russian German socialite convicted of grand larceny in 2019] on Dancing with the Stars with an ankle monitor on,’ says Sjøholm. Still, she believes the benefit of exposing crimes on a global stage far outweighs the risk. ‘It saves people and it sends a message to anyone committing these sorts of crimes that they could be exposed.’

Fjellhøy, who co-founded LoveSaid – a non-profit organisation that supports victims of romance fraud – agrees that victims are the priority. ‘I think true-crime documentaries can reduce the stigma that surrounds these crimes,’ she explains. ‘We believe that between five and 15 per cent of romance fraud goes unreported because there is so much shame. It’s good people that this happens to. We can’t let evil win.’
