ITV’s Adultery is a guilty pleasure that can’t keep its pants on

TheTools · 26.10.2026 03:59 · source · permalink

This soapy drama of dangerous liaisons is part Harold Pinter, part Jilly Cooper

3/5

Beth (Romola Garai) and Tom (Dominic Cooper) are the two main players in a drama called Adultery (ITV1), so it’s hardly a spoiler to tell you that they have an affair. Indeed, from the moment Beth, an addled mum in a permanent rush with a useless husband and two teenage boys in tow, knocks Tom off his bike… from the moment they exchange a smouldering you-nearly-killed-me god that’s so hot – glance, Adultery is headed inexorably to the bedroom.

What follows is an absorbing – if at times lopsided – mixture of soap and sociology. Sometimes it’s Jilly Cooper, others it’s Harold Pinter. Tom, who is a well-to-do GP, and Beth, a fitness instructor who drives around in a car with her name on it (illicit affair 101: don’t do that), fall hook, line and sinker for one another and by the middle of episode two have gone from lingering stares via brushed hands at parties to full-on Ugandan discussions wherever they can get away with it.

The plot follows the standard dangerous liaisons narrative from early excitement to near-discovery to actual discovery (courtesy of that most modern gumshoe, the doorbell cam.) Three episodes (of six) in and catastrophe already awaits. Neither Beth nor Tom wants to set fire to a stable-ish family life and both are well aware that their actions and deceptions will hurt the people they love. But then a couple of glasses of wine and off they go again.

Writer Danny Brocklehurst (Brassic) is too good not to try to add something to this well-storied spiral, and he does it by paralleling Tom and Beth’s search for excitement and danger with their own children’s. Beth’s son Ollie (Johnny Sanpher) has been going out with Tom’s daughter Jess (Leila Khan) for a while now, and they fizz with the intensity of love’s young dream. Thus framed, you can see why Tom and Beth might also want some of that elixir, even if they keep almost being caught with their pants down by their own kids.

At the same time, Brocklehurst shows how Tom and Beth’s selfishness corrupts the whole. Cheating is not just betraying a partner, Adultery suggests, it’s cheating everyone you know.

That’s the thinkie bit, now for the soap: Brocklehurst has been spending a lot of time adapting Harlan Coben novels for Netflix in the past few years, and it shows. Adultery really wants to be a schlocky thriller when it grows up, with every episode bringing in new secrets and unexpected connections. Ordinary people are always – always – haunted by trauma from their past, trauma that is withheld and then conveniently drip-fed on an ad-break by ad-break basis to the point where the authenticity that a tale like this relies on is lost. Would two people like Tom and Beth embark upon a ruinous affair? Possibly. Would it play out like this, involving Coben-esque plot twists and minor characters happening to see vital things at important moments? Probably not.

That’s why Adultery sits a little strangely. Its title promises an anatomy of betrayal, and there are enough hints of the cerebral to think that’s where we’re heading, but it can’t resist the sensational. Sometimes, when presented with a chance for some easy excitement, you’re better off saying no.

Adultery is on ITVX and continues on Monday, Oct 26 at 9pm
