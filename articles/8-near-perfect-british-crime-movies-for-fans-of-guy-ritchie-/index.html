8 Near-Perfect British Crime Movies For Fans Of Guy Ritchie's The Gentlemen

TheTools · 21.11.2026 17:20 · source · permalink

Considering how irresistibly drawn to the genre he seems to be and how much British crime movies and TV shows owe to him over the past 20-odd years, you'd be forgiven for thinking that Guy Ritchie invented the entire British crime playbook. Netflix's The Gentlemen has exploded in popularity since its debut a couple of years ago as a spinoff from Ritchie's original movie. It feels like a greatest hits compilation of all the best parts of what he's done in the genre so far.

Despite the diversity of his work, including, of course, working with Disney and making two Sherlock Holmes movies, Guy Ritchie's crime movies have a distinct identity to them. He exploded onto the scene with Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels, introducing frenzied editing, hilarious dialog, and irresistibly charming criminal caricatures. Even when slumming it in the murkiest parts of the underbelly of Britain, he's always shown an interest in the aspirational opulence of crime. He shares that with Martin Scorsese, of course.

On top of that, his movies capture a sort of romanticized brutal realism of the British crime underworld with his cartoonish characters. Ritchie has a particular flair for eccentricity and often drops in aristocratic figures to clash with his outright villains, which is why The Gentlemen works so well as a concept.

With fans waiting for The Gentlemen Season 3, which is now on the way and likely to have already devoured Guy Ritchie's own crime movies, there are a lot of British crime movie classics from the past six decades that can actually scratch the exact same itch while you wait.

8 Sexy Beast (2000)

While several of Guy Ritchie's movies focus on heists and the often calamitous aftermath, Jonathan Glazer's 2000 directorial debut, Sexy Beast, flips the standard script on heist movies, focusing instead on the miserable process of trying to get a gang back together. The Gentlemen's Ray Winston plays Gal, a retired safe cracker whose peaceful post-crime life in Spain is interrupted by the hand grenade that is terrifying sociopath Don Logan, played by a spellbinding Ben Kingsley, who arrives to drag him back to London for one last job.

Instantly critically acclaimed upon release, the film rightly earned Kingsley an Oscar nomination for his utterly transfixing performance. Sexy Beast leans a little more psychological than Ritchie's grander capers, but in Don Logan, it arguably has the most Guy Ritchie-like villain that he never created.

7 Legend (2015)

Brian Helgeland's 2015 crime biopic might end on a more mournful note than Guy Ritchie's, which tend towards criminal victories, but it's still one of the best crime movies released during Ritchie's career and shares some of the same DNA.

Tom Hardy pulls double duty as both Ronnie and Reggie Kray, the notorious twin gangsters who ruled London's East End during the 1960s with an iron fist. In those two performances, Hardy offers the full spectrum of Ritchie-like characters, from Reggie's suave, business-minded operator to Ronnie's terrifyingly volatile wild card.

And despite dealing with incredibly heavy subject matter, which is quite ingeniously framed around Reggie's relationship with his troubled wife Frances Shea (Emily Browning). It offers the same sort of Game of Thrones-like power machinations, has moments of brutal violence, and features some of the real-life criminal figures who were clearly so inspirational to Guy Ritchie's approach to storytelling in the first place.

6 Gangster No. 1 (2000)

Long before he was breaking hearts and cracking heads as Vision in the MCU, Paul Bettany delivered a stunning performance in Paul McGuigan's Gangster No. 1 back in 2000. He plays a nameless, narcissistic young enforcer obsessed with his charismatic boss, Freddie Mayes, played by Harry Potter's David Thewlis. Again, in 1960s London, the story is retold by an older, embittered version of the same character, played by British legend Malcolm McDowell, and shows how envy and ambition destroy a criminal organization from the inside out.

Gangster No. 1 actually wasn't critically acclaimed at the time of its release, mostly thanks to its brutal portrayal of violence, which will come as a bit of a shock to Paul Bettany's more recent fans. But this movie deserves to be more than just a cult gem, and it has aged very well.

Ritchie fans will immediately recognize the period Mod fashion, stylish camera work, and the fascination with the underworld hierarchy at the heart of the movie. There isn't the same sense of cheeky humor because that would have made the violence feel too jarring, but this is an incredible companion piece to Ritchie's later work that reveals the psychotic reality beneath the colorful characters.

5 Layer Cake (2004)

It should come as no surprise that Guy Ritchie's long-term producing partner Matthew Vaughn would appear here, and his throughout-2004 adaptation of J.J. Connolly's novel is one of the closest movies of the period to what Ritchie would have made himself.

Starring a pre-Bond Daniel Craig as an unnamed London drug dealer trying to cash out quietly, Layer Cake tells the almost clichéd story of the last tasks he must take on before he's allowed his freedom. First, he has to find a rich associate's missing daughter, and then broker a massive drug deal, which drags him into a destructive web of Serbian war criminals and corrupt British elites and is inevitably far from straightforward.

Layer Cake reportedly played a big part in Daniel Craig landing the 007 gig, but more importantly for The Gentlemen fans, it is very much a spiritual cousin to Ritchie's modern work. Craig's antihero is the same mix of businessman first, gangster second as Eddie Hornyman, and the parallels between the two worlds, focusing on class division and the murky truth of country estate aristocrats, are an undeniable draw.

4 Performance (1970)

By far the weirdest movie on this list, 1970's Performance was co-directed by Donald Camell and Nicolas Roeg and offers a very different take on conventional British crime movies. James Fox plays Charles, a sadistic East End enforcer who is forced to go on the run from his own crime boss and seeks sanctuary in the Notting Hill basement of reclusive burnout rock star Turner (played by Mick Jagger). Over a series of drug-fueled mind games, the pair begin swapping identities.

It's another of those class-clash stories that makes The Gentlemen so compelling, but with a very different spin. Warner Brothers infamously hated the movie and shelved it for 2 years, and initial reviews were baffled by its psychedelic approach to storytelling. But decades later, it is an undisputed cult masterpiece that belongs in the upper echelons of British crime movies, precisely because of how different it is. Its opening half is a conventionally brutal slice of London gangland, but then it spirals into a confident surrealism and it's still a great watch.

3 Get Carter (1971)

Mike Hodges, 1971, British crime noir, is somewhat unconventional alongside the usual London-centric gangland movies that have followed it because it's set up in the North East of England. It stars Michael Caine at his absolute coolest as Jack Carter, a London-based mobster who travels home to Newcastle when his brother dies in a suspicious car crash. Refusing to accept the official story, Carter tears through the local underworld, mercilessly looking for answers, and uncovers a web of corruption and exploitation.

It's an unashamedly bleak movie. It trades hard on Caine's aura and doesn't give him the opportunity to show the sparkling charm that he was already known for, but his screen presence is mesmerizing.

Get Carter is now recognized as an absolute classic that redefined British crime cinema. Though it lacks the dark comedy of Ritchie's work, it shares the same obsession with how criminal enterprises infect every level of local society. Kane's sharply dressed outsider also feels like the blueprint for a lot of Ritchie's elegant hitmen, particularly Charlie Hunnam's Ray in the original The Gentlemen movie.

And incidentally, if anyone ever suggests you should watch the 2000 remake with Sylvester Stallone, delete them from your contacts book. They do not like you.

2 In Bruges (2008)

Again, set away from London but very, very much tied to the capital's criminal underworld, Martin McDonagh's 2008 dark comedy follows two London-based Irish hitmen: Ray, played by Colin Farrell, and Ken, Brendan Gleeson, who are ordered to lie low in the beautiful Belgian city of Bruges when a job goes catastrophically wrong.

In Bruges' comedy is excellent, clashing Ken's wide-eyed enthusiasm for the medieval architecture of the fairy-tale city with Ray's misery, and when their enforced isolation is interrupted by volatile boss Harry (Ralph Fiennes on excellent form), the drama escalates into a brilliant standoff.

In Bruges was a sleeper hit that earned widespread critical acclaim and an Oscar nomination for McDonagh's screenplay, and rightly so. It has wonderfully colorful characters, excellent performances by all three of the main characters, and, like Ritchie's work, makes it perversely inescapable to empathize with deeply flawed criminal characters, as well as the contradiction of the underworld having an unbreakable moral code. And of course, that leads to a very memorable ending.

1 The Long Good Friday (1980)

Long before Guy Ritchie ever picked up a camera, John Mackenzie's 1980 masterpiece set the blueprint for modern British gangster movies. In The Long Good Friday, Bob Hoskins gives one of his greatest performances as Harold Shand, an ambitious London crime lord trying to go legitimate by partnering with the American mafia to redevelop London Docklands.

It's a story that sounds like it could have been in any number of Guy Ritchie movies. Over a single Easter weekend, a series of coordinated bombings destroy his empire while he desperately hunts for the traitor in his ranks.

Often hailed as one of the greatest British movies of all time, The Long Good Friday is certainly one of the best British crime movies ever made and features the exact DNA of Ritchie's later works. Harold's vision of respectable corporate legitimacy colliding with violence is the kind of contradiction in Guy Ritchie's best stories. Hoskins' explosive, charismatic performance basically set the gold standard for every London crime boss who followed on screen for years.
