Europe’s Streets Reject the Economy of War

TheTools · 06.11.2026 07:00 · source · permalink

Photograph Source: Albinfo – CC BY 4.0

Europe’s autumn unrest did not begin beneath the television cameras in Paris. Before students blockaded schools in mainland France, teachers and parents in Mayotte (a French territory in the Indian Ocean) and students in French Guiana (a French territory in South America) had already demanded the elementary conditions of an education. Their protests received little attention, since the geography of recognition follows an old colonial map. When the same anger reached the French capital and its suburbs, it became a national crisis. From there, images of marches, school occupations, and confrontations travelled across a continent where millions recognise the same predicament: everything necessary for life becomes more expensive, while the institutions meant to sustain life are steadily weakened.

There is no single European uprising, with one organisation or an agreed programme, but there is an unfolding of unsettled anger and frustration. In Albania, the dispute began over the sale of coastal land, in Germany the argument is over possible conscription for a meaningless war, and then in Portugal and Spain the issue was housing. Nevertheless, four grievances recur: the erosion of social provision alongside enrichment at the top, police repression of dissent, restrictions on solidarity with Palestine, and the diversion of political energy and public resources into warfare. These grievances increasingly encounter one another in the streets. What connects them is a crisis of confidence in governments that demand sacrifice while insulating wealth and military expenditure from popular scrutiny.

The 31st Fructidor of Emmanuel Macron

Mayotte and French Guiana sounded the alarm first. At Mayotte’s Cité du Nord school, mobilisation began on 31 August. Roughly 2,300 pupils are accommodated in school buildings designed for 900. In Cayenne, French Guiana, students at the Félix-Éboué school presented seventeen demands on 7 September, including ventilation, decent sanitation, and an affordable, functioning canteen. The French media does not always document these protests, so far away, and yet technically in France, but they start with the protests at Saint-Exupéry in Créteil when pupils and parents protested a lack of teachers. The overseas protests expose inequalities within the French state that disappear when France is described through national averages. It was because of the protests that the inequality became tangible rather than statistical – in Mamoudzou (Mayotte), on 29 September, pupils spoke of such unbelievable things as hunger, insecure journeys to school, and insufficient facilities. Trade unions in Mayotte demanded that social rights on these islands of 320,000 people be brought in line with those of the entire French nation. What was presented by the students as a local problem contained an indictment of French imperialism. The promise of equal citizenship had not produced equal conditions of life.

On the mainland, the spread of school blockades brought familiar grievances into sharper focus: unfilled teaching posts, overcrowded timetables, deteriorating buildings, and anxiety about access to university. By 29 September, Le Monde reported disruption at 180 establishments. The school gate became a gathering place for a generation instructed to compete for a future whose foundations were being dismantled. Parents and teachers could recognise their own exhaustion in these demands. Public education was revealing what austerity means in practice: the transfer of responsibility from collective institutions to households, and within households to those least able to purchase a private alternative.

France’s movement carries the accumulated experience of earlier confrontations. The Yellow Vests (Gilets Jaunes) of 2018 made visible the costs imposed on working people whose lives depended on cars and whose wages could not absorb another petrol increase. Battles over pensions raised the question of who should bear the cost of longer working lives if retirement ages are raised. The present students inherit that argument about the distribution of sacrifice, but their mobilisation also supplies a different emphasis. Before they can fight over retirement, they must fight for teachers, meals, and classrooms. The social contract is being contested at both ends of a working life, and throughout the years between them. French President Emmanuel and Brigitte Macron were too busy dancing at a Céline Dion concert in Nanterre to be bothered with the burning streets.

Go Live in a Tent

Belgium, Spain, and Portugal widen the picture. Belgian demonstrations have joined anger over austerity to unrest in education. Spanish housing encampments and Portuguese marches insist that a wage is insufficient protection when rent absorbs the means of living. These are struggles over the social wage: the housing, transport, education, and healthcare that determine what an income can buy. A nominal pay rise cannot compensate indefinitely for their deterioration. The household budget and the public budget meet in the same place. When governments reduce collective provision, families pay again through fees, rents, debt, and unpaid labour.

The class content of capitalist life is made absolutely clear in the arena of housing. For a tenant, an apartment is a means to live, while for an investment fund, it is a stream of revenue. The clash between the two is the frontline of the class struggle. Government policies that increase the value of property assist the investment funds, but they then deepen the insecurity of the tenants. The same fundamental contradictions of class warfare run through tourism development, where public authorities advertise prosperity while residents face displacement and declined access to their neighbourhoods. Airbnb becomes the face of housing, pushing older residents further and further from the tourist-heavy city centre. Europe’s housing protests challenge the economic model that relies on asset values and that relies upon tourism, rather than the economic model that builds a productive economy for the people themselves.

Albania’s Flamingo Movement

In Albania, this class contradiction has been defined by a flamingo. Demonstrations against luxury developments associated with Jared Kushner and Ivanka Trump began in late May and grew into a wider challenge to Prime Minister Edi Rama. Projects around the Vjosa-Narta protected area and Sazan Island brought together environmental objections, disputed property claims, and anger about government accountability. Residents confronted fences on land that they claimed, followed by clashes with private security forces. The flamingo, a threatened inhabitant of the wetland, became a symbol through which people defended their own place in the country’s future. Albania’s national bird is the golden eagle, but if these protests succeed, the country might have to swap it with the flamingo.

The Albanian government argued that major investors, such as Kushner and Trump, will transform tourism and create opportunities, but protestors ask why such transformation should endanger protected landscapes and proceed through contested arrangements. Their anger is sharpened along the axis of class as they compare these luxury projects to inadequate everyday services, including public housing. Land that is the common inheritance is being given away to people like Kushner-Trump without payment, which makes this no longer about development but about corruption and ecological survival.

Rama, who comes from an artistic and political family, has been in power since 2013, but miscalculated the public mood this time. In late October, he announced that there would be substantial fines for people who remained in crowds where violence or looting occurs, even if they themselves do not do any of those things. Such collective exposure is intended not to protect property, but to intimidate those who would otherwise want to be part of the peaceful protests. Albania, like every other European country, treats anger over austerity as a security problem rather than an opportunity for democratic dialogue. A protest that began around investors seizing land has now been transformed into a debate about the treatment of ordinary citizens.

GL-304

On 5 October, the police near a protest at a school in Lens (France) threw a GL-304 stun grenade, which exploded near some students. One student, age 15, lost his hand in the blast. Two days later, France’s interior minister Laurent Nuñez announced the temporary suspension of the weapon during school demonstrations. The education minister Édouard Geffray announced that planned job cuts would be suspended. There was money in the state’s budget to deploy heavily armed police to the school, but no money to hire new teachers to fill in the already depleted ranks. This was the decision of the state, and this gave parents and pupils further reason to distrust its institutions.

Distrust leads to anger, which can germinate into hate. People took to the streets to meet the police violence with violence, which meant attacks on shops and looting, s well as the burning of barricades. They do not define the totality of the protests, but they are an accurate measure of the feelings of a large number of people. The government used images of the destruction to try and displace discussion about their own policies, but however many broken windows they showed, the popular mood focused on the lack of desks and teachers in classrooms. An inventory of damage has not fully prevented the examination of its social setting, namely the austerity wasteland of working-class France.

Bedarfswehrpflicht in Germany

The German government, caught in its own hype about the Ukraine war, has begun to consider the use of conscription to draw in its youth to the military. Laws have been passed that ensure that if there is a shortfall in troops, then the government can trigger an emergency conscription (Bedarfswehrpflicht). This is deeply unpopular amongst the youth, who are frustrated like their European peers with lack of investment in education, lack of relief for rising living costs, and hostility by their government for a climate agenda and for an end to the genocide in Palestine. On 31 October, thousands of people – under the initiative of Gen Z regelt (Gen Z takes charge!) – gathered at Berlin’s Brandenburg Gate. Their message was peaceful and simple: more investment in people and less in war. The previous day in Italy, USB, an Italian trade union mobilised workers for a general strike against the war economy and inflation, connecting wages and pensions to spending on weapons. Dockworkers blocked parts of Genoa’s port. NATO foreign policy took on a material form at the docks: ships and their cargo linked decisions about war and the daily organisation of production. Warfare has entered daily life through the ugliness of inflation for necessities.

NATO’s Hague Declaration (2025) committed the member states of this military alliance to a target of 5% of GDP toward military spending by 2035, making a powerful claim on scarce public resources. The European Union, which had been strict about fiscal rules, has suddenly become flexible for increased military expenditure. Germany’s parliament lifted a debt brake for the military. The political choices are straightforward – when it comes to the military industries, prudence is unnecessary; austerity is only required against the working-class and the working-class is crushed when it tries to lift its voice into the debate about these kinds of choices.

Young people across Europe are being told to shoulder two responsibilities: to work hard in precarious employment with expensive housing so that they can take care of the pension claims of their grandparents and parents, and to prepare themselves for military conscription for wars that they do not understand and do not want.

Viva Palestine

Across Europe, at the protests, the issue of Palestine has a shadowy presence. From the start of Israel’s genocidal war in October 2023, governments across Europe effectively banned protests for Palestine. In France, the Interior Minister Gérald Darmanin’s October 2023 instructions called for prohibitions on pro-Palestinian demonstrations. A few days later, the Conseil d’État clarified that such a blanket prohibition was not legal, but that prefects in different parts of the country had to assess the circumstances one-by-one. In fact, since then, the prefects have largely followed the instruction from Darmanin and banned demonstrations, with the idea being that solidarity with Palestinians comes with a presumption of danger, making the defence of Palestinian lives inseparable from the defence of freedom of assembly.

The pressure continued beyond that first confrontation, since by 2025 human rights organisations had begun to document disproportionate restrictions on Palestinian solidarity, not just in France but in Germany and in other European countries. Between banned events, even on university campuses, and police violence came the deportations or denial of entry to foreign participants for events. These experiences created a bitter lesson: European governments could speak about universal rights (such as, for instance for Ukrainians) while restricting those demanding that the same rights apply to Palestinians. Protesters carried that experience into subsequent struggles over education, racism, public spending, and the conduct of the police. Shared encounters with police lines, disciplinary proceedings, and official denunciation widened political understanding. Young people, in particular, questioned why democratic participation is tolerated only while it remains inconsequential and why action for Palestine is banned.

Organisation

These movements face difficult questions about organisation and direction. Anger alone cannot determine whether the outcome will be stronger public provision, repression or gains for the far right of a special type. The latter can redirect insecurity towards migrants while leaving concentrated ownership intact. A durable alternative requires links among students, tenants, workers, and the movements for peace and Palestinian freedom. Their common ground lies in a demand that wealth sustain human life and that democratic rights protect the struggle to secure it. Across Europe, people are asking for schools they can learn in, homes they can afford and futures beyond war. Whether these demands acquire lasting political force will depend on patient work beyond the spectacular day of protest, through assemblies, unions, and neighbourhood organisations capable of turning shared injury into a programme and the strength to win. Elections are an encounter with power, which will be tested in the French presidential election. But beyond that is the arena of public opinion, which is increasingly moving away from the establishment and towards other considerations……on the streets of Paris, they are singing the Internationale.
