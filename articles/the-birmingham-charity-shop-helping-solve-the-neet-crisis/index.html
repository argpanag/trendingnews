The Birmingham charity shop helping solve the NEET crisis

TheTools · 18.11.2026 06:00 · source · permalink

As the spotlight on the number of young people not in employment, education or training (NEET) intensifies, we headed to Birmingham to meet the people at Jericho, which runs seven non-profits giving people who face traditional barriers to employment supported work placements

One of the young apprentices working at the ReUsers. Image: The Jericho Foundation

Demi Laville considers herself a bit of a magpie. So, a job in an enormous charity shop where she gets to sort through an eclectic mix of donations is a nice gig.

We’re at ReUsers in Sutton Coldfield in Birmingham. It’s a ‘charity department store’, a one-stop shop for almost anything you might need for your home, filled with preowned treasures. There are sections for furniture, tech, clothes, toys and even a room dedicated entirely to Christmas decorations. 

ReUsers is a non-profit run by Jericho, a social enterprise backed by Big Issue Invest, Big Issue’s social investment arm. It provides supported work opportunities to people who are facing extreme challenges in getting a job. It supports people of all ages but is particularly important for young people now, as nearly a million are out of work, education or training.

Until six months ago, when she secured an apprenticeship at ReUsers, 18-year-old Laville was among them.

“I was struggling finding employment or something to do,” she says. “I think everywhere expects you to have experience and you can’t expect that. It’s unrealistic.”

Laville’s apprenticeship has already been transformative, helping her “get better at communicating and customer service”. She hopes to go into property management one day and this is a stepping stone.

The spotlight is on Britain’s youth unemployment crisis again this week, as former health minister Alan Milburn has completed his published his government-commissioned review and offered his recommendations on how to tackle the issue.

In the landmark report, he proposed an ‘Opportunity Guarantee’ to ensure that young people have a legal right to a route into work, education or training. Alongside this would be a ‘Support Guarantee’, so that young people are legally entitled to support to help them get into work or education.

Although the promise of employment support for young people is welcomed, Jericho chief executive Katie Webb argues that there isn’t enough value placed on supported work placements like those offered at ReUsers and their other non-profits. 

“We need our projects to be recognised by government as part of an ecosystem of support and we’re not. We’re very much seen as an add-on that can be funded through small charitable trusts and foundations and bigger funders,” Webb explains. 

She adds that a “supported work placement can make the difference between a lifetime of unemployment and a lifetime of participation”.  

“Let’s fund that. It’s not cheap. It’s not a quick fix. We’re not an organisation that works with a high volume of people. But when we do that long-term intensive support, it changes the course of that person’s life and it’s invaluable,” Webb says. 

Big Issue recognises the value of Jericho’s work. Big Issue Invest stepped in after the organisation faced a £140,000 rise in running costs, following increases in the apprentice wage, national minimum wage and employer national insurance contributions. 

“In an already challenging financial climate, this additional burden, while undoubtedly positive for the individuals we support, made it more difficult than ever to deliver our mission,” said Rebecca Stone, director of income generation at Jericho. 

“The support we received from the Big Issue Invest helped us to navigate that challenging period, without sacrificing the quality of our social impact.” 

And that impact can be life0changing. Mohzam Malik has been with ReUsers for 11 years now, having started as an apprentice. He showed an interest in electronics so, after his apprenticeship finished, his manager offered him a full-time position in that department – a department which he now runs.

He had previously done a carpentry apprenticeship but this was “a completely different challenge” which he grew to love.

“I didn’t think I’d last the apprenticeship here and I’ve lasted 11 years,” Malik says.

“We just don’t give up on people,” adds Webb. “There are so many people who come through our doors and they just feel that there’s nothing left for them, every which way they turn there’s no opportunities and we create an opportunity where no other chance exists.”

One of Jericho’s other non-profits, the Wood Shack, sits just next door to the ReUsers. They craft beautiful items out of used pieces of wood and sell it on, training up people who traditionally face barriers to the workplace, many of them survivors of modern slavery.

“For some of the guys, they’re in shared housing and they’re a bit isolated from other people. They’re coming in and they’ve got some people to chat to and it’s the nicest way to spend their days rather than sitting around waiting for the next step,” says Wood Shack manager Jon Owens.

“We have a lot of guys coming in on six-month placements and we’ll do anything from breaking pallets and cleaning stuff up to some of their own little projects and making stuff. It’s just really fun. 

“They’re a great bunch of guys and it’s always an eclectic mix. You get all sorts of different people. We have 17-year-old kids that won’t talk to anybody and 40-year-old blokes who are just after a bit of companionship and chatting.”

In April to June 2026, 981,000 young people aged 16 to 24 were NEET, representing 13% of that age group. That’s roughly one in eight people in that age group out of work or education.

“It’s more important now than it ever has been that Jericho’s social enterprises offer supported work placements for young people and other people who just can’t get a break in the labour market. It’s such a tough climate to enter the world of employment for people without barriers to the workplace,” Webb adds.

“For people who are disadvantaged by a myriad of barriers, we provide them with an opportunity that no one else will give them. We run seven different not-for-profit businesses and the purpose of all of that activity is to just take someone who’s stuck and help them become unstuck and ready for the world of work.”

Gina Stonehouse, a ReUsers senior supervisor, remembers supporting one young woman who had recently had a child taken away and was in an abusive relationship.  

But after she had built her confidence up through working, “she got herself out, met somebody else, got her son back and completely turned her life around”. 

Dawn Hall, the people support manager at Jericho’s Sutton Coldfield sites, points to another individual: a young man with learning disabilities who had been in a specialist school setting before he became an apprentice.

When she first met him, Hall remembers he was very quiet. But he successfully completed his apprenticeship and went on to work for the NHS.

Another of the apprentices she supported had been a school refuser and hadn’t sat an exam. While undergoing his apprenticeship, he managed to get the equivalent of Level 4 in his GCSE Maths.

“It just makes you so proud,” says Hall. “It’s what gets you out of bed.”

Webb adds: “We see a lot of young people who feel forgotten, like the system has failed them every which way. Individuals have failed them and there’s nothing for them. They come in with huge anxiety, low self-worth, no belief that their future can be any better. This is a pattern we see again and again. 

“We watch them bloom into this beautiful, confident person who knows they can succeed in life, and they go out the door 15 months after they come in, ready to take on the world.”
