Venice Film Festival kicks off with Danny Boyle’s raucous Rupert Murdoch drama

TheTools · 04.09.2026 17:27 · source · permalink

With superb performances from Jack O’Connell and Guy Pearce, Ink examines what the tabloids’ excesses reveal about our national id

4/5

Is there a better surveyor of the modern British psyche working in film today than Danny Boyle? Barely a year after his 28 Years Later grappled with the country’s ongoing hangover from the Thatcher, Blair and Brexit eras through the medium of the zombie apocalypse, with Ink he burrows into our national id from another angle. Slickly adapted by James Graham from his 2017 play, it is a raucous and riveting dramatisation of the first year at the Sun newspaper under Rupert Murdoch – and also Larry Lamb, the editor Murdoch poached from the Daily Mail in 1969 to oversee the formerly staid tabloid’s circulation-spiking lurch downmarket.

Jack O’Connell is superb as the bluff, chippy, ambitious Yorkshire-born Lamb, whose passion for popular journalism steadily warps into a desire to win the circulation war, and thereby best the establishment, at any cost. And Guy Pearce impressively transcends pantomime villainy as his dynamic patron and enabler, who as an Australian outsider can’t quite anticipate the sheer scale of the cultural shift his red-top is about to foment. (In this very male world, Claire Foy provides invaluable balance as Jules Davies, the paper’s fictional women’s editor and Lamb’s mistress.)

In a recurring dream sequence, Boyle shows Lamb and Murdoch sitting opposite one another on an otherwise deserted London Underground train – the general ambience of which, as it thunders deeper into the dark, takes on an increasingly Stygian pall. Every copy that flies off the newsstands is crammed with “birds”, puns, telly, footie, scandal and freebies. But are they jointly responsible for the coarsening of British public life, or is Britain itself spurring them on? (The introduction of topless Page 3 models is cannily positioned as the climactic gamble on how much the Sun’s readership will stand for – or lap up.) Yet Graham’s script is less keen to apportion personal blame than you might think. All it says is that having climbed on board a runaway train, both men are now fated to see the journey through to its infernal end.

One senses that Boyle takes a more straightforwardly dim view of the tabloid press’s excesses, with a frenzied closing montage that positions Murdoch’s Sun as the decisive breach in the dam that would lead to the ascent of Fox News and, later, Elon Musk and and Bonnie Blue. But Ink also affirms Boyle as a populist by nature. When old hands complain about Larry’s fondness for using italics to hammer home a point, it is hard not to think of the equally slanted camera angles Boyle deploys to crank up the excitement during conversations in grotty offices and printworks.

He savours the swashbuckling romance of it all, too. In its own down-at-heel, whisky-breathed, nicotine-fingertipped way, Ink might be Britain’s belated answer to Mad Men, in which a nation’s self-image is lucratively remade in an office by men with a hotline to their customers’ innermost selves, and entire racks of axes to grind.

Screening at the Venice Film Festival. In UK cinemas from January 8
