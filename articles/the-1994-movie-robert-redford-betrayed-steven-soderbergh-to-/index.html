The 1994 movie Robert Redford betrayed Steven Soderbergh to make

TheTools · 17.09.2026 20:45 · source · permalink

The professional relationship between Robert Redford and Steven Soderbergh began as one that was mutually beneficial to both parties, but several years later, it had been shattered completely.

Soderbergh’s feature-length debut, Sex, Lies and Videotape, had premiered at the 1989 edition of Sundance, winning widespread acclaim, putting the first-timer’s name on the map, and ultimately winning the illustrious Palme d’Or at Cannes, laying the groundwork for the career that followed.

As for Redford, he called the low-budget independent picture the most important in the history of the festival that he created, with the success enjoyed by Soderbergh’s maiden film becoming a watershed moment for Sundance, illustrating that premiering there could lead to the very top of the industry.

That established something of a mentor/mentee relationship between the pair, and when Soderbergh signalled his interest in adapting AE Hotchner’s novel, King of the Hill, for the screen, Redford personally stepped in to help secure the rights to the book, while also attaching himself as an executive producer.

However, by the time the coming-of-age drama reached the screen in August 1993, his name was nowhere to be found in the credits. Before shooting began, Redford summoned Soderbergh for a meeting, outlining his concerns that he’d been discussing the project with Sydney Pollack.

“I found that sort of odd,” the Ocean’s helmer recalled. “Since I was the one who brought the material to Wildwood [Redford’s production company], and I had every intention of making it. He felt as though something was amiss, but he put it in the back of his mind until he was sent a screenplay written by Paul Attanasio called Quiz Show, detailing a real-life TV fixing scandal of the 1950s.

The day after reading the script, he called producer Mark Johnson, agreeing to direct. After that, things went silent for six months, until he got a second phone call, informing him that he was no longer the director of Quiz Show, because Redford had swooped in and taken the job for himself, which took him completely by surprise, especially when he didn’t hear it first-hand.

“It’s a call I would have made to someone,” Soderbergh suggested. “It’s a call most people, I think, would have made. The image that is given, as being a friend of the filmmaker, is not what I experienced.” It would be another six months until he finally spoke to Redford, but he didn’t want to talk about Quiz Show.

Instead, he wanted to see King of the Hill. “He saw the movie, and I have never heard from him to this day,” Soderbergh revealed in 1997. Redford claimed that he didn’t feel the need to reach out to Soderbergh about stealing Quiz Show away from him because he was told that Johnson would take care of it, but the whole thing sounds a little shifty, and it’s easy to see why the pair drifted apart.
