The troubling trend hiding in plain sight across the arts sector in Britain

TheTools · 21.09.2026 20:52 · source · permalink

Back in 2015 Jack Rooke brought his debut show Good Grief to the Edinburgh Fringe. Based on his own life as the son of a Watford cab driver, it was a scrappy little thing containing slightly amateurish videos. But the story of love and loss it told - Rooke’s dad had passed away when Rooke was just 15 - was sincere, honest and, most importantly, very, very funny. And though it played to audiences of less than 50 each night it got him noticed.

Another show followed in 2017, by which time he was being commissioned by BBC Three to make Happy Man, a series about identity, mental health and depression among young men. He then created the highly acclaimed Channel 4 sitcom Big Boys which ran for three series. It won Rooke a Bafta. He has since been part of the writing team working on the new BBC adaptation of Sue Townsend’s much-loved novel The Secret Diary of Adrian Mole Aged 13 and 3/4.

So, if anyone knows what the Edinburgh Fringe can do for one’s career it’s Rooke.

So when he brought Good Grief back to Edinburgh for a limited run last month it should have been cause for celebration. And it was. (It was also, by the by, one of the best things I saw at the Fringe this year.) But at the end of the show Rooke came to the front of the stage, stood beside the show’s notable prop - a coffin - and told his audience, “in all honesty I believe in the landscape of today Good Grief wouldn’t exist.

“I would not have been able to have made it without the arts funding and opportunities that have now been cut and vanished,” he continued.

Jack Rooke (Image: Jack Rooke)

“And without Good Grief there wouldn’t be Big Boys. Even Big Boys was funded by a government fund called The Young Audiences Content Fund. That got cut the month after we came out.”

In short, he said, we are in a moment where working-class voices are being priced out - of both the Fringe and TV - and working-class stories are disappearing as a result.

“It really feels like we’ve lost a whole new generation of comedians and artists who just can’t afford to do it,” Rooke lamented. “There’s way less scrappy little weirdos like the guy who dragged this coffin down Cowgate.” It’s an argument he elaborates on when I speak to him a couple of weeks after his short Edinburgh run. He is bringing Good Grief to Glasgow next week, something he’s hugely excited about. But returning to the Fringe this year felt a bit depressing, he tells me. Back in 2015, he recalls, it still felt as if the Fringe offered a possible route to a career in the creative industries, supported by various funding sources and good will.

“But the budget for my show was two or three grand, whereas nowadays I’m hearing of Edinburgh solo shows people are spending 40, 50 grand on. There’s one show this year - I heard a rumour - that had 200 K invested in. I thought, ‘Oh my God that’s like a house.’”

“It’s become such a financial burden to go to the Fringe.”

Well, he added, maybe not for everyone. “I saw so many people performing whose parents are really well connected, or whose parents are just rich and able to bankroll it. And I saw the general industry flock to those shows.

“A lot of the industry nowadays is middle class. Actually, I’d say a fair proportion are from an already culturally established family background, or are constantly being bankrolled to come back to the Fringe year after year in this kind of chase and pursuit of the dream.

“I don’t want to name names, but it’s clear if you look at the programme. I think that’s a bit depressing. “And some of them make amazing work and some of them make rubbish work, but will be there next year because they’ve got the Bank of Mum and Dad to keep bankrolling them in the pursuit of the dream. And I think that’s the disparity that I find sad.

“Because there are often a lot of artists who are juggling jobs and have saved up for years to get to the Fringe and you do really do get one shot. More privileged artists are allowed to fail a couple of times. “It’s a shame that that creative process can’t always be afforded to others. And the Fringe has now become such a financial risk that it’s hard to reason with yourself as to why you would take that risk again if you didn’t smash it out of the park the first time, so to speak.”

For Rooke the state of the Fringe is the canary in the mine for the rest of the creative industries. What is happening in Edinburgh is also happening throughout TV, film, theatre and comedy more generally.

The figures back him up. Last year’s Sutton Trust “Elitist Britain” report into the creative industries found that less than 10 per cent of creative workers born after 1990 come from working-class backgrounds. The Trust has also reported that young people from a working-class background are four times less likely to work in the creative industries than their middle-class contemporaries.

And Rooke was not the only one to have had something to say about this issue at Edinburgh last month. A few days before Rooke took to the stage in Edinburgh the historian David Olusoga was onstage at the Edinburgh International Book Festival pointing out the failures of the television industry “to represent the country in terms of who gets to make programmes and who gets to decide whose stories get told.”

Olusoga who was raised by a single mum on a council estate in Gateshead and is now one of the main faces of TV history in the UK thanks to the success of A House in Time is in some ways a poster boy for diversity in the sector. But, he told his Book Festival audience, the way the TV industry operates actively militates against working-class kids.

Star Penders (Image: Star Penders)

“In some ways the creative sectors - the arts, journalism, museums, curation, art galleries, the film industry, the TV industry - have some of the highest barriers of entry to kids from working class backgrounds of any industry,” Olusoga suggested.. “And these are industries full of liberal, progressive people who talk a good talk about inclusion and diversity in all its forms.”

He pointed to the traditional route into television, internship - “what we used to call work experience”. What did that amount to, he asked? “Go and work in London for six months with no payment for your work, no support for your astronomical rent. And if you can do that for six months you might get a job with a three-month contract.

“If you were going to design a system to say, ‘This is only for rich kids,’ you would come up with something quite similar to the system we have in the industry,” Olusoga argued.

The result? Industries full of people with the right contacts (we live in the era of the nepo baby) and the right resources to sustain the dog work required to make yourself known.

“Of course there are a million and one things going on,” Rooke adds. But look at who the industry is backing and pushing, he suggests. “It does feel a bit more homogenous.

“Because you can’t see class I think a lot of producers or commissioners or broadcasters or even theatre don't see how bad it’s got. They don’t see what is missing.”

And if fewer people from working-class backgrounds are entering the industry it’s almost an inevitable corollary that fewer working-class stories will get told.

In part this is a reflection of a TV industry that is being squeezed financially - the BBC is a corporation shrinking visibly every day - and being pulled in new directions by developing technologies. Perhaps inevitably it is looking for sure things.

In this age of streaming, glamour is in. As Rooke points out, “we’ve been living through an era of TV about really rich people being really awful.” Indeed. Think Succession. Think White Lies. It’s understandable. If you want to reach an international audience, posh bad people are an easier sell. Do you want to watch something set on the Amalfi coast or in Watford? Or Wishaw for that matter? We also live in an algorithmic age where one success means we inevitably get offered more and more of the same. As a result there are fewer chances being taken, fewer “weirdos”, as Rooke would have it, breaking through.

And yet when chances are taken the results can be spectacular. Rooke cites Lisa McGee’s Northern Irish sitcom Derry Girls as a prime example.

“She is showing Northern Ireland in a completely different light and she’s also making it feel really authentic,” he says of McGee. “And everyone in Northern Ireland is watching it and engaging with it. That’s what TV should be.”

You could say the same of Rooke’s own sitcom Big Boys, which like Good Grief, examined working-class family life, male identity and grief. That’s why it hit so hard. But if he’s honest Rooke didn’t think it always got the backing it should have from Channel 4.

“Big Boys couldn’t have had better reviews and we still didn’t really have a marketing campaign for the final series. And I think those disparities are frustrating because, as a writer trying to write a show about predominantly working-class people, when you see that it isn’t getting any marketing, but other shows are … There’s a lot of I think disparities that people just aren’t even aware of.”

In short, an industry that is defined and reflects the experiences of those who run it struggles to see working-class stories as anything other than the stuff of soap operas and crime dramas, you could argue.

What is the trickle-down effect of all of this? Let’s ask someone who knows. Star Penders graduated from the Royal Conservatoire in Glasgow in 2023. Originally from Largs, she is now a working actor who has gained experience on both sides of the Atlantic. She will soon be seen in Scott Gilmour and Claire McKenzie’s new musical CEILIDH at Pitlochry Theatre. “I didn’t know it was a possibility to go into the arts until I was going into my final year of school,” Penders admits. “I didn’t think a young person from Largs could be an actor.”

That’s an issue, she suggests. “There isn’t enough support in schools to nurture and encourage young people to do something creative as a career choice because there’s a huge lack of funding and also knowledge of what that career path could look like, so it’s ignored and challenged.”

A Derry Girls mural in Northern Ireland (Image: James McAnaney)

Penders was fortunate enough to be offered a place at the Conservatoire with the help of the Transitions 20/40 programme (now called Fair Access), ending up on the BA Musical Theatre course. Free tuition fees helped. “Attempting to go to a drama school in London would have been impossible.”

But even now that she has a foothold in her chosen profession, it’s not straightforward.

“When I’m working on a theatre job, I’m often still having to work in a restaurant or pub or teaching alongside it to keep myself afloat.

“I’ve had to move back home a few times and commute to and from Glasgow because trying to afford rent and a stable life is extremely difficult when in between creative jobs.”

In her short time in the industry she has been asked to tone down her accent and not been considered for some jobs because she doesn’t have a base in London. “I do think this industry can be elitist,” she says plainly.

And yet it’s important that people should be able to see themselves reflected in the stories we tell.

“I think it’s extremely important to share these stories, but they need to be handled with care - sometimes we are made to look as if we are all sad and down about life just because we come from a certain place, or have a certain financial standing, or a different background and upbringing. It’s brilliant being working class. Getting stuck into work and learning trades, having community and neighbours who you lend a hand to or owe favours. You really learn a lot about yourself and others when things aren’t just handed to you.” What role do the arts have in 2026? We live in an era of cuts, where the arts are constantly targeted - in schools and in wider society - because they are seen as a luxury. And yet at the same time we live in a culture obsessed with stories. They are fed to us every day on screens big and small. Some of those small screens may ultimately be helpful in allowing new working-class voices to flourish. There may be opportunities via TikTok and YouTube to circumvent the barriers to access. Rooke points to the example of comedian Al Nash who started off making funny shorts online and is now part of the Saturday Night Live team in the UK.

Even so, I keep thinking of something Rooke said at the end of Good Grief in Edinburgh. “The more we cut arts subjects in only state schools and the more it’s only well-off artists that can fail a few times compared to those who are juggling jobs and trying to make work, and the more arts funding vanishes bit by bit by bit, the more it all becomes a game for those who can afford to play.”

Who gets to come to the Fringe? Who gets to appear on our stages and our screens? Who gets to tell their stories? Who gets to play? And maybe more importantly, do we care?

Jack Rooke performs his show Good Grief at Oran Mor, Glasgow, on September 30.

CEIDILH opens at Pitlochry Theatre on October 7 and runs until October 17 before going on tour.
