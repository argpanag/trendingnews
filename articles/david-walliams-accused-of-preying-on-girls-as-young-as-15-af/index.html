  1. Were social media users free to identify Walliams as the anonymous celebrity?published at 16:33 GMT
    16:33 GMT

    Dominic Casciani
    Home and legal correspondent

    There is a massive practical issue concerning how far the writ of English law runs in cyber space.

    One judge I recently addressed over a reporting restriction issue described social media as a "wild west" - which just underlines the concern among legal professionals.

    When the courts in the UK order that someone should remain anonymous, that order applies to all forms of publishing and broadcasting. It matters not whether the publication is in a newspaper or online - it is still publication.

    But the question is: how to enforce that? If a newspaper were to breach an order, the nature of a bona fide large media organisation’s audience means that publication is seen by judges to have a wide impact.

    One random user of X publishing the suspected identity of an anonymised person is obviously less impactful. But that does not take into account the overall effect of thousands of people doing the same.

    The Walliams case was complicated because his name and the media organisations he was seeking to gag were briefly published on a register of live cases when the case was filed. That register is not directly public - but the name somehow got out. Court officials later deleted it, as per other anonymity cases.

    But that incident made some people think that they were free to reveal the identity of a seriously unwell man when, unless they were either lawyers or journalists trained in how reporting restrictions work, they may not have had a clue about the rules of anonymity.

  4. Did David Walliams have a right to appeal against court's decision?published at 15:06 GMT
    15:06 GMT

    Dominic Casciani
    Home and legal correspondent

    Yes - but he did not exercise it.

    When the High Court refused him his forever injunction - the judge naturally turned to the question of whether Walliams could challenge his ruling in the Court of Appeal.

    That higher court doesn’t exist to give a claimant another go if they don’t agree with the outcome: there has to be a proper legal reason to appeal, such as if the judge made a serious mistake or had not followed a fair process.

    Walliams’ legal team couldn’t give Mr Justice Nicklin last week an indication of what those grounds would be, saying in court that they had not been able to take instructions from their client because he was in hospital.

    The judge, in fairness to Walliams, decided to grant him permission to appeal in principle - but on the proviso that he filed a proper challenge by 16:30 GMT yesterday.

    That appeal - or alternatively a request for more time - was not filed. The BBC and others learned yesterday afternoon that Walliams had not filed his appeal.

    So why didn’t we reveal his name?

    The High Court had earlier ordered that if the deadline was passed with nothing from Walliams, ITN and the Guardian were free to publish - if they chose to do so - and only if they did go with their story would the rest of the media be able to identify that the comedian and author who had been fighting to remain anonymous.

  14. Walliams' lawyers tried to block publication on mental health groundspublished at 13:12 GMT
    13:12 GMT

    Dominic Casciani
    Home and legal correspondent

    David Walliams has been named because he had no legally sound reason to prevent the Guardian and ITN (which produces news for ITV) from disclosing their allegations, which he denies.

    His lawyers wanted the two organisations to be permanently gagged from running their story. They argued that if they were not stopped from running the claims, it would cause irreparable harm to Walliams’ mental health, potentially leading to him trying to take his own life.

    That, they argued, would be a breach of the state’s duty, under the European Convention on Human Rights, to protect his right to life.

    Under that law, public bodies have a duty to take reasonable steps to try to preserve life, where they can do so.

    There’s no expectation that a state agency can save all lives in all circumstances - but it must show it has done its best if it’s clear it had a responsibility to act. And this is why Walliams lost.

    The responsibility to protect his life and help him get mentally better is on clinicians, not the court or the media.

    The bid to use human rights law to gag the media was exceptionally novel. Critically, the court did not say the allegations were true.

    That neutral position means Walliams, like anyone else who may feel unjustly maligned in the media, retains his right to try to sue for defamation or breach of privacy.

Walliams' lawyers tried to block publication on mental health groundspublished at 13:12 GMT
13:12 GMT

Dominic Casciani
Home and legal correspondent

David Walliams has been named because he had no legally sound reason to prevent the Guardian and ITN (which produces news for ITV) from disclosing their allegations, which he denies.

His lawyers wanted the two organisations to be permanently gagged from running their story. They argued that if they were not stopped from running the claims, it would cause irreparable harm to Walliams’ mental health, potentially leading to him trying to take his own life.

That, they argued, would be a breach of the state’s duty, under the European Convention on Human Rights, to protect his right to life.

Under that law, public bodies have a duty to take reasonable steps to try to preserve life, where they can do so.

There’s no expectation that a state agency can save all lives in all circumstances - but it must show it has done its best if it’s clear it had a responsibility to act. And this is why Walliams lost.

The responsibility to protect his life and help him get mentally better is on clinicians, not the court or the media.

The bid to use human rights law to gag the media was exceptionally novel. Critically, the court did not say the allegations were true.

That neutral position means Walliams, like anyone else who may feel unjustly maligned in the media, retains his right to try to sue for defamation or breach of privacy.
