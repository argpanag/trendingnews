'I'm hit with a feeling of self-healing': The moment I realised GTA 6 was one of the greatest games ever

TheTools · 23.11.2026 00:06 · source · permalink

Grand Theft Auto 6 has been hailed for the awe-inspiring scale and detail of its fictional world. Here one writer explains how an underwater adventure convinced him it was a masterpiece

Immersed in rich midnight blue colours, and swimming in time to soothing synth music that mirrors the mating calls of whales, my first transcendent moment in Grand Theft Auto 6 occurred as I scuba-dived toward the murky depths of the ocean floor. 

I've never been a good swimmer – and because the aquatic details here (corals, fish, great white sharks with pitch black eyes) are so intricately realised, I feel like I'm living vicariously through the co-lead character Jason's precise downward strokes. 

A truly magical moment occurs when I decide to finally stand still and, pardon the pun, soak it all in. Suddenly a smiling dolphin with a playful spirit emerges and nudges its head against mine, both curiously and tenderly. It whistles as it disappears off into a fog of never-ending cerulean, and I'm hit with a feeling of self-healing that takes me a million miles away from my 9-to-5 hustle. I finally understand why Grand Theft Auto 6 – released this week – is one of the greatest video games ever created.

Much has already been written about the sheer scope of Grand Theft Auto 6 – a game that cost more than $1bn (£763m) and took over 10 years to make – and how its open-world depiction of the fictional US state of Leonida, including the Miami-like Vice City, is so immersive it is difficult to differentiate the virtual world from reality. 

Naysayers continue to claim that, GTA's clever satire of Floridian excess aside, having a game with visceral extreme scenes of violence will result in real-world repercussions. The debate around whether violent games themselves cause violence has been going since the early 2000s – a 2015 report by the American Psychological Association suggested they could contribute to increased aggression, though many other studies have found no evidence to support this. 

Yet most critics and gamers seem, rather, to be in collective awe at the astonishing scope of GTA 6's beautiful, pink-hued vistas. This is a game where exploration leads to bumping into everyone from crooked cops –who you can film on your in-game smartphone, as they harass other motorists – to the mythical creature Big Foot. 

As you play, at various junctures, Jason and his partner-in-crime Lucia learn how to maintain a loving adult relationship. In the process of developing their domesticated Bonnie-and-Clyde dynamic, relatable side-quests include fixing leaking roofs, buying flowers and remembering to show up to a couples' bank appointment on time. 

From the airport to the open sea, each little detail in this game has been meticulously crafted by specialist teams at its British American developers Rockstar. Therefore tonally it feels like each new experience was made by a team competing to stand out over the other departments, making their little corner of this world sing just that little bit more loudly. And, beyond GTA 6's glitz and gore, I believe it is the little moments – like going for a swim – that will leave the deepest impression on gamers. 

The game's meditative power

In a contemporary landscape where many people are manically reacting in real time to pings from their smartphone, it feels like GTA 6 has been carefully engineered by Rockstar to try to help gamers slow down and take in their surroundings a little more – a skill they might then hopefully bring into their real lives. The inclusion of a sub-mission where you learn how to do transcendental meditation, for example, doesn't feel like a coincidence.

Back to my special moment out-at-sea. Despite being 15 hours into the game, as Jason, I've let my relationship with Lucia go to hell. I am completely ignoring her irritated "where are you?" text messages. Instead, I am working with an eccentric Rastafarian sea captain called Kenton to find abandoned pirate treasure, buried deep across the local waters. As I burn through fuel on Kenton's party boat, the gloriously upbeat sounds of Cliff Richard's Devil Woman hit my ears like a cushion. I swear I can feel the breeze, and even taste the saltiness of the water.

Before long, Kenton's tasks are all completed – but my cosy exploration of the ocean floor has only just begun. I am now moving through the sea purely in the hope of encountering something extraordinary.  

Previous GTA games – the immigrant tale of GTA 4 aside – tended to prioritise chaos and reckless abandon, but GTA 6 hopes you will become empathetic, drive slower, and even believe in love. Standing out from all the previous action-adventure game epics released under the Rockstar umbrella, GTA 6's true genealogy is more in line with 1999's Shenmue – a kooky role-playing game on the Sega Dreamcast that found joy in the everyday, from walking around a lake, to stacking boxes in a factory. 

GTA 6, like Shenmue, inspires you to stand still and notice all of the world's weird little details unfurl.

Gliding more and more through the calm waters, I became convinced I was seeing things flying in the distance, speeding through the fishes and moving upwards. I remember a previous in-game chat in GTA 6 where a throwaway character spoke about the Ancient Aliens conspiracy theory of UFOs using the sea floor to navigate quicker through our world – could this all be connected? Probably. 

While the lush fictional world of Leonida teems with life above me, it's clear I care much more about what's going on down here. Sorry Lucia, but my heart belongs to the mystery of the waves. Don't wait up for me tonight; I'm too busy taking it all in.
