14-year-old beaten to death by Iranian husband days after giving birth

TheTools · 27.11.2026 12:43 · source · permalink

A 14-year-old Iranian girl was beaten to death by her husband days after giving birth, campaigners have revealed.

Sogand Moshtareki, from the country's Kerman province, was killed on November 6 after she had asked her 20-year-old husband if she could visit her family. 

The teenager, who had given birth ten days earlier, had been forced into marriage at the age of 13. 

Sogand's husband confessed to her murder and said the motive was 'family disputes', according to the National Council of Resistance of Iran (NCRI)'s Women Committee. 

The schoolgirl had recently survived a traffic accident and was still recovering when she gave birth. 

The Iran Human Rights (IHR) organisation said they had learned from Sogand's father that her husband had restricted her contact with family and would lock her inside the house when he went out. 

Her husband is understood to have been arrested, according to IHR. 

Sogand's death came on the same day as the murder of Sepideh Raeisi, a 21-year-old pregnant woman from Fars province.

Like Sogand, Sepideh was also forced into marriage and was killed at the hands of her husband. 

Sepideh, who had a three-year-old child and was four months pregnant, was shot by her husband after attempting to pursue a divorce. 

Her unborn child did not survive, while her mother was injured. 

According to the NCRI, Sepideh got married at the age of 13 and had endured years of domestic abuse. 

The two recent murders of young Iranian women shed light on the dangers of child marriage in the Islamic republic. 

According to the NCRI, girls forced into marriage are 'denied their rights to education, personal development, and free choice.'

Many, it says, become vulnerable to domestic violence, early pregnancy, economic dependency, and, in some cases, 'lethal violence'.

Their murders also come after a recent NCRI report found that in October 2026 alone, 24 cases of femicide had been reported in Iran. Most of the victims were killed in their own homes by men known to them. 

However, the figure could be much higher, as many cases of femicide in Iran go unreported. 

Separately, UK-based Persian-language broadcaster Iran International reported that at least 84 women and girls were killed in Iran between March 21 and October 13.

In 75 cases, the perpetrators were known to the victims, and eight of the under-age murdered girls were killed by fathers, brothers or other blood relatives.

There were at least 207 femicides across Iran in 2025, including 31 involving suspected so-called 'honour' motives, according to Hengaw.

Of these, at least 13 women were killed after rejecting marriage proposals, ten after seeking divorce, and ten were victims of child marriage, killed by their husbands.

In a separate recent case, a pregnant woman was murdered by her parents in Iran in a case of femicide motivated by so-called 'honour'.

Nineteen-year-old Helia Fattahi was two months pregnant following a relationship with a young man and had disclosed her pregnancy to her family last week.

Her parents, Voria Fattahi and Leila Veisi, went to court on Saturday to file a complaint against her.

On returning home, they allegedly forced her to swallow approximately 30 anticonvulsant pills and severely beat her, according to the Hengaw Organisation for Human Rights.

When they realised she was still alive, the parents suffocated her with a pillow, the human rights group said.

Sources added that they both confessed to killing their daughter when emergency medical personnel arrived at the family home.

Police arrested them later that day in the western Iranian city of Sanandaj.

The police said the parents told them the motive was 'family disputes'.

On November 18, Sadaf Haghbin was shot dead at her birthday party in Gilan province, in the north.

Local media said a former boyfriend was the gunman, and the Khabaronline news site said the likely motive was the 23-year-old's refusal to marry him.

On November 15, the body of Sogand Sayyadi was discovered in the courtyard of her residential building in Kermanshah, western Iran.

Her death occurred a day after administrators at Razvan Girls' High School allegedly summoned her father upon learning that she had been in contact with a prospective suitor, whom he opposed.

The 16-year-old student had warned her high school the day before that if she went home with her father, he would hurt or even kill her.

In front of classmates and school staff, Kioumars Sayyadi severely beat his daughter, Hengaw claimed.

He allegedly continued the assault outside the school, repeatedly slamming her head against a wall.

Hengaw says Ms Sayyadi's father murdered her in a so-called 'honour' killing the next day, citing sources who said he threw her from the fifth floor of the apartment building where the family lived.

Ms Sayyadi's father was detained on suspicion of assault and murder, while her school principal was arrested on suspicion of negligence, the judiciary-affiliated Mizan news agency reported, citing provincial chief prosecutor Hamidreza Karimi.

The Islamic Penal Code does not specifically criminalise 'honour' crimes and offers leniency towards perpetrators of honour killings.

Under Article 301 of Iran's Islamic Penal Code, fathers and paternal grandfathers are exempt from qisas – or retaliatory execution – for killing their children.

Most honour killings are punishable by three to ten years' imprisonment, while non-honour-related killings are punishable by the death penalty.

Estimates on the number of 'honour' killings vary, with some sources stating that at least 450 'honour' killings occur each year.
