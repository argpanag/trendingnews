Opinion | Sydney Sweeney’s sports betting ad shows that controversy sells

TheTools · 16.09.2026 10:00 · source · permalink

For the second time in just over a year, actor Sydney Sweeney is at the center of an advertising controversy that illuminates lurking realities of American culture. This time, Sweeney posed nearly nude, strategically covering her body with various sports equipment, for sports predication market platform Novig.

At one point, while wearing soccer cleats and little else, Sweeney looks at the camera and says, “No betting on wars or deaths and no politics. Just you, your takes and sports.”

No politics, eh?

I find it hard to argue that politics have no bearing on an intentionally provocative ad for a prediction market platform featuring a blatantly sexualized woman during a time when women’s reproductive rights are under attack.

Rage bait works. Hot takes work. Spectacle works. Algorithms reward engagement.

In what appears to be a response to the considerable backlash to the ad, including post after post from Olympic and collegiate athletes showing “what a woman in sports looks like,” Sweeney posted a series of photos from ESPN Magazine’s “The Body Issue.” Discontinued since 2019, the long-running issue featured athletes nude or partially nude, showcasing the power, strength and physicality required for athletic excellence. What Sweeney conveyed in the advertisement is wholly different, namely because she is not, nor is she claiming to be, an athlete of any kind.

Sweeney’s previous marketing firestorm — a campaign for American Eagle — became so polarizing and so contentious that I got death threats for writing about it.

Then, among other things, my column posited that advertisements, like Sweeney for American Eagle and Kendall Jenner for Pepsi, reflect what is pervasive in American culture at that time. And while I maintain that that is true, what is perhaps more interesting is what these advertisements suggest about making money today: If attention cannot be guaranteed, then controversy should do the trick.

Entirely unsurprising, Sweeney is not just the face of Novig’s campaign, but she is also an equity holder in the company. It makes logical sense that she might want Novig to get the American Eagle treatment, too. Anecdotal evidence, from her American Eagle ad to marketing her own bathwater to her own lingerie line, suggests that Sweeney is mightily aware that controversy, outrage and think pieces — like this one — can equate to money. A search for Novig’s financial gains in the wake of the advertisement can be quantified in social media traffic: Novig’s social media mentions grew nine times the original number with Sweeney’s campaign.

Rage bait works. Hot takes work. Spectacle works. Algorithms reward engagement, not morality, or kindness, or community or human decency.
