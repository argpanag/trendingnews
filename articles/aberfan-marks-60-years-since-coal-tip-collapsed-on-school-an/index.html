Aberfan marks 60 years since coal tip collapsed on school and homes, killing 116 children and 28 adults

TheTools · 21.10.2026 19:21 · source · permalink

Summary

  • People in Aberfan, south Wales, remember the disaster 60 years ago when a coal waste tip collapsed on a school and homes

  • On 21 October 1966, 116 children and 28 adults were killed when the colliery spoil tip collapsed and engulfed Pantglas Junior School and surrounding houses

  • Mair Morgan, the last surviving teacher at the school, still remembers having to identify the children who died

  • An official inquiry found the National Coal Board was responsible for the disaster, but no-one has ever been prosecuted

  • On the anniversary, King Charles, Queen Camilla and the Prince of Wales laid wreaths at a memorial garden that stands on the site of the school, and joined survivors, bereaved parents and other members of the community

  • In a speech, the King described how "very special" it is to be back in Aberfan, saying "today in our nation and across the world, thoughts will turn to you in in Aberfan"

  • A prayer and moment of silence was held at 09:15 to mark the moment the coal spoil struck the village

  • Warning: This live page will contain upsetting details that some may find distressing

People in Aberfan, south Wales, remember the disaster 60 years ago when a coal waste tip collapsed on a school and homes

On 21 October 1966, 116 children and 28 adults were killed when the colliery spoil tip collapsed and engulfed Pantglas Junior School and surrounding houses

Mair Morgan, the last surviving teacher at the school, still remembers having to identify the children who died

An official inquiry found the National Coal Board was responsible for the disaster, but no-one has ever been prosecuted

On the anniversary, King Charles, Queen Camilla and the Prince of Wales laid wreaths at a memorial garden that stands on the site of the school, and joined survivors, bereaved parents and other members of the community

In a speech, the King described how "very special" it is to be back in Aberfan, saying "today in our nation and across the world, thoughts will turn to you in in Aberfan"

A prayer and moment of silence was held at 09:15 to mark the moment the coal spoil struck the village

    Members of the Royal Family have been in Aberfan, south Wales, remembering the disaster 60 years ago when a coal waste tip collapsed on a school and homes.

    The King, Queen and Prince William laid their wreaths in the memorial garden alongside Mair Morgan, the last surviving teacher, and First Minister Rhun ap Iorwerth also paid tribute to the 116 children and 28 adults that were killed.

    Other key moments from today include:

    • Hundreds of people gathered on the street near where Pantglas School once stood
    • A minute's silence was held at 09:15 - the approximate time the disaster happened
    • The Royal family spoke to the crowd after making their way to Aberfan by helicopter just after midday.
    • King Charles was then taken to the community centre where he met some of the survivors and described how "very special" it was to be back in Aberfan

    15:53 BST

    Ray Lee described being very close to his eight-year-old daughter Anne, who would comb his hair.

    They had breakfast, and she left for school.

    After the disaster, he walked the streets until late, and still had hopes she was alive until he saw her in the mortuary.

    "It's hard to think about now," he said.

    "I'd have liked to have known her that big longer, because eight years went very quickly."

  4. 'You never forget it... it's always with you'published at 15:44 BST
    15:44 BST

    Jordan Davies
    BBC News, reporting from Aberfan

    Brenig Parker, now 74, was 14 when he narrowly avoided the coal tip collapse, but two of his friends died.

    “I only missed it because I walked into the senior school, it must have been coming towards me as I was going in," he says.

    “I just heard a rumble, I turned around and the doorway behind me was full of stone. There was a tree in a classroom - the sewing room."

    He says his two friends now rest in the cemetery.

    “I lost friends that day - I haven’t been in Aberfan for quite a few years."

    With a tear in his eye, Brenig tells me: "You never forget it, I can’t explain it... it’s always with you."

  5. 'My siblings and friends died... and life changed forever'published at 15:40 BST
    15:40 BST

    Gaynor Madgwick, who was eight, described hearing a tremendous sound like a hurricane, before a black mass crashed into the classroom.

    She woke up unable to see her legs, and was half-buried along with other pupils, some alive, some who had died.

    Gaynor remembers crying as her grandad smashed the window to rescue her, before her parents hugged her, and then ran off to find her siblings.

    "I was told Carl and Marylyn [her brother and sister] had died, and all my friends had gone as well," she said.

    "My life changed forever."

    15:36 BST

    The Central Beacons Mountain Rescue Team has paid tribute to those affected by the tragedy which "hit hard upon our team's hometown and neighbouring valley communities".

    "Soon after 09:15 our team members responded alongside other rescue agencies, coal miners and members of the public, to assist the rescue and recovery effort at the Aberfan disaster," it said.

    A small group of team members joined the memorial service in Aberfan.

    "As on this day every year, our thoughts continue to be with all those involved and affected," it added.

  7. Schoolboy tells survivor 'my great-grandad saved your life'published at 15:31 BST
    15:31 BST

    Gareth Jones was talking to children at Troedyrhiw Primary about how he survived the Aberfan disaster when a pupil put his hand up and showed him a photo.

    It was of 10-year-old Noah's great-grandad Stephen Andrew who had pulled Gareth from the rubble.

    He had always known the school caretaker saved him, pulling him to safety through a window, but he had never seen a photograph of him before.

    "I was absolutely gobsmacked... that a bit of the jigsaw has come together," said Gareth.

    Noah had grown up hearing stories about Stephen's heroics that day, after he had lost two of his own sons in the disaster.

  8. 'I almost missed school sick - but was determined to go in'published at 15:23 BST
    15:23 BST

    After suffering from pneumonia, Janett Bickley almost stayed home sick, but was determined to go in for the last day before half term.

    She remembers seeing smoke in the distance getting closer and closer to the window, and instinctively put her head on her desk with her book over it.

    When she awoke, Janett was covered in soil and muck, and remembers sucking in water when she tried to breathe.

    A classmate next to her had died, but another was walking around and helped dig her out.

    She remembers children gathering in the hallway, waiting to be pulled out of a window by a teacher, and one little boy not moving, just standing there stunned.

    "I was pulled out and never knew what happened to him," she said.

    "Then I found out years later he'd died of a heart attack before being rescued."

  10. The portraits that helped bring the children of Aberfan homepublished at 15:13 BST
    15:13 BST

    For almost 60 years, many of Aberfan's lost children have watched over living rooms, hallways and family gatherings across the village.

    Some were painted from faded school photographs. Others were shaped by the memories of grieving parents, who wrote letters to an artist describing the children they had lost - the colour of their eyes, their favourite clothes, the flashes of personality that made them unique.

    The portraits arrived in the years after one of Wales' darkest days. And for many families, they became far more than paintings.

    In a new BBC programme, Unseen: The Aberfan Portraits, some have shared their paintings and spoken about how the art helped them grieve.

    Unseen: The Aberfan Portraits is available on BBC iPlayer

  11. Fire engine that helped in tragedy returns to villagepublished at 15:07 BST
    15:07 BST

    One of the first emergency vehicles sent to help rescue people returned to the village ahead of the 60th anniversary.

    It has been restored by Henry Cotton and is kept at the Norfolk Fire Museum.

    Children from Ynysowen primary joined staff from Merthyr Fire Station in finding out how the engine helped save people following the disaster.

    “Seeing this appliance back in Aberfan has been a deeply moving experience," said firefighter Sam Gill.

    "It is an important part of our shared history and today has been about helping young people understand the courage, compassion and community spirit shown in the face of unimaginable tragedy."

  14. Hero dinner lady saved five children by shielding thempublished at 14:55 BST
    14:55 BST

    "If it hadn't been for Nansi Williams, then instead of 116 children dying that morning it would have been 121."

    This is the view of Paul Harries, who is now 66.

    He describes dinner lady Nansi Williams as his hero, because when her body was found after the disaster, she was crouched over five children, protecting them.

    A purple plaque, external is set to be unveiled recognising Nansi's selfless act.

  15. Why was nobody ever prosecuted?published at 14:48 BST
    14:48 BST

    Prof Iain McLean, the co-author of Aberfan - Government and Disaster, has questioned why nobody was ever prosecuted for the Aberfan disaster.

    Speaking on the BBC Radio Wales Phone In earlier, he said the tribunal that took place in 1967 was "devastating" in the words of then-Prime Minister Harold Wilson.

    He added: "And so one of the other failures of the government of the day is their, to me, incomprehensible failure to sack Lord Robens and his senior directors."

    Robens was the chairman of the National Coal Board at the time, and faced numerous complaints about the dangers that loomed a quarter of a mile high above the village.

    One of these included a petition from Pantglas school in 1963, three years before the disaster.

    In 1997, McLean met three of the counsels who had appeared before the 1967 tribunal and asked why no legal action was taken.

    "They all said 'nobody thought of it at the time'," he said.

  17. 'This is not a community trapped in the past - it has endured and rebuilt'published at 14:36 BST
    14:36 BST

    Tony Brown and Huw Thomas
    BBC Wales

    We've worked on the story of the Aberfan disaster many times before, including the 50th anniversary in 2016.

    But returning for the 60th anniversary felt different.

    Aberfan is not just a story in Welsh history, it's a defining moment that continues to shape lives today.

    With every year that passes, there is a greater urgency to record memories before they are lost and to ensure future generations understand what happened here.

    Spending time in the village, we were reminded how deeply the disaster still resonates.

    The tip slide robbed Aberfan of half a generation of children and teachers, while the fight for justice left many families feeling betrayed by those in power.

    Sixty years on, the emotions remain raw. For many people, the disaster is not history, it is something they still live with every day.

    What stayed with us most was that this is not a community trapped in the past.

    While Aberfan will never forget the children who died, it is also a community that has endured and rebuilt.

    We met extraordinary people who have spent 60 years carrying its legacy forward.

    The responsibility to remember remains as strong as ever, and we feel privileged that so many people trusted us to help tell that story.

  18. Famous image that haunts photographer who took itpublished at 14:23 BST
    14:23 BST

    It is one of the most famous photographs of the disaster aftermath, and it won Mel Parry British News Photographer of the Year in 1966.

    In it, PC Victor Jones is shown carrying an eight-year-old Susan Maybank through the crowds to safety.

    But Mel, who was an 18-year-old apprentice at the Merthyr Express, admits it haunts him.

    He puts capturing it down to chance, as he was on a bus travelling through Aberfan, when somebody shouted "the school has fallen down".

    Despite it resonating and winning awards, Mel says he wishes he never took it, and later abandoned his career as a photographer.

  19. Who was responsible for the Aberfan disaster?published at 14:14 BST
    14:14 BST

    An official inquiry found the National Coal Board was responsible for the disaster - but survivors and bereaved families have long pointed to the many injustices they feel they have suffered.

    Money was taken from a public disaster fund to help pay for the removal of remaining coal tips above the village, while compensation payments were limited.

    They also said there was little help to deal with the trauma, and survivor Jeff Edwards recalled a psychologist telling him to think of birthday parties when he had nightmares.

    But he said many children who attended those parties had died, adding: "There was no help. It was basically self-help."
