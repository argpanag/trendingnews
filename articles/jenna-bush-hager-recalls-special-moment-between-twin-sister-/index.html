Jenna Bush Hager Recalls Special Moment Between Twin Sister Barbara, Secret Service Agent and His Daughter on 9/11

TheTools · 11.09.2026 18:17 · source · permalink

Rachel Paula Abrahamson

Twenty-five years after the terrorist attacks of Sept. 11, 2001, Jenna Bush Hager reflected on the fear and uncertainty. But she also remembers a small moment of joy: a little girl jumping on a motel bed, thrilled to be reunited with her father.

Jenna shared the memory on the Sept. 11 episode of TODAY With Jenna and Sheinelle as the co-hosts reflected on the 25th anniversary of the attacks — and the sense of unity that followed one of the darkest days in American history.

At the time, Jenna was a college student in Texas. Her father, George W. Bush, had recently been elected president, and the family was still adjusting to the presence of Secret Service agents.

On the morning of Sept. 11, Jenna’s twin sister, Barbara Bush, was attending Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut. As the attacks unfolded, Secret Service agents rushed to get her to safety.

The agents’ New York office was based at the World Trade Center, Jenna explained, making the events especially personal for those who were tasked with protecting the president’s family.

Barbara was taken to a motel, where one of her Secret Service agents, Steve, eventually reunited with his young daughter.

Steve’s daughter arrived at the motel for what had been planned as a sleepover with her father. Jenna remembers Barbara describing the girl jumping on the bed, simply excited to be with her dad.

Against the backdrop of television images showing the World Trade Center towers collapsing, the scene offered a striking reminder of the human cost of that day.

“(Barbara) said that image of (the little girl) jumping on the bed, so excited to be with her dad, as opposed to this image of towers falling...” Jenna recalled. “Their humanity.”

For Jenna, the image has stayed with her for 25 years.

It was a reminder that even in a moment defined by terror and loss, ordinary acts of love were still unfolding.

Jenna and Sheinelle began their conversation by acknowledging how strange it can feel to mark such a significant anniversary. For those who lived through Sept. 11, the day can feel both impossibly recent and impossibly distant at the same time.

Twenty-five years later, Jenna sees the anniversary as a chance to remember what happened in the aftermath.

She spoke about the way Americans reached for one another in the days and weeks following the attacks, when political and personal differences seemed, for a time, less important than the shared understanding everyone was grieving.

“We still have that in us,” she said. “That’s still part of our DNA.”

Sheinelle agreed.

The hope, she said, is that Americans do not need another tragedy to remind themselves that they are connected.

“You wish it didn’t take something so horrible to bring us together,” she said.
