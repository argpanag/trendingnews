Week in Review: Tests and Pressures

TheTools · 11.09.2026 12:39 · source · permalink

The Mladic test

The lavish funeral of the convicted war criminal Ratko Mladic, complete with military honours and attended by senior Serbian and Bosnian Serb officials, drew condemnation from EU states and renewed questions about how deeply the glorification of wartime figures remains embedded in regional politics.

Experts told BIRN that the spectacle in Serbia reflected a state-sponsored politics of memory rather than Serbian society as a whole, while warning that Mladic’s funeral has become entangled with election campaigns and could further damage already strained regional relations.

Writing from Brussels, Gjeraqina Tuhina argued that this is also a test for the EU: after years of accommodating Serbian President Aleksandar Vucic, Brussels must decide whether its opposition to war-criminal glorification will have consequences beyond strongly worded statements.

Judgment day

After more than five years in detention and a trial marked by delays, controversy and fierce debate over the legacy of the Kosovo Liberation Army, KLA, Kosovo’s former President Hashim Thaci and three other former KLA leaders are finally due to hear the verdict in their war crimes case on September 16.

Our analysis articles examine the arguments over command responsibility, the fairness of the proceedings and prolonged detention, as well as the allegations of witness intimidation that have cast a shadow over the case.

As the verdict approaches amid deep distrust of the court among Kosovo Albanians, the judgment will decide more than the fate of four prominent defendants. It will also test perceptions of a tribunal that has been controversial since its creation.

Read more: Intimidation Claims Loom Large as Kosovo’s Thaci Awaits Trial Verdict (September 11, 2026)

Profits under pressure

Turkey continues to attract millions of tourists, but falling occupancy rates, stubbornly high inflation and shrinking margins suggest that impressive visitor numbers are concealing growing problems for one of the country’s most important industries.

Our economic focus story looks beyond the headline figures to examine why hotels and agencies are struggling to turn arrivals into profits – and why increasingly expensive holidays at home are pushing some Turkish tourists towards cheaper alternatives abroad.

Read more: Falling Profits Cloud Horizons for Turkey’s Booming Tourist Industry (September 8, 2026)

Following the money

Montenegro is racing to demonstrate progress against organised crime as it closes in on possible EU membership; a new law allowing criminal assets to be permanently confiscated before a final conviction gives its authorities a potentially powerful new weapon.

But expanded powers bring concerns over property rights, judicial safeguards and selective enforcement. Meanwhile, experts warn that Montenegro’s perennial problem remains unchanged: adopting ambitious legislation is one thing, but having the institutions and political will to enforce it is another.

Read more: Nearing EU Accession, Montenegro Targets Money Behind Organised Crime (September 7, 2026)

State of independence

Moldova has sharply reduced its dependence on Russian gas, replacing an energy model once dominated by Russia’s Gazprom and breakaway Transnistria with supplies from Romania and Europe, thanks to the new cross-border connections and renewable energy.

Moldovan Energy Minister Dorin Junghietu tells BIRN in an interview why there will be no return to the era of “cheap Russian gas” – and how making Moldova’s energy independence irreversible will depend on balancing security and reforms against higher costs for consumers.

Read more: No More Cheap Russian Gas: Minister Sketches Out Moldova’s Energy Future (September 10, 2026)

100 days of protest

Albania’s “Flamingo Revolution” has reached the 100-day mark without forcing Prime Minister Edi Rama to resign or accept any of the protesters’ demands. But analysts tell Balkan Insight that the movement’s real achievement may lie elsewhere: creating a new form of citizen-led opposition and challenging the sense that Albania’s political landscape is confined to the same old parties and leaders.

Read more: ‘Transformative Protest’: 100 Days On, What Has Albania’s ‘Flamingo Revolution’ Achieved? (September 7, 2026)

A way out of chaos?

Kosovo’s parliament is reconvening, but deep divisions between and within the main parties have left little certainty that lawmakers can agree on a new president and so finally end months of institutional deadlock.

With the parliamentary arithmetic working against an easy compromise and another election looming if the impasse cannot be broken, our analysis examines whether Kosovo’s political rivals can find a way out of what one expert describes as “political, institutional and constitutional chaos”.
