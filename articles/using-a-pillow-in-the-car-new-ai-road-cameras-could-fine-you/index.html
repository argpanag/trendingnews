Using a pillow in the car? New AI road cameras could fine you hundreds

TheTools · 25.09.2026 04:37 · source · permalink

Fines for not wearing seatbelts have surged by almost 700 per cent in one state while experts believe artificial intelligence (AI) technology is unfairly penalising some drivers.

New analysis from Compare the Market shows NSW seatbelt fines have soared by 690.2 per cent between 2023 and 2024, following the introduction of AI-powered cameras capable of detecting drivers not wearing their seatbelts correctly.

Tasmanian fines in the same period increased by 98 per cent and Victorian fines by 75 per cent.

National seatbelt fines rose from 85,768 in 2023 to 183,315 in 2024, an increase of 113.7 per cent in just one year.

It comes as Queensland has just updated its road rules to allow drivers to nominate adult passengers for camera-detected seatbelt offences, the first of any state or territory in Australia.

NRMA spokesman Peter Khoury told The Senior that seatbelt laws should be uniform across the nation, but also warned older drivers were being targeted by new AI cameras.

"Older Australians tend to sit on a pillow, or use pillows and other things for comfort, which then adjusts the seatbelt, and that's how they're getting fined," Khoury said.

"Our bigger concern is the enormous percentage of people, the overwhelming percentage of people, who are anchored into the seatbelt but getting fined because they're not wearing their seatbelts properly."

"For a multitude of reasons, that's happening. That was our biggest concern when we saw the data."

According to NRMA data, 88 per cent of NSW fines in 2024-2025 were people who were buckled up.

"So 88 per cent of all those fines are actually people who are wearing their seatbelt - it's anchored in - but it's not over their shoulder. It's under their arm."

NRMA's recent Buckle Up Right report called for an immediate public education campaign on correct seatbelt use.

Khoury said he will like the campaign to focus on vulnerable demographics, such as pregnant women and older Australians.

"The first thing we want the government to do is run a comprehensive campaign reminding people of the rule," he said.

"The rule is that just being anchored in is not enough."
