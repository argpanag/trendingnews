U.S. Coast Guard searching debris field linked to missing jet

The Latest

  • The U.S. Coast Guard is still searching a "debris field" it said was associated with an air ambulance that went missing Saturday.
  • Officials speaking Sunday in Boston said they didn't want to speculate on the cause of the incident, nor have they found a so-called "black box" that may provide further insight into what happened.
  • Six people were on board, including two Canadian pilots, two Canadian medical workers, one patient and the patient's relative.
  • The Gulfstream G100 jet lost contact overnight as it was flying from Bermuda to Boston, according to Latitude Air Ambulance's director of flight operations.
  • U.S. Coast Guard air and surface crews conducted searches off Nantucket, Mass. Saturday before crews located the debris field.

    How co-ordinators estimate search times

    David Common

    I've spent time inside rescue co-ordination centres in both Canada and the U.S. and am familiar with the tools that search co-ordinators use to determine how long a search will take.

    The Atlantic Ocean is cold — around 13 C at this time of year off the coast of Massachusetts. At those temperatures, the average person who is immersed up to their neck in water would enter hypothermia and lose movement within a couple of hours.

    Search co-ordinators use analytics to determine survivability timelines. But coast guard and air force teams in both Canada and the U.S. will routinely go well beyond the maximum survivability window suggested by their analytics, to make sure they don't abandon any chance of rescue.

    Aircraft flying over water, like this air ambulance frequently does, are required to carry a life raft that can rapidly inflate. But that assumes the aircraft made a controlled water landing and the crew could escape with the raft, rather than experiencing a rapid impact with the ocean.

    The U.S. Coast Guard has already indicated an extensive debris field has been found. The size of a debris field is sometimes associated with a severe impact. But it can also be a product of ocean currents.

How co-ordinators estimate search times

I've spent time inside rescue co-ordination centres in both Canada and the U.S. and am familiar with the tools that search co-ordinators use to determine how long a search will take.

The Atlantic Ocean is cold — around 13 C at this time of year off the coast of Massachusetts. At those temperatures, the average person who is immersed up to their neck in water would enter hypothermia and lose movement within a couple of hours.

Search co-ordinators use analytics to determine survivability timelines. But coast guard and air force teams in both Canada and the U.S. will routinely go well beyond the maximum survivability window suggested by their analytics, to make sure they don't abandon any chance of rescue.

Aircraft flying over water, like this air ambulance frequently does, are required to carry a life raft that can rapidly inflate. But that assumes the aircraft made a controlled water landing and the crew could escape with the raft, rather than experiencing a rapid impact with the ocean.

The U.S. Coast Guard has already indicated an extensive debris field has been found. The size of a debris field is sometimes associated with a severe impact. But it can also be a product of ocean currents.

"Our thoughts go out with the members on the aircraft, as well as their families and friends," he said.

Cpt. John Mctamney, chief of response for the guard's northeast district, said crews will continue their search "until we're at the point where if there were any survivors on the surface of the water, we're confident we would have found them."

The weather has been favourable for teams searching through the area this weekend, he said.

Difficult to identify components of debris field due to size, amount

Officials aren't providing the exact size of the debris field yet, but the amount of components they discovered has made it difficult to identify "exactly what's there," said Lake, commander of the U.S. Coast Guard's northeast district.

The size and amount of debris has made it "very difficult to get into the details of what's there," said Kastrati, search-and-rescue mission co-ordinator.

The field was discovered roughly 32 kilometres off the coast of Nantucket, Mass., Lake said, adding that the water in the area is between 24 and 28 metres deep.

He said teams are now solely focused on finding the six missing people who were on board the plane and that the debris field has been preserved for further investigation.

Jet's black box has not yet been found

The next step in the search will be looking for the jet's black box, said Lake, which might prove difficult given how "extensive" the debris field is.

"In the debris we've identified at this point, there is no indication that we found a black box," he said.

Lake said he didn't want to speculate on what may have caused the jet to crash, adding that it would be part of the upcoming investigation.

Next of kin have been kept updated throughout the search process, said U.S. Coast Guard Rear Admiral Matt Lake.

"Our priority is to find the six personnel on that aircraft," said Lake, referring to the four Canadians and two Bermudians on board the missing jet.

Lake would not provide further details on the missing people, out of respect for their families.

"Right now, we're really just focused on finding the people," said Lt.-Cmdr. Kastriot Kastrati, who is in charge of co-ordinating the search-and-rescue mission.

Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand said on social media this morning that she was "deeply concerned" by the disappearance of the Latitude Air Ambulance.

Global Affairs Canada is in touch with "relevant authorities," she said, as the search continues for the six missing passengers and crew, four of whom are Canadian.
