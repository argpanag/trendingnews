Europe takes on Egypt’s Suez Canal model with an $8.5 billion waterway stretching 107 kilometres across France, Belgium and the Netherlands

TheTools · 27.10.2026 11:59 · source · permalink
  • France is building the $8.5 billion Seine-Nord Europe Canal to strengthen trade links with Belgium and the Netherlands.
  • The 107-kilometre canal will allow much larger cargo barges, reducing dependence on road transport and easing congestion.
  • The canal’s navigation charging system will be similar to, but smaller in scale than, Egypt’s Suez Canal, with precise toll rates yet to be set.
  • While aiming to boost regional freight and reduce truck traffic, environmental groups question whether these benefits outweigh its construction impact.

The 107-kilometre Seine-Nord Europe Canal will accommodate larger cargo barges, reducing reliance on road transport and improving freight movement across northern Europe.

Construction is now expected to be completed in 2032, two years later than the 2030 target reported by CNN in 2023.

The revised timeline comes amid rising costs, with Société du Canal Seine-Nord Europe (SCSNE), the public body overseeing the project, announcing in January 2026 that its budget had increased from €5.1 billion to €7.3 billion ($8.5 billion), citing inflation, construction delays and additional environmental requirements.

Beyond improving regional freight transport, the waterway is expected to operate under France's existing navigation charging system, drawing comparisons with Egypt's Suez Canal, which generates billions of dollars from international shipping tolls.

France's new canal to strengthen European trade

According to SCSNE, the project forms part of the wider Seine-Scheldt transport network, linking France, Belgium and the Netherlands through major European waterways and commercial ports, including Rotterdam and Antwerp.

Stretching across the Hauts-de-France region, the waterway will measure 54 metres wide and 4.5 metres deep, accommodating barges carrying up to 4,400 tonnes of cargo, compared with the 650-tonne capacity of vessels using the existing Canal du Nord.

With this increased capacity, project officials estimate that the canal could replace up to one million heavy goods vehicle journeys on French roads annually, easing congestion and improving freight movement across the region.

Construction will include seven locks, three canal bridges, road and rail crossings, alongside environmental restoration projects intended to limit the ecological impact.

Financing comes from the European Union, the French government and regional authorities, reflecting the project's importance to Europe's transport network.

However, rising costs have raised questions about its long-term economic value, while environmental groups have challenged whether reduced road traffic will offset the effects of construction.

How Europe's new canal compares with Egypt's Suez Canal

France already collects navigation tolls through Voies Navigables de France (VNF), the country's inland waterways authority, which oversees routes including the Canal du Nord.

Under its 2026 tariff schedule, commercial operators pay fixed charges ranging from €24.90 to €93.30 per journey, depending on vessel capacity, alongside additional fees based on cargo weight and distance travelled.

Similarly, the Seine-Nord Europe Canal is expected to operate within France's existing charging framework, although authorities have yet to announce specific toll rates or projected annual revenue.

Egypt's Suez Canal, however, serves a much larger shipping market, stretching 193.3 kilometres between the Mediterranean and Red Seas, with a navigational channel approximately 200 to 210 metres wide and a maximum water depth of 24 metres.

Unlike France's inland waterway, the Egyptian canal accommodates large ocean-going vessels and provides a shorter maritime route between Europe and major Asian economies, including China, India and Japan.

In 2015, Egypt invested $8.2 billion in expanding the Suez Canal, adding a parallel shipping channel and upgrading existing sections to increase capacity and reduce transit delays.

The investment supported one of Egypt's principal sources of foreign currency, with the Suez Canal Authority reporting record annual revenue of $10.25 billion in 2023.

By comparison, France's $8.5 billion project will focus on regional freight rather than competing directly with the Suez Canal, with its economic benefits expected to come from lower transport costs, increased cargo capacity and improved access to European markets.
