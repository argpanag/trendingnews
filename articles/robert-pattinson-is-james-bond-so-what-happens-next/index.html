Robert Pattinson is James Bond. So what happens next?

TheTools · 17.11.2026 07:00 · source · permalink

The wait for the new Bond is over but that’s only the beginning – there are still small issues such as the script, title, setting, casting, branding and promotion …

Behind Bond’s bankability

It’s official: Robert Pattinson is the new James Bond. While never a certainty for the role, for those reading the runes inside the film industry, the announcement will have been a tiny bit anticlimactic. Ever since it emerged that the film’s producers appeared unsatisfied with their initial shortlist and called in Pattinson alongside Nicholas Hoult, it appeared that in this new era for Bond under Amazon MGM, the traditional Bond barrier against an established Hollywood leading man was no longer in place. And with no shade to Hoult, Pattinson’s record at delivering in major franchises – Twilight, The Batman, Dune – as well as his classy acting CV put him firmly in the driving seat.

In another departure from traditional 007 practice, Amazon MGM announced the casting decision via posts on social media, rather than at a press call in a fancy London hotel, which is how the world officially found out about George Lazenby, Roger Moore and Pierce Brosnan. Although this will have been thought through as a way to maximise audience engagement with the forthcoming film, it perhaps is a disappointment to Bond purists that a big show wasn’t staged, and it may be Amazon MGM’s next order of business to organise a fan-type convention – or even piggyback on the next big fanclub event, due in December – to present Pattinson to an adoring audience.

The priority, of course, is to get the actual film, Bond 26, up and running. Peaky Blinders creator Steven Knight, who has been hired to write the script, says he has delivered his first draft – but if previous iterations of the series are anything to go by, there are likely to be copious notes, rewrites, and other writers hauled into the process. It is the Bond movie’s special curse that it needs to be immediately comprehensible to global audiences while putatively delivering a labyrinthine narrative of bluff and counterbluff, contain several massive action set pieces and a handful of drawn-out romantic and/or sexual encounters.

It has also got used to providing ample product placement and branding opportunities, although it is yet to be seen whether Amazon MGM will continue with this method of covering the film’s budget. In recent years, Bond has faced a tonal challenge too: retaining its customary small-c conservatism while attempting to grapple with more modern ideas about PTSD and sexism.

Having landed its main man, Amazon will seek to fill out the rest of the cast, though exactly who and what will be required is not yet apparent. We do know that Bond movies require at least one and sometimes two supervillains, a gig that has provided a nice paycheque for many a central European actor. There will also be the requisite number of female actors to hire, as there’s no sense that Amazon MGM is going to turn its back on the “Bond girl” motif.

Without a script, a shooting schedule can’t be nailed down, but industry rumours suggest production has been planned for 2027, for a release in November 2028. Pattinson’s other big gig is The Batman Part 2, the sequel to his hit superhero movie, and its recently announced delay meant that Pattinson was freer than expected to test for Bond. (The turmoil at Batman 2’s DC Studios may also have played a part in pushing Pattinson towards another franchise.)

Perhaps the one vulnerability in Bond’s current armour is director Denis Villeneuve, an auteur who works on a large scale and whose past films include Dune, Blade Runner 2049 and Arrival. His hiring marked another departure from traditional Bond practice, whose pre-Amazon producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G Wilson preferred less extravagant directors they could keep control over. Lead producers David Heyman and Amy Pascal will be keeping a sharp eye on what Villeneuve gets up to.

If Amazon MGM does take the brand-partnership route between now and the film’s release, we’ll no doubt see a tidal wave of display ads and promo spots with Pattinson in character while driving whichever car, chugging whichever beer and glancing at whichever timepiece the film-makers have struck a deal with. These commercial activities will also provide an unrivalled avalanche of marketing for the film itself, reaching areas that traditional side-of-the-bus methods can’t.

A key element in the Bond promotional gunroom is the title song, which are nearly always substantial hits in their own right. Had Callum Turner been given the role, producers would have been confident of securing Turner’s wife Dua Lipa on the soundtrack; now not so much. Even so, they will be thinking about this already and no doubt are putting feelers out to purveyors of brass-and-string power balladry as we speak.

Either way, at the other end of the production tunnel, we can expect the mother of all promotional launches when Bond 26 – whatever it ends up being called – is ready. (And if past movies are anything to go by, the shenanigans over its title will be a saga in itself.) A multi-country rollout, with premieres in a string of major cities across the planet, compressed into as short a time frame as possible to maximise marketing impact (and minimise the effect of digital piracy). Daniel Craig’s final three Bond films posted staggering box-office revenue, collecting $2.76bn ($3.57bn adjusted for inflation) at the global box office. Bond 26 has a lot to live up to.
