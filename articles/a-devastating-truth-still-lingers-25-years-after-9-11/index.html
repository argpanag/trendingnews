A 'devastating truth' still lingers 25 years after 9/11

TheTools · 10.09.2026 18:47 · source · permalink

  • Topic:Terrorism

Topic:Terrorism

When the World Trade Center's twin towers were levelled by a terrorist attack in 2001 a cloud of ash and dust was released. (Reuters: Sean Adair)

After two hijacked passenger jets slammed into the World Trade Center on September 11, 2001, emergency responders flocked to the scene to do what they do: search, rescue and recover.

But as they went about their work in the days and weeks after the tragedy, they were exposed to a cloud of dust and ash that hung in the air like a heavy blanket.

Inside that toxic cloud was a potent mix of asbestos, powdered glass and cement, heavy metals, and numerous toxic chemicals.

"You were just standing in a big pile of white dust … with paper blowing everywhere," said Michael O'Connell, a retired New York firefighter, of the weeks spent sifting through the pulverised towers now known as Ground Zero.

Michael O'Connell was sent to Ground Zero in New York as a firefighter. (Supplied: Michael O'Connell)

"That's why so many people got sick, because we didn't leave until we got each and every single person we could out of there."

Years later, he was diagnosed with an inflammatory disease, sarcoidosis, as well as an autoimmune disease. Both were later found to be related to the environmental toxins brought about by September 11.

Many of his colleagues have also fallen ill with a variety of illnesses and long-term health conditions.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates more than 400,000 people in Lower Manhattan, including those who lived and worked there, were exposed to toxic material from the collapse of the twin towers.

Some of them reported mild illnesses soon after the attack, while a myriad of other health issues were not diagnosed until many years later.

The source of all their sicknesses is difficult to track, but government programs have since recognised that people in Lower Manhattan experienced new or exacerbated respiratory sicknesses from 9/11 air, as well as orthopaedic disorders and all cancers.

Earlier this week, New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani acknowledged 9/11-related health issues as he directed his administration to release air quality reports, contamination records, and correspondence among city officials to the public for the first time.

"The devastating truth is that the suffering did not end once the smoke cleared and the dust settled," he said at a news conference.

"People got sick because the leaders they trusted lied and told them they were safe to breathe in toxic air."

The growing health crisis 25 years on has driven experts, lawyers and politicians to push for more recognition of the issue, as well as for better compensation for victims and their families.

'There was ash and dust everywhere'

Australian Ann Margulis was living and working in New York City with her husband when Al Qaeda unleashed its campaign of terror on America.

She was practising yoga in a studio facing the World Trade Center when she saw, through the floor-to-ceiling windows of the fitness centre, the first plane hit the north tower and the second hit the south tower.

"It was the worst moment in my life," she said.

Ann Margulis can only recall glimpses of 9/11 but she remembers walking through the dust left in its wake. (Supplied: Ann Margulis)

Much of the day remains a blur of confusing images. A blazing fire, destroyed buildings, black smoke, friendly faces and an empty hospital room.

Ms Margulis can still vividly recall when she and her husband got the all-clear to return to their apartment opposite the New York Stock Exchange, five days after the towers collapsed.

"[When we arrived] there was ash and dust everywhere. There was still smoke. There was the smell of burning plastics, electronics and fuel,"

"[There] was just no getting away from it."

The problem for residents in New York was the toxic dust coated everything. Outside and inside, at home and in the office, in air vents and on bedding, sometimes settling as inches (25 millimetres) thick soot on surfaces.

But the risks of direct exposure to 9/11 air were not well understood.

In 2001, government officials, including then-head of the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Christine Todd Whitman and then-mayor Rudy Giuliani, assured those in Lower Manhattan that the air was safe.

While emergency responders at the Ground Zero site were warned to wear respirators, citizens and workers in the surrounding areas, which were engulfed in dust and ash, were not.

New Yorkers like Ms Margulis instead returned to their homes and offices unaware of the risks.

Chief medical officer with the Fire Department of New York (FDNY), David Prezant, said for days the air was so dense you could not see your outstretched hand in front of you.

A firefighter walks amid rubble near the base of the destroyed World Trade Center in New York. (Reuters: Peter Morgan)

"Mostly the exposure was to particulate matter — incredibly coarse particulates from collapsed, pulverised concrete," Dr Prezant said.

"[That] has exposed firefighters to silicates … which create a chronic inflammatory state. [It] can lead to a host of illnesses."

Up to 10 months after 9/11, health officials still found evidence of asbestos almost 1 kilometre from Ground Zero, according to a sample of air quality records released this week.

Decades on, Dr Prezant said the full extent of the health crisis has not yet been realised.

Getting sick years after exposure

Four years after 9/11, Ms Margulis started feeling sick with a strange array of symptoms.

In 2005 she was diagnosed with thyroid cancer, and then, four years later, she was given another diagnosis: stage 4 stomach cancer.

It was while in recovery in Australia that she made the connection between the toxic atmosphere in New York in 2001 and her double cancer diagnoses.

Those sickened by the toxic dust and ash in its aftermath believe they are the hidden victims. (Reuters: Ken Cedeno)

"The EPA said the air quality was fine and they said we could go back home after five days. [But] it wasn't [fine]," Ms Margulis said.

Links between exposure to some of the hazards in the 9/11 debris and a range of health conditions, including cancers and respiratory illnesses, have been well documented in the years since.

One study of an estimated 30,000 police officers and recovery workers who worked at Ground Zero found the group had a higher risk of getting cancer compared to the general population.

Another study found intense exposure to the dust and ash in the aftermath nearly doubled the likelihood of systemic autoimmune disease.

Dr Prezant said experts had expected to see rates of a lung disease known as pulmonary fibrosis increasing "20 to 30 years later".

"But the numbers that we're seeing are quite worrisome. The magnitude and the severity of it is staggering,"

"The other thing that we are looking out for is late-emerging diseases. Cardiovascular disease. Autoimmune rheumatologic diseases, cognitive issues, loss of memory. These are things that we're now seeing in increasing numbers."

Florida International University professor and physician Roberto Lucchini worked directly with 9/11 survivors at Mount Sinai for eight years and said doctors were still discovering new information about the health impacts of the attack.

"We continue to learn," said Professor Lucchini, who now leads research involving 9/11 first responders who relocated to Florida.

"For example, now we're seeing this cognitive impact, and we have to be vigilant about that because if it's real, and unfortunately it looks like there is a lot of possibility [it is] … it's like an accelerated aging."

There are 154,700 people signed on to the World Trade Center Health Program which provides health care coverage and monitors people with ongoing medical issues connected to the attack.

Many have come down with illnesses and of those, 9,224 have died, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

A new report released last week by the FDNY World Trade Center Health Program found cancer remains a major issue among first responders, with non-melanoma skin cancers and prostate cancers among the most diagnosed.

It also found higher rates of thyroid cancer, prostate cancer, and certain haematologic cancers among firefighters exposed to the site when compared with a US reference population.

More than 400 first responders died on 9/11. (Reuters: Anthony Correia AC)

Retired FDNY firefighter Michael O'Connell is still struggling to come to terms with the scale of the tragedy from 9/11 and the many friends he has lost in the decades since.

"I didn't realise the magnitude of how many people I would actually know. And how many friends I would have lost,"

"We're still losing, on average, a responder a day from 9/11 illnesses like cancer. It's a sad reality we live with."

The grief and helplessness have given rise to a new dictum among his colleagues.

"We have a saying that it's not if [being diagnosed] is gonna happen, it's when."

The fight for compensation

The September 11 Victim Compensation Fund (VCF) was created by US Congress 11 days after the tragedy, at a time when the United States was still in deep mourning.

The idea behind the VCF was to provide cash benefits to two groups of people who had suffered physical injury: victims who were on the planes, and those who were at the sites that were targeted.

It took years before officials understood there was another group of 9/11 victims.

Those who became sick from exposure are able to access medical treatment and compensation through a special fund. (Reuters: Anthony Correia)

"The bad air quality ultimately affected my health and a lot of other people's health as well," Ms Margulis said.

Ensuring the people who fell ill after being exposed to 9/11 get access to the care they need has been as much a political challenge as a medical one.

The VCF closed in 2003, and it took almost a decade to reopen.

In 2010, Congress passed the James Zadroga Act to cover the health costs of those sickened by environmental toxins from the September 11 attacks.

The act was named in honour of a police captain who worked at Ground Zero and later died after developing respiratory problems.

When the VCF was renewed, its eligibility criteria were extended to those who suffered physical injuries or illnesses due to "rescue, recovery, or debris removal work". The fund was reauthorised again in 2015 and 2019.

"It's been an unnecessary fight," attorney Matthew McCauley said of the political push.

Matthew McCauley (right) has been involved in efforts to reauthorise the Victim Compensation Fund over the years. (Reuters: Leah Millis)

He was a paramedic and police officer when he was sent to Ground Zero and, decades later, joined the effort to reauthorise and permanently fund the VCF in 2019. Now he represents those with 9/11-related illnesses.

"We have an interesting saying with all the legislative [work] that we've done in [Washington] DC [over the years]: the science eventually catches up to what we know," Mr McCauley said.

The new legislation meant Ms Margulis could lodge a claim for her cancers to be recognised in connection with 9/11.

After doing some research on first responders who had fallen ill, she reached out to New York law firm Barasch and McGarry, which was helping 9/11 victims, to see what could be done.

An intense legal battle followed for several years. The American government eventually agreed the pollutants in the air after 9/11 contributed to her thyroid and stomach cancers. She was given an undisclosed settlement.

"It was very difficult. And then also recovering from cancer, especially from the stomach cancer," she said.

While she is grateful for the funding, she believes more should be done to support those living with chronic health conditions.

Hidden victims of 9/11

Ms Margulis is now one of more than 43,000 people who have been found to have a health condition related to 9/11.

It is an amount that far exceeds the number of people killed on the day, a reminder that the insurmountable losses of the terrorist attack did not end at the World Trade Center site.

"It was not a New York City event. It was a world event. And there are people who are sick around the world as a result," Mr McCauley said.

He said it is clear now the dust and debris were a problem, and maybe "things could have been done differently" all those years ago.

"But the resolve to recover everybody from Ground Zero in the nine months that it was there, you weren't going to take that away from the responders because to bring families closure is something else," he added.

Twenty-five years on, Ms Margulis said the images of the 9/11 aftermath are still burned into her mind.

"Everybody still remembers where they were on 9/11. I don't think anybody will forget. And neither should they,"

"[But] I would like that if people still need help, to see [them get it]. There's still a lot of suffering that's going on with all of this."
