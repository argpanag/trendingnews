‘It’s maths and science and consistency.’ The training formula that powered Spencer Matthews’ 2:49 marathon PB

TheTools · 08.10.2026 16:57 · source · permalink

The podcast host, TV personality and endurance athlete shares the strategies that helped him to surpass his finish time expectations at the 2026 Berlin Marathon – and how he’s now approaching a 2:45 marathon attempt in Valencia

At this year’s Berlin Marathon, Spencer Matthews entered what he calls ‘unicorn territory’ – he achieved the sub-2:50 marathon that he thought was almost unreachable. In fact, before hitting the start line and going on to secure a finish time of 2:49:46, he says that he gave himself no more than a 4% chance of breaking the 2:50 barrier at that race.

But broadcaster and entrepreneur Matthews, who first found fame as a star on reality TV show Made in Chelsea, is not unfamiliar with the marathon distance. In 2024, for example, he completed a gruelling 30 desert marathons in 30 days in Jordan. Then, in 2025, he conquered Project Se7en – a quest to finish a full Ironman triathlon in all seven continents within just three weeks. Alongside that, he’s completed several Abbott World Marathon Majors events, with his best performances pre-Berlin being a 3:00:04 in London this April and a 2:57:42 – his first sub-3 – a month earlier in Tokyo. It makes sense, then, that Matthews considers himself ‘a runner now above everything else’.

Off the back of his latest PB, we caught up with Matthews to find out what has made this particular marathon training block so special and successful – plus, how he hopes to hit an even bigger target at the Valencia Marathon in December...

The race day mindset – and execution

‘I’d played out a few scenarios in my mind of what ‘good’ looks like – what I’d be happy with and what would be a bit of a nightmare,’ says Matthews, reflecting on the way that he mentally approached Berlin.

‘If I’m being completely honest, I was expecting to be in 2:52, 2:54 territory,’ adds the Untapped podcast host, who documented the race as part of his ‘Road to 2:45’ YouTube series. ‘I’d never, ever, in my life, successfully run 42.2km splits that way – my second half [in Berlin] was only 15 seconds slower than my first half. I don’t want to call them perfect splits, but I couldn’t really have hoped for more. It was a really special day.

‘I knew that I was on for it [sub-2:50] at 30km, but then the legs got tired and I slowed down to a 4:08 kilometre, before pulling it straight back to a 3:56,’ continues Matthews, highlighting how mental strength can influence physical performance, whether that’s during a high-stakes race or an everyday training session. ‘I thought, “I’ve run enough at 3:56 now [during this race] for me to be able to have a few slower kilometres.” The marathon is an amazing distance because you have time to rationalise stuff – it’s not like a 5K, 10K or even half marathon, where you’re really pushing the limit for most of it. With the marathon, you can mentally drag yourself out of a shit place if you have resilience. Providing your heart rate hasn’t spiked to 180 and you’re in hell, if you’re really on that edge, you can get yourself back off the cliff. I like a marathon for that reason.

‘There were pain patches – I wouldn’t call it a pain cave, or anywhere near hitting the wall – but I was able to keep pace in the legs,’ he notes, debriefing the toughest part of Berlin. ‘The marathon is painful, full stop. There are moments of doubt during it. There are moments where you do physically slow up and have to pull yourself out of it to get faster again. But I wouldn’t trade that experience for another.’

The nuts and bolts of the Berlin Marathon block

‘Running to a particular time goal, especially when it’s a bit of a long shot, is a mixture of straightforward maths and science and consistency,’ notes Matthews. ‘You just have to stay consistently on plan and do the work. There’s no way of cheating it or brushing it under the rug. Certain marathon times don’t do themselves.

‘The fact that I’m now a sub-2:50 marathoner is still a pinch me moment,’ he reflects. ‘I wasn’t expecting that result this early, to be honest. I’ve been in a block of about 150 sessions, including the odd double session, and obviously a lot can change in that time. Broadly speaking, I now run six times a week and for the past four months, there has been a purpose to each session. I haven’t thought, “Oh, I’ll just run three laps of the park” – so even if the scheduled session is just three easy laps of the park, it still plays a role and has a purpose.

These scheduled sessions have been programmed by Irish Olympian and 2:09 marathoner Stephen Scullion, who became Matthews’ coach after being a guest on Untapped.

‘Stephen inspired me in the room, through that conversation, to take on this challenge,’ says Matthews. ‘I just had this burning desire to give more to running and become faster. I didn’t have that angle with it before meeting him. Not to be too dramatic, but you could say that he changed my life.

‘He is a running genius – a complete legend of the game,’ continues Matthews. ‘I find him and his knowledge deeply infectious. Having a coach, or someone in your corner, can shape your goals and make all the difference to your training.’

Group gains

Teaming up with others – not just Scullion – really has had a positive impact on Matthews during this training block. He even raced Berlin alongside endurance coach and events organiser Chris Taylor – the logistical mastermind behind Project Se7en and countless other demanding, daunting and complicated running challenges.

‘I love the track and I’m lucky because I live right next to Battersea Park – which is the centre of the universe for running, really – and I have a good group of friends who also love running,’ says Matthews. ‘Training for the 30 marathons in 30 days challenge was very lonely, because I did all the training on my own – and I kind of only really discovered running with other people through this and it makes the whole experience so much better. That’s not to say that you don’t grind out a hard session by yourself every so often, but it’s wonderful to run with mates.’

World record-breaking inspiration

Scullion isn’t the only elite athlete who has shaped Matthews’ marathon training. Josh Kerr, who famously broke the men’s world record for the mile this summer, has also been enormously influential.

‘I’ve got this little trick that I use all the time,' reveals Matthews, talking about another mental strategy that he deploys during hard sessions and races. ‘I’m obsessed with Josh Kerr’s mile. A mile is about 1600m – four laps of the track. So whenever I have 1600m to go of a hard session, I always just dial into Josh Kerr’s mile and his face at the finish line – and it really gets me amped up to ‘only’ have four laps left.

‘I broke the Berlin Marathon into eight-and-a-half 5K reps, averaging 4:00 min/km for each – and when I had 1600m to go for each rep, I really dialled into it. So I’d take the first bit a little easier, then really knuckle down in the final 1600m, on repeat. Then, of course, there was the actual 1600m left to go and I literally just took myself to that incredible mile world record moment. I think that I ran the last 2km at 3:51s. I’ve never had pace in my legs at the end of a marathon before – and I got that incredible goosebumps feeling throughout my body. Then there was the sign for 1km to go and I thought, “Just send it!” I’ll remember it forever.’

The upward trajectory to Valencia

With the 2026 Valencia Marathon – the ‘A’ goal – now just weeks away, it’s back to the reality of training. But although the 2:45 goal still stands, Matthews is keen to appreciate the journey that has taken him this far already – and the achievements that he has already banked.

‘Our game is to try to set goals that are exciting, interesting and barely within reach and to enjoy the process of reaching those goals, rather than achieving the goal itself,’ he notes. ‘If I’m fortunate enough to achieve the goal that we’ve set ourselves and run a sub-2:45 in Valencia, which now feels very much within reach, I’m likely going to cross the finish line and want to go sub 2:40. It’s like a disease – you’re always going to want to shave time off. So it’s about enjoying the process of getting faster, celebrating the wins and learning from the failures.

‘I think that it’s really important to be proud of yourself,’ continues Matthew, who previously had a troubled relationship with alcohol. ‘What got me through a lot of my harder, longer, deeper challenges was giving myself the satisfaction of getting rid of some of that former shame that I may have carried when I was an alcoholic, drinking like a fish and destroying myself – and to channelling all of that into something that is inherently positive. I find that running really helps with my periods of abstinence.

‘I’d love to have a really solid race in Valencia. I’d love to bring the ‘Road to 2:45’ to a positive close.’
