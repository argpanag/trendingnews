Social Media Rumor Claims Anthropic's Claude Cracked Millennium Prize Math Problem — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 06.09.2026 20:16 · source · permalink

A viral post on social media platform X has ignited speculation that Anthropic's Claude AI model has solved the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize challenges that carries a $1 million reward from the Clay Mathematics Institute. The claim, which has drawn more than 2.5 million views, remains unverified, with no corresponding paper, proof text, or expert review materials appearing in public channels.

The rumor traces back to a post by X user Andrew Curran, who predicted that Anthropic has resolved the century-old problem and that the result is currently undergoing expert review. Curran further speculated that the company would announce the breakthrough before its initial public offering. As of now, the Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem among its unsolved Millennium Prize challenges.

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids such as water and air move. The open question centers on whether solutions to the three-dimensional incompressible case remain smooth indefinitely from smooth initial conditions, or whether singularities—physically undefined blow-up points—can form in finite time. The problem has remained unresolved for nearly two centuries and represents one of the most formidable challenges in partial differential equations.

Tao's Hypothetical Scenario Fuels Speculation

What lent the rumor an air of plausibility was a series of posts from Fields Medalist Terence Tao two days earlier. On September 3, the UCLA mathematician used the Navier-Stokes equations as an example to discuss how AI might reshape mathematical research after solving major open problems.

Tao described a future research workflow in which an autonomous AI system with substantial computing resources continuously attempts different mathematical constructions, analyzes failures, adjusts approaches, and verifies results, ultimately producing an extremely complex candidate proof verified through formal proof systems like Lean. His central concern was not whether AI could solve such problems, but how the mathematical knowledge generated during the process would be preserved.

In traditional mathematics research, difficult problems often generate valuable byproducts over years of exploration: new lemmas, new tools, new research directions, and follow-up questions. Tao worried that if AI completes the entire exploration in a closed environment, humans would receive only a verified final result, with many valuable intermediate paths potentially lost to the mathematical community.

The specificity of Tao's scenario—AI searching candidate structures, performing numerical checks, and forming massive Lean proof files to solve Navier-Stokes regularity—led some readers to speculate whether he possessed undisclosed information. Social media discussions soon emerged asking whether Tao was hinting that Claude had already solved the problem, and Curran's subsequent prediction amplified the narrative.

Tao later issued a clarification. He stated that he was unaware of any significant new progress on the Navier-Stokes problem and that his earlier discussion represented a hypothetical AI research scenario. However, he added that given the current pace of AI development, such a scenario has become realistically plausible.

The 'Opportunity Cost' of AI-Solved Problems

The mathematician elaborated on what he called the opportunity cost of machine-generated proofs. Open problems like Navier-Stokes have driven the creation of new methods, new questions, and new researchers for decades. If AI rapidly delivers a final answer, the mathematical community must consider how to convert the machine's exploration process, methods, and failed routes into mathematical knowledge that humans can understand and inherit.

Tao was blunt about the stakes. If an AI company runs the entire iteration process internally and keeps it confidential, publishing only the final proof, the mathematical community would gain almost nothing. "From a technical standpoint, one of the most prominent open problems in mathematics would be solved; but for mathematics itself, almost no value would be added," Tao wrote. He added that third parties attempting to reverse-engineer the process through AI-assisted efforts could only recover part of it, and even that would be extraordinarily difficult.

The core issue, Tao argued, is that the value of solving such a problem lies not in the final answer but in the failed paths that reveal precise obstacles and force adjustments to hypotheses. These dead ends, which appear unproductive on the surface, are often highly instructive precisely because of how they fail. If an AI system knows the final hypothesis in advance, it naturally suppresses exploration of alternative routes—the very routes that would have revealed new insights about fluid mechanics.

Recent AI Milestones Lend Credibility

The rumor's rapid spread reflects a series of genuine breakthroughs in AI mathematics over recent months that would have seemed implausible just a few years ago.

On September 4, Anthropic announced that Claude had completed the first end-to-end, computer-checked formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem. The theorem was originally proved by Andrew Wiles in the 1990s, but mathematicians have spent decades attempting to translate the extraordinarily complex proof into Lean's formal language so that a computer could verify each step. Claude accomplished this in 11 days of largely autonomous operation, generating approximately 13 million lines of Lean code and proving roughly 30,300 machine-verifiable theorems, of which about 29,500 entered the final proof. Kevin Buzzard, a mathematician involved in long-term Fermat formalization efforts, responded positively to the result.

Less than a month earlier, Anthropic disclosed that an unreleased research version of Claude had attempted the Riemann hypothesis, another Millennium Prize problem. While it did not prove the conjecture itself, the model advanced a related problem: raising the lower bound for the proportion of Riemann zeta function zeros known to lie on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%—a record that had stood since 1989.

OpenAI has also been active in this space. In May, the company published a discrete geometry result in which a general reasoning model constructed a new set of unit-distance points, overturning a long-standing conjecture related to the Erdős unit distance problem. By August, OpenAI had released ten mathematical and theoretical computer science results, including solutions or substantive advances on multiple long-standing open problems.

What Changes When Answers Get Cheap

The shift from solving Olympiad problems to producing paper-level results and large-scale formal proofs is forcing mathematicians to reconsider their role. Verification is becoming increasingly critical. Language models can generate vast quantities of mathematical derivations at speed, but they can also produce subtle errors. Proof assistants like Lean break proofs into machine-checkable steps, and as AI-generated proofs accelerate, formal verification is emerging as essential infrastructure. Tao has repeatedly emphasized that future bottlenecks in mathematical research may shift toward checking, organizing, and understanding.

The division of labor is also changing. If routine derivation, literature search, computational experiments, and even portions of proof-writing can be delegated to AI, human researchers may concentrate on problem selection, formulating conjectures, designing research routes, and distilling machine-generated results into explainable new theories. Tao has described this future as a form of "big mathematics," where complex problems are decomposed into modules with humans, AI, and formal proof systems collaborating, then reassembled through machine verification.

The episode also highlights a deeper institutional question. For decades, a single important open problem could sustain an entire research direction, with the detours around it spawning new theories. If AI compresses that process dramatically, answers will arrive faster, but the mathematical community must redesign how it preserves research processes, allocates credit, and trains the next generation of researchers.

Whether Claude has actually conquered the Navier-Stokes problem remains firmly in the realm of social media speculation. But the episode itself is revealing: a few years ago, "AI solves a Millennium Prize problem" sounded like science fiction. By 2026, people are seriously debating what the mathematical community should do if it actually happens.
