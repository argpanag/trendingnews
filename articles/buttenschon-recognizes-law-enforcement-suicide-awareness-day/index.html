Buttenschon recognizes Law Enforcement Suicide Awareness Day

TheTools · 26.09.2026 11:30 · source · permalink

UTICA, N.Y. — On Friday, Assemblywoman Marianne Buttenschon, D-119, hosted a press conference at the Utica State Office Building alongside law enforcement leadership and officers in recognition of Law Enforcement Suicide Awareness Day.

The event happened ahead of Saturday’s observance when New York State Assembly Resolutions memorialized the proclamation of Sept. 26, 2026, as Law Enforcement Suicide Awareness Day in the state of New York.

The observance happened in conjunction with National Law Enforcement Suicide Awareness Day and coincided with the National observance of Suicide Awareness Month.

The national advocacy organization Blue H.E.L.P. created the statewide day of awareness in 2020 to recognize and honor officers lost to suicide, amplify mental health conversations within the law enforcement community and reduce the stigma around seeking support, affirming that a single moment does not define an officer’s life or legacy.

According to research, more law enforcement officers die by suicide each year nationally than are feloniously killed in the line of duty.

Between 2016 and 2022, 1,287 public safety personnel, including law enforcement and correctional officers, died by suicide nationwide, an average of nearly 184 deaths per year.

According to the data, these losses are concentrated among local departments and larger agencies in particular. This underscores that no department, regardless of size or jurisdiction, is
untouched by this crisis.

Chief Olney, Whitesboro Police Department Chief David Olney, a former New York State Police Sergeant and former chief of the Boonville Police Department, said two to three times as many police officers lose their lives to suicide each year as to line-of-duty incidents.

While the average civilian encounters two to three critical incidents in a lifetime, Olney said, a police officer experiences between 500 and 800 over a 20-year career.

“This cumulative trauma, combined with the severe professional pressures inherent to law enforcement, serves as a primary driver of these tragic statistics,” Olney said. “Consequently, any measures or resources implemented to support officer well-being and alleviate these burdens are profoundly valuable and appreciated.”

Open conversations about mental health within the law enforcement community remain essential to reaching officers before a moment of crisis becomes a permanent loss. Every department, agency, civic leader, and community member has a role to play in ensuring that no officer feels they must face that struggle alone.

“Every law enforcement officer we lose to suicide is a tragedy that ripples through their department, their family, and our entire community,” Buttenschon.

“This resolution is a reminder that seeking support is never a sign of weakness, but of strength, and that no officer should feel that they must carry that weight alone,” Buttenschon continued. “I am grateful to our local law enforcement community who have joined us today to help carry this message forward.”

If you or a law enforcement officer you know is struggling, help is available:

988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline — Call or text 988 for 24/7, free and confidential support.

Crisis Text Line — Text BLUE to 741741 to connect with free, 24/7 confidential support.

COPLINE — A confidential, 24-hour hotline answered by trained retired law enforcement officers, offering continuous critical clinical support. Call (800-) 267-5463 or visit www.copline.org.

First H.E.L.P. — A searchable database connecting first responders with emotional, financial, and spiritual assistance. Visit 1sthelp.org.
