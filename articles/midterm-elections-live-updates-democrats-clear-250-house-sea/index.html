Here’s the latest.

The Democratic House majority surged past 250 seats on Thursday as the party seized its biggest majority in nearly two decades by flipping more seats, including in Montana, where a former smoke jumper disrupted decades of Republican dominance. The last time either party held so many seats was 2008, when Democrats swept to power behind Barack Obama.

The latest race calls by The Associated Press continued to strengthen Democrats’ hold on the House, though they also hoped to add to their margin in the Senate with seats in Alaska and Mississippi, where their nominees held narrow leads over the Republican incumbents.

The political consequences of voters’ rebuke of President Trump and his party were still unfolding. In an interview with The New York Times, Secretary of State Marco Rubio dismissed reports that he was planning to leave the Trump administration. Mr. Rubio, a possible presidential contender in 2028, declined to answer questions about his political future.

More than 20 House races were still uncalled, and the Senate contests remained front and center.

In Mississippi, Scott Colom, a Democratic prosecutor, slowly increased his lead over Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, to a little more than 1,800 votes as of early evening. Lawyers for Ms. Hyde-Smith wrote to Mississippi state officials to request “the preservation of all affidavit ballots,” a sign that the courts could become involved in the race. A lawyer for Mr. Colom’s campaign wrote a letter to election officials objecting to aspects of the request from the Hyde-Smith campaign.

In Alaska, former Representative Mary Peltola was leading Senator Dan S. Sullivan by a few hundred votes in a race that continued to draw complaints from Mr. Trump, who said the only “fair solution” involved transferring votes from two other candidates to the Republican incumbent. A New York Times analysis of the available ranked-choice data in the state found that Mr. Sullivan would be unlikely to make significant gains if the race advanced to a ranked-choice tabulation, which will take place on Nov. 18 if neither candidate secures 50 percent of the vote. The findings suggest that Ms. Peltola, the Democratic nominee, is on track to eventually triumph in the race.

Elsewhere, Republicans managed to flip a House seat in Tennessee, where a Democrat had stepped down after conservatives carved up the city of Memphis, but Democrats continued their run of wins in traditionally conservative areas by capturing seats in Arizona, Virginia and Colorado.

In Montana, Sam Forstag, a union leader who used to parachute into remote areas to battle wildfires for the U.S. Forest Service, won a longtime Republican seat by running on a pro-labor platform that earned endorsements from progressives like Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez of New York.

While House candidates were learning the outcomes of their races, the remaining Senate contests seemed days, or possibly weeks, from being decided.

In Mississippi, the vote counts were rising slowly. Mr. Colom’s lead, which had been a few dozen votes on Wednesday, grew as more ballots were tabulated. But an unknown number of provisional and mail-in ballots were still to be counted.

Democratic gains could advance an alternative to the Electoral College.

Democrats’ midterm victories may raise the chances that enough states sign onto a mechanism that, in theory, could elect the next president effectively by popular vote instead of through the typical rules of the Electoral College.

The party’s surge in Tuesday’s elections included gaining a hold on the governor’s office and both statehouse chambers in at least four states, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Those states could join an agreement called the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, which promises that participants would give all their electoral votes to the presidential ticket that wins the national popular vote.

But the agreement, which has been more popular among Democrats, takes effect only once signed by states representing a majority of the Electoral College, or at least 270 electoral votes, the amount needed to win the presidency. Eighteen states and the District of Columbia have adopted the compact so far, representing 222 electoral votes.

If all four of the states that just won total Democratic control were to join the agreement, it would tip the group over the 270-vote threshold. Arizona, which is allotted 11 electoral votes, was also projected to have a blue trifecta, though not all of its races had been called by Thursday evening.

Lawmakers in key states said on Thursday that they had already begun having conversations this week about introducing legislation to join the agreement in the next session, and were hopeful that they would succeed ahead of 2028. But broadening the compact could present a mighty political lift.

“The time is right,” Carrie Rheingans, a Democrat in the Michigan House of Representatives, said in an interview. “This blue tsunami is really going to help push this forward across the whole nation.”

Still, Democratic lawmakers “can be apprehensive” with a new majority, said Mattie Daughtry, the Democratic president of Maine’s State Senate. She worked on the compact issue for over a decade before her state joined in 2024.

“The biggest roadblock is convincing them to use these majorities to do big things,” Ms. Daughtry said.

In 2019, Nevada’s Democratic governor vetoed a bill to join the agreement, saying it could diminish smaller states’ influence in presidential elections.

Now, it is unclear whether lawmakers will prioritize passing the compact after running campaigns dominated by other concerns, like easing high costs and imposing guardrails on the Trump administration.

“Time will tell,” said Clinton Anderson, a Democratic state assemblyman in Wisconsin, adding that he planned to reintroduce a compact bill. “I want to make it a priority, but it’s not the thing I heard on the doors I knocked.”

Kelda Roys, another Democratic lawmaker in Wisconsin, said that her initial attempt to get her state to join the compact in 2009 had been met with hesitancy among people who were unsure exactly what it was. Now, she said, there is a broader public understanding of what problems the Electoral College could present.

“National popular vote will be something that we’ll prioritize early in the session,” Ms. Roys said.

The idea for a compact among states to bypass the Electoral College gained traction among legal scholars and supporters after 2000, when George W. Bush won the presidency while losing the national popular vote. Momentum grew in 2016 after a second Republican candidate, Donald J. Trump, won despite losing the popular vote.

Supporters of the compact said they view the Electoral College as a less democratic mechanism than the popular vote. A YouGov poll conducted in June showed that 56 percent of American adults supported choosing the president via popular vote.

The compact is also more practical than amending the Constitution and popular among voters, supporters said.

Opponents and legal experts said the compact would face a slew of court challenges. One obstacle is that compacts traditionally require the approval of Congress and the president, said Derek Muller, an election law expert at Notre Dame Law School who has written about the agreement.

“It’s a very real possibility that enough states join this compact,” he said. “There will be a lot of challenges.”

Nithya Raman’s win in Los Angeles adds to the major cities with democratic socialist leaders.

Nithya Raman won the mayoral race in Los Angeles on Thursday, joining the ranks of democratic socialists who have been elected to lead major American cities over the past year.

Once she takes office, the mayors in the two most-populous American cities and the nation’s capital will be members of the Democratic Socialists of America, a political group of dues-paying members organized around a broad policy platform.

A strong emphasis on expanding public services, such as housing, health care, child care, public transportation and infrastructure, and heavily taxing corporations and wealthy individuals ties together their positions.

Here’s what to know:

Democratic socialists have been elected mayor in several cities.

In New York City, Zohran Mamdani’s election last year made him perhaps the most prominent mayor who is a member of the D.S.A. As part of his progressive agenda, he has pushed through a “rent freeze” for nearly one million rent-stabilized apartments, fought to tax high-end second homes and expanded a free child care program for 2-year-olds in partnership with Gov. Kathy Hochul.

In Los Angeles, where the mayor’s race is nonpartisan, Ms. Raman, a city councilwoman, is a member of the D.S.A. An urban planner by training, she promoted an aggressive expansion of the housing supply, advocated zoning changes and called for prioritizing public transit improvements, among other proposals. She will unseat Karen Bass, a more moderate Democrat who has served as mayor since 2022.

In Seattle, Mayor Katie Wilson, who was elected last year, has described herself as a democratic socialist but is not a D.S.A. member. In office, she has accelerated homeless shelter openings, led the passage of a major transit service expansion and rolled out new investments in preschool programs.

In Washington, D.C., Janeese Lewis George, a district councilwoman who is a member of the D.S.A., was elected mayor on Tuesday. She has called for enacting universal child care, building social housing owned by the city, strengthening tenant protections and lowering rental costs for small businesses. She is set to replace Muriel Bowser, a Democrat who is more moderate and did not seek a fourth term.

In Providence, R.I., David Morales, a state lawmaker and former professional wrestler who is also a D.S.A. member, was elected mayor on Tuesday. He easily defeated his Republican opponent, David Talan, after running a campaign focused on capping rent hikes, building affordable homes in vacant buildings and on empty lots and demanding that corporations contribute more to funding city services and public schools.

“Unfortunately, usually that happens so late that you’ll never be able to bring back a city, or a country or a state, when it goes too far,” Mr. Trump told reporters in the Oval Office.

Cait Conley flips a key N.Y. swing seat for Democrats, ousting Mike Lawler.

Cait Conley, an Army veteran and former national security official in the Biden administration, beat Representative Mike Lawler in a suburban New York swing district, according to The Associated Press, flipping a seat that Democrats had identified as a top target on their path to recapture the House majority.

Ms. Conley rode a wave of voter dissatisfaction with the policies of President Trump, whose tariffs and war in Iran have contributed to higher prices on gasoline, groceries and other essentials.

“We started this because we knew that America, the land of promise and opportunity, was on the wrong path — but we knew, if we came together, if we brought people together, that we could fix it,” Ms. Conley said late Tuesday at her election night watch party in Pleasantville. “And tonight we show that this fight wasn’t a partisan fight. This was a patriotic fight.”

The race was not called until Thursday afternoon, partly because of issues with the processing of ballots in Westchester County, which delayed the counting of tens of thousands of votes.

But long before the results were official, Ms. Conley’s strong performance in Mr. Lawler’s home base in Rockland County led the congressman to concede.

Speaking at a banquet hall in a Hilton hotel in Pearl River on Tuesday night, Mr. Lawler said that he had called Ms. Conley to congratulate her and that “when all the votes are counted tomorrow in Westchester, it will be pretty clear.”

The daughter of a postal worker, Ms. Conley grew up just over the border of the district, New York’s 17th, which includes the affluent suburbs north of New York City and narrowly favored former Vice President Kamala Harris in the 2024 presidential election. Ms. Conley moved back to run for Congress and talked up her years of military service on the campaign trail, beating two more liberal primary opponents in June.

Still, her work in the private sector after leaving government became the subject of attacks from Mr. Lawler and outside groups supporting him. They bankrolled television advertisements that portrayed Ms. Conley, a former U.S. Army special operations leader, as a corporate puppet beholden to her former employers.

Ms. Conley made $328,000 in nearly two years of private-sector work, according to financial disclosure reports, but declined to divulge the specifics of what she did for Hidden Level, an anti-drone-systems company, and for Primer AI, the firm where she consulted, citing nondisclosure agreements.

The two campaigns and outside groups backing them spent more than $60 million inundating voters with advertising over the course of the campaign, according to AdImpact, an ad tracking firm. About half of that money went to supporting Ms. Conley, with much of it spent on ads attacking Mr. Lawler as a rubber stamp for Mr. Trump. “Mike Lawler votes with Trump 100 percent of the time,” a narrator states in one of the Conley campaign ads. (An analysis by Congressional Quarterly found that in 2025, Mr. Lawler voted yes on bills Mr. Trump approved of 94 percent of the time.)

A former state legislator, Mr. Lawler shot to fame when he unexpectedly defeated Sean Patrick Maloney, the chairman of the House Democrats’ campaign arm, in 2022. That victory, and his re-election two years later, distinguished Mr. Lawler as a Republican who could win in a resoundingly Democratic state.

The congressman, who flirted with a run for governor last year, went to great lengths to emphasize his independence. He was a lone Republican voice during negotiations over Mr. Trump’s signature domestic spending bill, conditioning his support for the legislation on raising the cap on the state and local tax, or SALT, deduction. (Ms. Conley had argued that there should be no cap on the deduction, and criticized the fact that the legislation that lowered taxes also cut funding for Medicaid.)

Mr. Lawler also characterized Ms. Conley as aligned with Mayor Zohran Mamdani of New York City and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, both democratic socialists.

One exchange during the first debate of the campaign summed up the race’s fault lines. Mr. Lawler called on Ms. Conley to denounce Mr. Mamdani, whom he claimed was encouraging antisemitism.

“I absolutely call out my party when I believe that it’s wrong,” she said.

“But you don’t denounce Zohran Mamdani,” Mr. Lawler replied.

“Have you denounced Donald Trump and the harm that he has caused to our families here in the Hudson Valley?” Ms. Conley said in response.

The voting problems in Westchester County were the product of human error, officials said, but they nonetheless unsettled voters and poll workers on Tuesday. It turned out that the county had updated its ballots and the software for its ballot-reading machines to add an omitted race in Ardsley, N.Y. — but the software change had been made only in Ardsley, meaning the machines in the rest of the county were unable to read the updated ballots.

Ballots across the county were deposited into orange bags and delivered to a county Board of Elections facility in Ardsley, where a count, under the supervision of lawyers, began late Wednesday morning.

At the facility on Wednesday afternoon, a Democratic elections commissioner, Tajian Nelson, declined to give an estimate when reporters asked when the counting would be finished.

“We’re not even able to begin with the E.T.A.,” she said.

But she added that, by law, the election results must be certified within 25 days. “So based on that, we have to be certified before we have turkey at the end of the month.”

Marco Rubio rejects reports he wants to leave his jobs and run for president.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio on Thursday dismissed reports that he was planning to leave the Trump administration and declined to answer questions about his political future.

Working for President Trump until the end of his term has “always been my plan,” he said in an interview with The New York Times.

Responding to the news reports that he was mulling an exit, Mr. Rubio said, “I don’t know where these things come from, to be honest with you.”

“I love the work I’m doing,” he continued. “And plus I always laugh when they say he’s going to leave his job. I go, ‘Which one?’”

Mr. Rubio, who is also Mr. Trump’s national security adviser, declined to answer questions about whether he was considering running for president in 2028.

“I literally will not talk about 2028 anymore,” he said in the interview, in his White House office. “I just won’t do it.”

Mr. Rubio has said that he would support Vice President JD Vance if he ran for president.

“JD is a very good friend of mine,” he told NBC News in May. “If JD runs for president, I think he’d be a phenomenal candidate. I’ve said publicly, and I’ll say it again, I’ll be the first person to sign up and support him.”

Mr. Vance offered a striking critique in his post-election analysis on Wednesday in a lengthy post on social media, commentary that distanced himself from Mr. Trump. The vice president blamed the war with Iran for Republicans’ disastrous election results and said Republicans had turned against the administration’s policy on Israel and its messaging on the economy. The president has closely aligned himself with Israel and dismissed affordability concerns as a “hoax.”

Mr. Rubio initially deflected when asked whether he agreed with Mr. Vance’s assessment, saying he was not “really involved in domestic politics.”

But Mr. Trump brought Mr. Rubio onstage at his political rally on Sunday in Kansas and lavished praise on him, joking that he might have to fire him because he had become “too popular.” Mr. Rubio spoke briefly, praising Mr. Trump for keeping Americans safe.

Mr. Rubio explained why he was there, as the designated national security official traveling with the president, and then acknowledged that Mr. Trump had said the war was a factor in the elections.

“Higher prices at the gas pump, I acknowledge, is not like the kind of thing you campaign on and brag about,” he said.

But Mr. Rubio said Mr. Trump “deserves a tremendous amount of credit” for taking action that he thought was necessary even if it was not popular among voters.

“Ultimately, that is the price of leadership — is the willingness to do things that may not be politically popular or expedient but are necessary for your country and its long-term future,” he said.

Trump takes fresh aim at Alaska, where votes are still being counted.

In the lower 48 states, Democrats are exulting about election wins and Republicans are licking their wounds. But in the Last Frontier, they’re still counting the votes.

Alaska’s Senate race remained uncalled on Thursday, a product of a tight margin between the two leading candidates and the state’s 15-day grace period after Election Day to receive and count absentee ballots. Many of those ballots are arriving by mail from far-flung parts of the state. With 78 percent of the vote tallied, former Representative Mary Peltola, a Democrat, led Senator Dan S. Sullivan, a Republican, by just over 600 votes.

President Trump on Thursday injected new drama into the race’s final phase, which could stretch into next week or beyond if no candidate receives 50 percent of the vote. In a post on his social media site, Truth Social, the president took aim at the second Dan Sullivan on the ballot — a retired teacher from an Alaska fishing village whom The New York Times reported last month had been recruited into the race by allies of Ms. Peltola.

Votes for Dan J. Sullivan, as well as for Gerald Heikes, the fourth candidate on Alaska’s ballot, “should be immediately given to the REAL Dan Sullivan, and the Election is over — He easily wins,” Mr. Trump wrote. “There is no other fair solution!”

Mr. Trump’s call to interfere with the results of Alaska’s election stood in contrast to his relatively muted response to Democratic dominance in other elections this week. He told The Times that he was willing to accept the midterm results and was even looking forward to working with a Democratic-controlled Congress.

But in Alaska, Republicans have been furious for months over the presence of a second Mr. Sullivan on the ballot, an oddity that Democrats have repeatedly denied any involvement in. At Mr. Trump’s behest, the Justice Department sued unsuccessfully ahead of the election, arguing that the result should not be certified if the Republican senator lost and votes for the other Mr. Sullivan would have been enough to boost him over Ms. Peltola. The administration has filed an appeal in that case.

In Alaska’s ranked-choice voting system, candidates are ranked by voters and then eliminated in successive rounds until a winner surpasses 50 percent, with the votes for eliminated candidates reapportioned to voters’ subsequent choices. That means Republicans who mistakenly voted for the other Mr. Sullivan would still see their votes go to the incumbent once the other Mr. Sullivan was eliminated, as long as they ranked both candidates in some order.

But Republicans feared a scenario in which their voters ranked just a single candidate — the other Mr. Sullivan — and left the rest of their ballot blank, eliminating those votes once the challenger Mr. Sullivan was knocked out of contention. Data show that fewer Republicans than Democrats tend to choose second choices in ranked-choice voting.

A New York Times analysis of the available ranked-choice data from ballots tallied so far suggests that Ms. Peltola is on track to win. The remaining votes, about 60,000, are expected to favor Democrats. And the ranked-choice data suggests that the senator is unlikely to earn enough support from voters who ranked the other Mr. Sullivan or Mr. Heikes ahead of him.

In fact, voters who picked the other Mr. Sullivan as their first choice tended to select Ms. Peltola as their second choice rather than the senator, potentially undercutting the Republican argument that the other Mr. Sullivan’s backers were mostly G.O.P. voters who were confused. Still, 36 percent of voters for the other Mr. Sullivan left the rest of their ballot blank, which could include some potential Republican voters for the senator.

The Republican incumbent’s campaign expressed confidence in a memo analyzing the results on Wednesday, arguing that the senator was on track for a slim victory “despite the biggest election cheating scandal in the history of Alaska.”

Voters in four states approved bans on trans athletes on female sports teams.

In the same election that brought strong showings for Democratic candidates around the country, voters in four states chose to bar transgender athletes from competing on female sports teams.

The results, including in two states where Democrats are dominant, suggested that many voters do not view accommodations for trans athletes as part of a broad Democratic agenda. The results also appeared likely to inspire similar campaigns in future elections on an issue that has rarely been tested at the ballot box.

Since 2020, lawmakers in Republican-led states across the country have enacted laws limiting how transgender people can participate in public life. But this election was the first time voters were asked to consider new restrictions through statewide ballot measures and they approved nearly all of them. The margins were large in Arizona and Nebraska, and narrower in Colorado and Washington, where Democrats dominate.

Sponsors of the initiatives said they hoped the sports issue would be put directly to voters in other Democrat-led states. The results could also mean that decisions around broader rights for transgender people will increasingly be put before voters, political strategists said.

“To every female athlete who wondered whether her voice mattered: tonight, Washington heard you,’’ Brian Heywood, the Seattle-area hedge fund founder who financed the Washington initiative, said in a statement.

For supporters of the restrictions, the results felt like vindication.

Polls have shown that a solid majority of Americans, including roughly half of Democrats, think that transgender girls and women should not play on girls’ and women’s sports teams. Many believe that trans athletes hold a physical edge, and that their participation prevents others from having a fair chance to win, the polls indicate.

Over the past six years, 27 states enacted laws restricting the participation of transgender students in sports starting in kindergarten or middle school, extending in many cases to college. The Supreme Court upheld such bans last June.

But in nearly two dozen Democrat-led states, including Colorado and Washington, legislatures have taken no such action. That is why Erin Lee, executive director of Protect Kids Colorado, a parent advocacy group sponsoring the initiative, wanted to put the question on the ballot.

“My hope is that we can be the example of a blue state where your legislature is not following the will of the people,” she said. “Where we, the people, can do it ourselves, and hopefully encourage others to follow suit.”

Opponents of the restrictions said they viewed the election results as a major setback. Advocates for transgender rights have argued that it is important to defend trans athlete participation for reasons that extend beyond sports.

They say that treating trans girls as boys in the context of sports could set a precedent that might be applied to other areas, including access to bathrooms, identity documents and transgender health care. They also say the number of trans athletes is small, and that conservative groups have used the ballot measures as a wedge to either boost voter turnout or distract voters from more pressing economic issues.

“We know this is not over — Colorado is a testing ground,” said Nadine Bridges, co-chair of Families Not Politics, a coalition that opposed the initiative in Colorado. “What I will say is, no matter what, we will keep fighting for a Colorado where transgender kids can grow up feeling valued.”

In Washington, opponents of the initiative said its approval threatened to scapegoat a vulnerable population. A spokesman for No Hate in WA State said the new law was likely to be challenged in court.

“To all the trans kids, to all of the girls playing elementary, middle and high school sports, we care about you and there are people who will never stop fighting for you,” said Adrianna Suluai, one of the group’s leaders.

In the final weeks of the midterm election cycle, Republican campaigns and their supporters emphasized transgender issues to portray Democrats as radical and out of step. At the same time, several Democrats in close races distanced themselves from the party’s traditional support of transgender issues as a civil rights battle.

Several Democratic Senate candidates who won upsets on Tuesday in traditionally conservative states, including Annie Andrews in South Carolina, James Talarico in Texas and Josh Turek in Iowa, voiced opposition to transgender female athletes participating in girls’ and women’s sports.

Still, transgender advocates on Wednesday said the election results showed that anti-transgender messaging by Republicans had been ineffective and that the sports measures were focused on one of the thorniest issues in the national debate over the recognition of transgender identities.

“I think this shows that voters do want to see rules, they want to see guidelines in sports,” said Ari Drennan, a trans woman and founder of Pink and Blue Strategies, an L.G.B.T.Q. consulting firm. “But some of the margins on these are quite close.”

Nithya Raman, a democratic socialist, wins the Los Angeles mayoral race.

Nithya Raman, a 45-year-old democratic socialist who tapped into a widespread frustration with the quality of life in the nation’s second-largest city, will become the new mayor of Los Angeles, The Associated Press reported Thursday.

Her victory over Karen Bass, the 73-year-old incumbent, is a sweeping success for a new generation of progressive leaders and the arrival of younger Angelenos as a political force in the sprawling metropolis.

A Harvard-educated policy expert who served six years on the City Council, Ms. Raman turned the page on a liberal bastion whose leaders had been consumed with big-picture battles with the Trump administration. Instead, she zoomed in on the nuts-and-bolts distress of young voters who increasingly found themselves priced out of the famously expensive city. Polls consistently showed her ahead by a wide margin with voters under 35.

The vote also revealed a broad appetite for change among the four million residents of the city — an echo of calls for a fresh perspective that on Tuesday night propelled a Democratic wave from Maine to California. In Los Angeles, which is already deep blue, it also suggested a widespread openness to the Democratic Socialists of America, the leftist movement that helped elect New York’s mayor, Zohran Mamdani.

By Thursday, Ms. Raman was leading with 52 percent of the vote and D.S.A.-backed candidates had won races for city attorney and Congress. Members of the movement already hold three seats on the 15-member City Council. Ms. Bass called Ms. Raman, her one-time ally, to concede on Thursday morning as the ongoing vote count showed a widening lead for Ms. Raman.

“I have committed to ensuring a smooth and seamless transition,” the mayor said in a statement. “Moving this city forward is what Los Angeles needs from all of us.”

On Thursday evening, Ms. Raman, who until recently was little known outside of progressive political circles, spoke to reporters at a farmers market in the Fairfax District, surrounded by throngs of news cameras, supporters and onlooking shoppers. She wore a blood-orange leather jacket and seemed to pause in front of the microphones, as if taking in her first moments before the public as mayor-elect.

“The key priorities that we’ve heard across this entire city are homelessness, housing costs and public safety,” Ms. Raman said, adding, “These will have to be the priorities that I take on in the city.”

She sought to reassure those who voted for her opponent or opted to not participate in the election. “I think that’s a sign of the deep distrust and cynicism that has taken hold here in the city of Los Angeles,” she said. “My goal is to earn your trust.”

Ms. Raman, an immigrant from India with a screenwriter husband and two children, said her story of becoming mayor was emblematic of the opportunity the city represented to the rest of the world. “This city has opened its arms to people to come and build their dreams here,” she said. “And the idea that Angelenos voted for an immigrant to be their next mayor, at this moment in American history, is so deeply moving to me.”

Preliminary election results showed that Ms. Raman had amassed support on the city’s gentrifying Eastside and in neighborhoods dense with young people, newcomers and renters. Ms. Bass drew from homeowners on the city’s Westside and in the San Fernando Valley and historically Black neighborhoods in South Los Angeles.

In one largely Latino neighborhood — Boyle Heights — where a cold storage warehouse fire burned for days in June, filling the air with the stench of rotten meat and seafood, voters who had backed Ms. Bass during the primary election flipped and supported Ms. Raman by large margins in the general election vote.

In interviews this week, voters said they welcomed Ms. Raman’s return to basics. Anthony Nabea, 36, said that he liked her focus on city services and potholes. Marisol Medina, 52, disapproved of the money Ms. Bass had spent on the police, whose union was among her primary supporters.

Zanisha Butler, 33, said she was simply ready for a new face in the mayor’s office. “I feel like Karen Bass has run her course to be honest,” Ms. Butler said.

Others expressed concern over the loss by Ms. Bass, whom they viewed as a steady hand compared to the relatively untested Ms. Raman. Daquan Houston, 42, a truck driver in the South Los Angeles of Vermont Square, said the mayor deserved a chance to finish the work she had started.

“If you get someone new,” he said, “then they’re going to be starting from scratch.”

Overall, however, Zev Yaroslavsky, the director of the Los Angeles Initiative at the University of California, Los Angeles, and a longtime civic leader, said Ms. Raman’s victory was “a reflection of an electorate that feels the city is on the wrong track.”

An annual survey conducted early this year by the Luskin School of Public Affairs at U.C.L.A., found that residents across the county were less satisfied with their quality of life than at any point in more than a decade.

Few members of the civic establishment publicly backed Ms. Raman. But those who did cited pragmatic motives.

George Kieffer, a former chair of the University of California board of regents and a principal coauthor of the Los Angeles City Charter, said that at 78, he had decided that only a major political shake-up would wake the city bureaucracy out of its lethargy.

“My party’s establishment is caught in its own bubble,” he said. “Nithya sees it. When she jumped, I did.”

A physician assistant and native Angeleno who organized in South Los Angeles against the crack epidemic in the 1980s, Ms. Bass rose politically as a progressive outside the city’s establishment circles. When she was elected mayor in 2022 , as the city strained to emerge from the Covid-19 pandemic, she defeated Rick Caruso, a billionaire real estate developer and civic leader who spent $100 million of his own money on the campaign.

But her fortunes turned in January 2025. Traveling to Africa at the behest of the Biden administration, she was out of the country when devastating wildfires erupted.

The disaster all but leveled the star-studded Los Angeles enclave of Pacific Palisades, and many survivors never forgave her for being absent during a time of crisis. Her campaign for re-election was an uphill battle from then on.

Six months after the fire, a violent crackdown on immigrants in Los Angeles by the Trump administration set off protests that the White House then seized upon to deploy some 4,000 National Guard troops and 700 U.S. Marines into Southern California. Throughout the occupation, which lasted until the end of the year, the Republican president and his allies clashed with Ms. Bass.

The partisan tension shored up her support, but not enough to fend off a serious challenge. After Austin Beutner, a civic leader from Pacific Palisades, entered the race and then withdrew in the wake of a family tragedy, Ms. Raman jumped in.

Ms. Raman, a specialist in urban planning who had been elected to the City Council in 2020 during the Black Lives Matter movement, channeled both Mr. Mamdani and San Francisco’s more centrist mayor, Daniel Lurie. Los Angeles was “broken,” she charged, and Mayor Bass had fallen short on ensuring a basic quality of life.

Although Ms. Raman at times stumbled on camera, and had to scramble to clarify her missteps, her campaign also shrewdly deployed social media videos designed to reach younger voters. Following in the footsteps of Mr. Mamdani, she gamely appeared in spoofs of microdramas made by real Hollywood production crews and election-themed skits with D.S.A.-aligned actors and influencers. In one video, she talked about creating more shade in the urban sprawl of Los Angeles, while pretending to be burned by hot surfaces — a car door, a picnic bench.

She also distanced herself in some ways from the D.S.A., supporting changes to a local “mansion tax,” for instance, that would have benefited one of the D.S.A.’s usual targets: apartment owners. But she pledged to replace the police chief, whose aggressive crowd-control tactics during immigration protests were denounced by the D.S.A. The group also accused the Police Department of protecting federal agents.

Ms. Bass seized on Ms. Raman’s promise, calling it reckless with the Olympics coming to Los Angeles in 2028, and charged that her challenger was “untested” and “not ready for prime time.” Ms. Bass’s 2022 opponent, Mr. Caruso, came to her aid, urging voters to reject Ms. Raman and the D.S.A. Every member of the City Council expressed support of Ms. Bass, except for Ms. Raman.

None of it was enough. After a hedge fund manager and advocate for increased bicycle use in the car-crazy city, backed Ms. Raman, becoming the race’s largest donor, Ms. Raman began airing ads on Dodger games and national news shows.

As her victory became clear, lobbyists, unions, downtown businesses, nonprofits and other entrenched civic interests were already preparing for a realignment. The pressure will be on the mayor-elect, according to Mr. Yaroslavsky at U.C.L.A.

Ms. Raman, he said, “now must face the challenge of erecting a new administration and beginning to fulfill her promise to fix the city she will lead in six short weeks.”
