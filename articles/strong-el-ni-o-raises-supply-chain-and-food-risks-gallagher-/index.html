Strong El Niño raises supply chain and food risks, Gallagher warns

TheTools · 24.11.2026 03:10 · source · permalink

Gallagher is warning brokers to start supply chain and food production conversations with agribusiness clients now, ahead of what it describes as one of the strongest El Niño events on record.

In a recently published report, the broker said this year's event is on track to be one of the strongest on record. It warned of disruption from climate extremes, supply chain volatility and commodity cost pressures.

For brokers, the analysis points to a case for early scenario planning, product recall cover and faster-paying parametric options.

Gallagher cited a July forecast from the United States' National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), which gave a 97% chance of El Niño lasting into early spring 2027.

NOAA's latest update, issued on November 12, says El Niño is still strengthening. It puts the chance of a strong-to-very-strong event through January to March 2027 at more than 79%.

Closer to home, the Bureau of Meteorology's outlook for December to February says below-average rainfall is likely in parts of south-east Australia and much of the north. Above-average temperatures are very likely for most of the country.

The Bureau has also cautioned that El Niño strength does not always match its impact on Australia. Its September update noted other climate influences remain at work.

Sophie Griffin, head of Climate, Sustainability and Resilience Solutions at Gallagher Australia, said impacts can build. “In a severe El Niño year, the cumulative impact can be extensive,” she said. “Wildfires may cause immediate damage to stock and infrastructure, but they can also lead to canceled bookings, reduced tourism and significant economic losses across multiple sectors.”

Gallagher described food and agriculture as a bellwether, with problems there often spreading to energy, freight and prices.

It said the sector is already under pressure from the conflict in the Persian Gulf. Since late February, that conflict has disrupted fertiliser trade through the Strait of Hormuz and lifted nitrogen-based product prices.

Gallagher said more than one-third of global urea exports originate in the strait. Australia is exposed, with agriculture minister Julie Collins saying in April that about 60% of the country's urea typically passes through it, Reuters reported.

Gallagher Re chief science officer Steve Bowen said weather shocks can trigger knock-on effects. “When a weather or climate shock occurs, it can trigger a cascade of second-order effects — from reduced canal capacity due to lower water levels, to power constraints during heatwaves, to limitations in worker productivity,” he said.

Adam Nisenson, Gallagher's managing director for product recall, said heat and humidity can raise contamination risks in crops such as maize and peanuts. Fast-changing supply chains also leave less time to vet suppliers, he said.

Griffin said timing is a challenge for clients. “The window for putting protection in place on favorable terms sits earlier in the cycle than feels natural — often before there's anything concrete to respond to — and that's a hard call for any business juggling competing priorities,” she said.

Gallagher recommended scenario planning, business impact analysis and supplier diversification. It also pointed to parametric insurance.

James Bosley, Gallagher's global head of Parametric Solutions, made the case for faster payouts.

“In the event of a significant shock, such as a severe drought, farmers often cannot afford to wait for traditional insurance payouts,” he said.

Parametric products can pay within weeks when predefined conditions are met, he added.

Brokers advising agribusiness, food processors and freight-exposed clients may want to test those scenarios now, before the peak of summer.
