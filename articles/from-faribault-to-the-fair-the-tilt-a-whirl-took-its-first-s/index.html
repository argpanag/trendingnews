From Faribault to the Fair, the Tilt-A-Whirl took its first spin 100 years ago this summer

TheTools · 03.09.2026 13:04 · πηγή · μόνιμος σύνδεσμος

In its first year on the Fairgrounds, in 1926, the now-iconic attraction cost 15 cents to ride

When the Tilt-A-Whirl debuted at the Minnesota State Fair in 1926, the same year it was invented in a Faribault backyard some 50 miles south of the Fairgrounds, each ride cost 15 cents.

Out of the 401,000 or so people who visited the eight-day State Fair that year, 4,233 took a spin on the new contraption.

Tens of thousands more have tilted and whirled over the past 100 years, and the ride remains a popular Fair staple, though it has since moved from the high-thrill Midway to the family-friendly Kidway.

In his 1926 patent application for the Tilt-A-Whirl, Faribault woodworker and craftsman Herbert Sellner fairly dispassionately described his creation as “a new and improved amusement apparatus which will furnish pleasurable and unexpected sensations.”

Growing up the son of a respected cabinet-maker, Sellner seemed to have been known as a woodshop tinkerer. His first patent, granted in 1910, was for a sandpapering machine; his second, in 1917, outlined a particular type of foldable drop-leaf table. But his first big break came in 1922, when he created a water toboggan ride — a massive slide that riders would fly down on wooden sleds, a sort of self-service log flume — that he began selling to beachfront resorts.

He had another idea in the works, a madcap merry-go-round with the unpredictable instability of a floating raft, but his early prototypes evidently didn’t manage to nail the rotational physics he envisioned. So he sat his 10 year-old son Art in a chair he’d rigged up on caster wheels, and placed chair and boy atop an oak dining table.

To the Pioneer Press in 1992, Art’s son Bruce Sellner explained what happened next: “Here’s my dad sitting on top of this — my dad’s got a Buster Brown haircut and knickers — and my grandfather and his brother are lifting up this table to send this chair around, to figure out the angles they need to make the chair spin.”

As Herbert Sellner watched his son roll around the table, the lore goes, something clicked for him. In his basement woodshop that summer of 1926, he fabricated the components of what would become the first Tilt-A-Whirl, which he assembled in a neighbor’s backyard.

Like the water toboggan ride, to put it bluntly, the first Tilt-A-Whirl was a bit crude. It consisted of nine passenger cars on wooden octopus-style arms, with rotation powered by “a cable hooked up to a tractor engine,” as a 2006 Pioneer Press retrospective noted. The now-classic rounded shell car design wouldn’t come till later; the early cars looked like the back halves of Christmas sleighs covered with fabric canopies resembling those atop four-poster beds.

And riders were immediately enamored. Seeing the ride’s success at the State Fair, the Wildwood Amusement Park, which sat at the end of a streetcar line in White Bear Lake, immediately bought Sellner’s first Tilt-A-Whirl to install for the 1927 season. The price: $4,500, equivalent to more than $87,000 today — still much less than the estimated mid-six-figure price tag of a new Tilt-A-Whirl in 2026. (Wildwood closed in the 1930s and the fate of its history-making Tilt-A-Whirl is unknown.)

Herbert Sellner died of a blood disease in 1930, at age 43. The family business continued under Art Sellner and later Bruce Sellner, who grew up accompanying his father on business trips to Midways and carnivals and took over operations in his mid-20s.

“When I was a kid, I spent more time upside down than rightside up,” Bruce Sellner told the Pioneer Press in 1992.

Separately, Art and Bruce Sellner both died in 1995. The Sellner Manufacturing Company factory continued to produce Tilt-A-Whirls in Faribault until it was sold to a Texas company in 2011.

Even though the Tilt-A-Whirl is no longer made in its hometown, the ride has undeniable staying power a century after its Minnesota State Fair debut.

“People identify with it,” Walt Wittmer, then the vice president and general manager of Valleyfair, told the Pioneer Press in 1992. “It’s a ride that dad has grown up with; now he’s a grandfather, he passes it on to the kids. It’s a three- or four-generation ride.”
