Westpac faces downgrades after 'soft' result: JP Morgan

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

The first review of Westpac's FY26 $6.8 billion profit is in, and it isn't a 5-star, standing applause sort of take.

JP Morgan's veteran bank analyst Andrew Triggs says the result was softer than expected, with a "0.7% miss at NPAT (net profit after tax) level ex notable items, driven by weakness in core NIM (net interest margin)".

Mr Triggs said revenue was a 0.5% miss compared to JP Morgan's estimate, while expenses were 0.4% better.

"Core NIM  was very weak in 4Q26, and will likely be the focus of the conference call and driver of small EPS (earnings per share) downgrades," he said.

"Lending pressure was attributed to Australian and NZ mortgages."

Mr Triggs was also concerned by Westpac's weaker overall revenue trends.

He identified the bank's strong capital levels and credit quality as key positives, along with solid guidance on costs and an "in-line" dividend payout.

Manufacturing contraction continues

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

Activity in Australia's factories has contracted for the second consecutive month, according to S&P Global's latest Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) survey.

S&P Global noted a sharper fall in new orders drove another solid decline in output.

"In turn, goods producers scaled back both their purchasing activity and employment and also cut back on their inventory holdings as demand deteriorated," S&P Global said.

"In turn, goods producers scaled back both their purchasing activity and employment and also cut back on their inventory holdings as demand deteriorated," S&P Global said.

While the decline to 49.6 points represents only a marginal decline in activity — 50 points is the break-even point between expansion and contraction — the October PMI reading was the joint lowest since December 2024.

S&P Global economist Jingyi Pan said the survey showed manufacturers faced a "double whammy" at the start of the fourth quarter, with supply constraints piling on top of worsening demand.

"Subdued market conditions, higher interest rates and elevated prices contributed to the sharpest drop in new orders in almost two years, while shipment delays and port congestions were causes for one of the steepest extensions of delivery times since the post-COVID-19 pandemic period," she said.

"It was positive, however, to see business confidence recovering slightly from the September low amid hopes for improvements in the coming months. That said, the level of overall business optimism remained subdued by historical standards."

NT data centre scraps start date on EPA concerns

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

The developer of one of Australia's biggest planned data centres has scrapped its target start date after a regulator said it needed longer than usual to set environmental parameters, a setback to the country's ambitions to be a fast-track destination for AI investment.

Startup Energy North, which says it has finance lined up for a sprawling gas and solar-powered data centre on a cattle ranch 100 kilometres south of Daly Waters (680km south of Darwin), had given a "first power" date of June 28, 2028, in regulatory filings four months ago.

But after the Northern Territory environment regulator said in an October 7 ruling it was suspending its seven-week deadline to decide what hurdles the project must clear, the developer told Reuters it had scrubbed its timeline.

"The June 2028 date in our referral was an indicative schedule at the time of lodgement, and we're no longer working to it," said Energy North vice-president of project development Daniel Hunter in an email.

The project is the biggest in Australia to put back its timing and shows how data centre ambitions face hurdles even in the isolated north, which has sought to differentiate itself from cities in the contest for the $7 trillion McKinsey forecasts will be invested in "AI factories" globally by 2030.

The Territory is counting on luring projects with its vast supply of uninhabited land and sunshine — 260,000 residents in an area twice the size of Texas, with as much sun as the Sahara — and a huge just-opened shale gas reserve for backup power.

But stopping the clock on Energy North, the Territory's regulator said it would seek advice from the federal government, which is "developing policy expectations for data centres and AI infrastructure developers", and gave no new timeframe.

Mr Hunter said the project was unfazed and would confirm timing once the regulator sets out terms for an environmental impact statement, required to identify and address potential adverse effects. He declined to say who is lined up to finance the project.

By Stephen Letts

Halfway through Wall Street Q3 reporting season, earnings are coming in better than expected, but that has not stopped conniptions like last Friday's sell-off occurring.

But still investors are worried, and this week another wall of worry confronts them.

The quarterly results so far have intensified concerns about financing and financial returns needed to support the AI investment theme at the heart of the US bull stock market.

The questions are likely to persist, with Apple, AMD, Qualcomm and Palantir among the major tech and AI players due to report next week.

Also, the midterm elections could determine the outlook for AI regulation.

Tuesday's elections will determine whether Democrats can wrest control of Congress away from President Donald Trump's Republican party.

Democrats are widely predicted to win the House of Representatives, but the Senate is more in doubt. Republicans controlled both chambers of Congress during the first two years of the Trump administration.

Despite doubts that any viable legislation will pose serious risks to AI investment, regardless of which party wins, a Democratic sweep is expected to bring more scrutiny.

Defence and health care are other stock market areas that could be sensitive to the election results.

Binance hit by customer withdrawals after returning to trade

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

First, it was the thieves, then the customers moved in to shrink the assets of the world's biggest crypto exchange Binance.

Having just resumed trading after hackers made off with $US6.3 billion in what's being called the biggest bank heist in history, customers moved with the speed of the hackers themselves to shovel another $US3.6 billion ($5.1 billion) out the door.

That (legal) withdrawal represents more than 2% of Binance's assets.

However, that may be understating things. Crypto analytics platform DefiLlama said on its dashboard that outflows were higher, at $US4.4 billion ($6.3 billion).

Binance said withdrawals were stabilising after an initial spike and deposit volumes had remained "substantial". The exchange said it had now covered 95% of the $6.3 billion of stolen assets.

As Reuters reports, the heist, which analysts believe may be the biggest financial robbery ever, adds to a string of recent crypto thefts.

Withdrawal levels are an early test of whether customers still trust the exchange with their money after the attack.

Attackers stole $US2.9 billion ($4.1 billion) worth of cryptocurrency across nearly 150 incidents last year, according to researchers at TRM Labs.

Big tech's really bad month

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

A trillion here, a trillion there — and pretty soon you're talking serious money.

Indeed, more than $US2.6 trillion ($3.7 trillion) was wiped off the market value of the big tech stocks last month.

The Nasdaq shed about 6% as the AI trade ran into turbulence, with hyperscalers Meta Platforms, Alphabet and Amazon raising spending forecasts further as they race to expand AI infrastructure.

Alphabet suffered its biggest one-day percentage drop on record on Thursday after disappointing results in the core business of search advertising and a massive acceleration in cash burn.

Meta's plans for a record $US97.5 billion fund raise heightened concerns about shareholder dilution and whether mounting AI spending could force other Big Tech companies to increasingly tap capital markets.

Independent global investment strategist Gerard Minack says the investment bear case for AI is simple.

"The profits on selling AI services will generate a lousy return on the invested capital," Mr Minack wrote in a note to clients last week.

"There are no signs that AI providers will build the barriers to entry required to generate super-normal profits.

"Timing when the boom busts is fraught but AI stocks are no longer outperforming the market.

Wall Street tumbles on mounting AI fears, ASX on edge

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

Wall Street closed the week, maybe not in a blind panic, but there was certainly a disquieting dumping of stocks as investors fled to the exits and the weekend.

All three key indices fell sharply, the tech-centric Nasdaq fell the hardest and is down almost 8% from its record high of just three weeks ago.

  • S&P 500: 1.8%
  • Dow: -1.1%
  • Nasdaq: 2.9%

The dump saw the global MSCI down 1.5% for the day, while the pan-continental Eurostoxx 600 fell a comparatively modest 0.4%.

The tech-lite ASX is unlikely to be as affected by anxieties over cash-burning AI investments as Wall Street was on Friday. 

When trading closed on Saturday morning (AEDT) ASX 200 futures were flat, or to be precise 4 points (0.04%) lower.

The tech rout was sparked by disappointing quarterly results from Intel and Amazon released late on Thursday.

While Amazon pared losses as the session progressed, Intel shares cratered, down almost 30%.

Chip stocks veered sharply lower, with Nvidia down more than 4%.

The broader chips index shed 7.2%, slumping to a monthly loss of more than 15%. Server makers Dell and HP dropped 10.8% and 9.6%, respectively.

"It's fair to say that this week, not just today, has been very much about the future of AI and what that's going to look like, whether there will be a positive return on investment and if so, how quickly," Wealthspire adviser Oliver Pursche told Reuters.

"Companies that are performing and meeting the very lofty expectations that have been set are doing well," Mr Pursche said.

"Those that are falling short and seem to be missing the mark or not executing as expected are getting punished."

Listed crypto firms Coinbase, Strategy and Robin Hood all fell between 7 and 11%. All said they were safe, but they would say that.

Putting aside the Binance contretemps, it's worth noting that hackers have made off with about $US3 billion in crypto in around 150 incidents.

US Treasuries were one of the beneficiaries of the tech rout as investors decided they were a safer bet at the moment, which is saying something.

The benchmark 10-year yield closed the month with its biggest monthly drop in nearly three years.

Gold, once also considered a safe haven, didn't benefit from the conniptions — indeed, it slumped (-3.5%) to a one-year low, along with other precious metals. Silver shed 6.5% and palladium shed 3.0%.

"The sky is falling," independent metals trader Tai Wong told Reuters with a dramatic flourish.
