At the Venice Film Festival, TV continues to exert its influence - The Boston Globe

TheTools · 12.09.2026 19:08 · source · permalink

VENICE, Italy — The membrane between movies and television gets thinner by the day, even at major film festivals. At the Venice Film Festival (which ran from Sept. 2 to Sept. 12), the oldest festival in the world and the unofficial launching pad for movie awards season, the presence of the small screen has been an undeniable fact for a few years now. That means series premieres, and also other projects that tap into TV as a subject.

I come to Venice every year to serve on a panel with fellow critics discussing the films of the Biennale College, a selection of microbudget films from around the world that the festival develops and helps finance. But while I’m here I can’t help but drift toward television. Even with the one series in this year’s lineup, the Italian meta-showbiz comedy “Peccato,” premiering after my departure, I still had ample opportunity to consider TV’s considerable reach.

The world premiere of “Primetime” was Exhibit A. Robert Pattinson stars as Chris Hansen, who rose to fame as the host of the popular reality series “To Catch a Predator,” which aired from 2004 to 2007 on NBC and routinely ventured right up to the edge of entrapment. Luring potential online sexual predators with young actors playing bait, Hansen would then enter with a camera crew and tell said predators they were on TV and the cops were waiting outside. The film, directed by Lance Oppenheim (“Ren Faire”), tracks Hansen’s rise as a network darling and his fall after a “Predator” tragedy that won’t be revealed here since, like me, you may not remember the details.

Oppenheim, who also co-directed the Elizabeth Holmes documentary “You Can See Everything” with Nathan Fielder (it premiered with a bang at the Telluride Film Festival last Sunday), brings a deadpan thriller approach to “Primetime.” Without preaching, the film examines the blurred lines between performance and reporting, justice and spectacle, that defined “Predator” and shows of its ilk. Pattinson plays Hansen as an icy, deeply flawed, ambition-fueled missile, a pure product of television. It opens in theaters Sept. 25.

A far more heroic form of TV news can be found in the gripping documentary “My Undesirable Friends: Part II – Exile.” Like Part I, 2024’s “Last Air in Moscow,” “Exile” follows the mostly female TV journalists of TV Rain, an independent Russian television channel that embraces the journalistic mission of speaking truth to power. This, of course, is a dangerous practice in Vladimir Putin’s Russia, where the only free-flowing information is state-friendly propaganda. In “Last Air in Moscow,” currently streaming on Mubi, we see the TV Rain journalists bring journalistic rigor to stories including the Russo-Ukrainian War. They know they could be arrested at any time for bucking the official government line. As the title of “Exile” suggests, that threat is now so immediate that the Rain crew has fled to safer harbors, including Tbilisi and Istanbul, in order to keep committing journalism.

“Exile” checks in at about six hours, which means it has more in common with a bingeable TV series than a standard documentary, and Mubi will eventually offer it for streaming. But both parts of “My Undesirable Friends” speed along at an agile and engaging clip. You feel the urgency and immediacy that director Julia Loktev invests in every frame; she was born in the Soviet Union and immigrated to the US as a child, and there’s never any question that her subjects trust her intuitively. If you think life is hard right now for journalists in the US, “Exile” demonstrates just how easy it is to take a free press for granted. The TV Rain reporters are for all intents and purposes stateless. But they keep on doing the work.

In recent years some great series have premiered at Venice, including both “Disclaimer” and “Mussolini: Son of the Century” in 2024, and it’s telling that such an august cinema event has so fully embraced television. TV can’t be ignored, as a medium or as a subject. It’s in the air we breathe, even off the Adriatic Sea.
