Max Verstappen touches perfection in dramatic Las Vegas F1 GP win

For a race still in its infancy, this year’s Las Vegas Grand Prix delivered a spectacle for the ages. The victory for Max Verstappen once more exemplified why he must be acknowledged as one the greats of Formula One after a compelling race-long battle where the lead changed 34 times but on every occasion, and most important on the very last lap, it was the Red Bull driver who had his nose in front.

This was a race unique to this season and not seen in the sport for a long time, an old-school duel of slipstreaming and the sort of over-the-top extravaganza perfect for Las Vegas, Cirque du Soleil fashioned from carbon fibre and noise.

There were echoes here of that extraordinary meeting at Monza in 1971 when slipstreaming ruled the day and by the close there had been 25 changes of the lead. That was decided by the smallest margin in F1 history, just one hundredth of a second, when Peter Gethin took it on the final corner of the final lap.

There was a point during the race in Nevada when it looked as if even that estimable record might fall, with Verstappen’s lead as he crossed the line in the last few laps just one thousandth of a second. His victory margin of six tenths in the end felt like a chasm by comparison and was surely more than he expected.

The total overtakes across the field amounted to 352, which will have left F1’s clicker counter ringing with the fervour of a slot machine paying out heavily. The combination of the new regulations, the use of energy deployment, active aerodynamics in straightline mode and the lack of high-speed corners made it all but impossible for anyone to break from the field.

Drivers vied in a perfect storm lit beneath neon lightning, a long train of cars all within seconds, passing and repassing on the same lap. So close were they running that even those who dropped back were soon returned to contention, but at the front of the pack was Verstappen.

Rival after rival came at the Dutchman: Mercedes’ George Russell, Isack Hadjar in the other Red Bull, the world champion, Kimi Antonelli, and McLaren’s Lando Norris. But Verstappen remained absolutely indomitable, his skill and racecraft unmatched until he finally sealed it from Norris in second, with his McLaren teammate Oscar Piastri finishing third.

Antonelli was fourth for Mercedes and Alpine’s Pierre Gasly fifth. Russell, who vied for almost the entire race for the lead, overcooked it at the death and locked up, causing him to drop down to ninth. It was a moment Verstappen took no little pleasure in after the pair had clashed in an incident in practice earlier in the weekend.

Are you not entertained? Of course there was more, as Ferrari found a way to make it all about them too, when Charles Leclerc’s clumsy attempt to pass Lewis Hamilton led to the pair hitting one another. Hamilton went into a spin, losing places. The incident was investigated by the stewards and Leclerc was given a 10-second penalty for causing a collision.

An already somewhat fractious relationship had taken another blowafter the pair had been given a dressing-down by the Ferrari team principal, Fred Vasseur, for clashing in Mexico. This was deja vu after Hamilton had already insisted the team institute some form of rules of engagement between them, a desire Ferrari will still not meet.

Another long and urgent debrief awaits, with Vasseur on team radio adopting the tone of a schoolmaster who would strike fear into miscreant boys. “Charles, I want to see you just after the race, before everything,” he said. Which in the olden days meant a slippering at least. Especially after the team had told Leclerc not to try to overtake, five corners before he did so. Hamilton finished seventh, with his teammate 10th.

Yet for all that Ferrari were again Tom Stoppard’s Rosencrantz and Guildenstern in this drama, Verstappen was the captivating lead. Once he held first place from the off he was hunted to the flag, but no one could break his grip for more than a few corners. The pattern was established in the early laps, Russell closing on Verstappen and sweeping past on the Strip but the Red Bull immediately coming back at him, with absolute precision and execution at turn 17, the final corner, to retake the lead.

It was a dance he and other drivers would repeat time and time again as a great train of cars trailed behind them clustered together, the top 10 within five seconds as Verstappen defended his place. First Russell, then Hadjar, then Antonelli, then Norris. Every time they took the lead, Verstappen would comeback with almost breathtaking insouciance.

Purists will point to it being a factor of these cars but Sir Jackie Stewart – who enjoyed a similarly enthralling slipstream fight with Jochen Rindt in 1969 at Silverstone – would have recognised the immense skill it takes to race in this way without error.

So they circulated, to the roars of a delighted crowd, some of whom might think all F1 is like this (just don’t watch a repeat of the Madrid GP). As with all the great sporting spectacles it could not be called until the wire, and as at Monza in 1971 the final lap was all.

Verstappen entered it in the lead and he pushed hard even as Antonelli, who was losing power at the crucial moment, was passed by both McLarens – but they were then not close enough to challenge the Dutchman, who had more than earned the win. Vegas, now just four years into a tenure set to last until at least 2037, can lay claim to a bonafide classic.
