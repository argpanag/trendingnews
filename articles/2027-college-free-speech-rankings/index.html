Executive summary

For the seventh year in a row, the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (FIRE), a nonprofit organization committed to defending and sustaining the individual rights of all Americans to free speech and free thought, and College Pulse surveyed college undergraduates about their perceptions and experiences regarding free speech on their campuses.

This year’s survey includes 62,997 student respondents from 262[1] colleges and universities. Students enrolled in four-year degree programs were surveyed via the College Pulse mobile app and web portal from Jan. 2 through June 3, 2026.

Overall, the findings suggest some improvement in campus free speech climates, while also showing that many students still report difficulty discussing controversial topics and remain skeptical of allowing controversial speakers on campus.

The College Free Speech Rankings are available online and are presented in an interactive dashboard that allows for easy comparison between institutions. The full preregistered methodology is available on the Open Science Framework website.

Key findings:

  1. The University of Chicago ranks first for freedom of speech for the third time in seven years. Claremont McKenna College, last year’s top-ranked school, ranks second, followed by the Georgia Institute of Technology, Michigan Technological University, and Vanderbilt University.
  2. New York University ranks last. The University of Washington, Texas State University, Northeastern University, and Rutgers University–New Brunswick round out the bottom five.
  3. This year, the average overall score (61.36) is the highest recorded in seven years, though it still corresponds to a D- on the report’s grading scale. Overall, 121 schools still receive an F, while just 24 receive a C or better. 
  4. The University of California, Davis had the largest year-over-year increase in its overall score and now ranks 24, compared with 252 last year. Other schools with notable score increases include Harvard University, Barnard College, and Columbia University.
  5. In contrast, Texas State University had the largest year-over-year decrease in its overall score and now ranks 259, down from 112th last year. Other schools with considerable score decreases include Texas A&M University, the University of Arkansas, Utah Valley University, New York University, and Clemson University.
  6. Half of students surveyed said that the Israeli-Palestinian conflict was difficult to discuss openly and honestly on campus, down from 53% last year. Notable portions of students also identified Donald Trump (46%), Charlie Kirk (44%), abortion (43%), immigration (38%), and transgender rights (38%) as difficult topics to discuss.
  7. The percentage of students who said it was acceptable to shout down a speaker, block entry to a campus speech, or use violence to stop a campus speech declined slightly from last year’s record highs.
  8. For the second year in a row, a majority of students opposed allowing each of the six controversial speakers included in the survey — three conservative and three liberal — to speak on campus.

Overview

In 2020, in collaboration with College Pulse and RealClearEducation, the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (FIRE) launched a first-of-its-kind tool to help high school students and their parents identify which colleges promote and protect the free exchange of ideas: the College Free Speech Rankings. Over the past seven years, the annual rankings survey has captured the voices of almost 350,000 students — and over 60,000 this year alone. Whether you’re a prospective student sizing up your options, a professor curious about classroom openness, or an administrator charting a path forward, FIRE’s 2027 College Free Speech Rankings offer a data-driven look at where campus free speech is flourishing — and where it is failing.

As in previous years, the College Free Speech Rankings dashboard offers a unique tool to compare schools’ rankings and survey responses and also explore other factors — such as cost and proximity to home.

This report begins by presenting this year’s rankings, highlighting the top-performing schools and detailing why schools near the bottom perform poorly.[2] It then briefly discusses schools whose rankings improved or declined significantly over the past year before turning to an analysis of national trends that have emerged among undergraduate students over the past seven years.

How the rankings are calculated

Each school’s overall score is the sum of 12 components. Six measure student perceptions, three assess campus speech policies, and three evaluate how schools respond to speech controversies. Higher scores indicate a better climate for free speech and expression.

The six survey-based components are:

  • “Comfort Expressing Ideas” measures how comfortable students are expressing controversial views in five campus settings.
  • “Self-Censorship” measures how often students withhold their views in conversations with students, professors, and administrators, and during classroom discussions.
  • “Disruptive Conduct” measures students’ acceptance of shouting down speakers, blocking access to events, and using violence to stop campus speech.
  • “Administrative Support” measures whether students believe administrators clearly protect free speech and would defend a speaker during a controversy.
  • “Openness” measures how many of 22 social and political topics students identify as difficult to discuss openly and honestly.
  • “Political Tolerance” measures students’ willingness to allow six controversial speakers on campus — three offensive to liberals and three to conservatives. The score rewards overall tolerance and tolerance across ideological lines.

The three policy components are the Chicago Statement, which recognizes an explicit commitment to free expression; Institutional Neutrality, which recognizes a commitment to avoid taking positions on social and political issues unrelated to a university’s mission; and Spotlight Rating, which evaluates written policies regulating student expression.

The remaining components — Campus Deplatformings, Scholars Under Fire, and Students Under Fire — account for institutional responses to speech controversies. Schools may receive bonuses for clearly defending free expression or penalties when administrators restrict speech. These bonuses and penalties decrease over time.

The full preregistered methodology explains the scoring process in greater detail.[3]

2027 College Free Speech Rankings

The release of the 2027 College Free Speech Rankings bookends a jarring year for free expression on campus. One day after the release of last year’s rankings, conservative activist and Turning Point USA founder Charlie Kirk was assassinated at Utah Valley University, doing what he often did: engaging in open political debate.[4] His assassination had a profound impact on students nationwide. A FIRE survey of 2,028 college undergraduates conducted last October found that half of the students surveyed felt less comfortable attending or hosting controversial public events on their campus than before Kirk’s assassination. About half also said they were less comfortable expressing their views on controversial topics in class, in common campus spaces, and on social media. One in five even said they were less comfortable attending class in person.[5]

Charlie Kirk’s assassination also prompted increased pressure from public officials to sanction faculty speech, primarily in response to social media comments about his death.[6]

For example, Indiana Attorney General Todd Rokita launched an Eyes on Education platform, which is “designed to give parents, students, and educators a way to share examples of what is being taught and promoted in schools and to provide transparency for parents and keep Hoosiers informed.”[7] Multiple submissions to this platform concern comments made by college and university faculty about Kirk’s political views and his assassination. Alabama’s U.S. Sen. Tommy Tuberville took a more direct approach, demanding that the Huntsville and Tuscaloosa campuses of the University of Alabama fire multiple professors for their comments about Kirk’s death on social media.[8]

Elsewhere, Clemson University condemned the expression of two professors — Joshua Bregy and Melvin Earl Villaver Jr. — who made controversial social media posts in response to Kirk’s death, but initially resisted punishing them while emphasizing its commitment to the First Amendment. After U.S. Rep. Nancy Mace and South Carolina Attorney General Alan Wilson amplified public backlash, Clemson reversed course and decided to terminate both professors.[9] Similarly, the University of Tennessee capitulated to demands from state and federal lawmakers to fire professor Tamar Shirnian because of a social media post she made following Kirk’s assassination.[10] While at Fort Hays State University, professor Nuchelle Chance was placed on administrative leave after the Kansas Republican Party condemned her social media comments and demanded that the university terminate her.[11]

Other on-campus censorship efforts focused on a different target: Turning Point USA, the organization Charlie Kirk founded and led. These efforts came primarily from student governments. Attempts to establish TPUSA chapters at Brown University,[12] Duquesne University,[13] Fort Lewis College,[14] Point Loma Nazarene University,[15] the University of Tennessee in Chattanooga,[16] and Valparaiso University[17] were all rejected by student governments on those campuses.

Other campus actors also imposed obstacles on TPUSA-related activity. At the University of Maryland, College Park, campus police refused to approve a TPUSA event featuring The Daily Wire’s senior editor Cabot Phillips unless the organization paid for four hours of security, despite no such requirement for similar past events.[18] At Concordia University in Wisconsin, an administrator pressured students attempting to establish a TPUSA chapter on campus to denounce the national organization’s “Professor Watchlist” in the group’s charter as a condition for being recognized as a student organization.[19] At Manchester Community College, administrators required a student affiliated with the campus TPUSA chapter to relocate his tabling display to a “free speech zone” because of its political content.[20] At Barry University, administrators denied the recognition of a campus TPUSA chapter but later overturned the decision under pressure from the Florida attorney general.[21]

All of these incidents and more occurred after Charlie Kirk’s assassination, and they help illustrate why student perceptions of openness, tolerance for controversial speakers, and confidence in administrative support for speech remain central to assessing campus free expression. They also unfolded amid the ongoing efforts of the Trump administration to eliminate diversity, equity, and inclusion programs in higher education,[22] as well as attempts in a number of states to influence the higher education curriculum through laws regulating the teaching of “divisive topics.”[23]

Against this backdrop, FIRE releases its 2027 College Free Speech Rankings. Despite these conditions, the report contains some positive developments.

  • This year, the average overall score (61.36) is the highest recorded in seven years, though it still corresponds to a D- on the report’s grading scale. 
  • Overall, 121 schools receive an F, while just 24 receive a C or better. But this is an improvement compared to last year, when 166 schools received an F and just 11 received a C or better. 
  • Some schools, including the University of California, Davis, Harvard University, Columbia University, and Barnard College, considerably improved their overall scores and rankings. 

Still, the average score corresponds to a D- on the report’s grading scale; a majority of students opposed allowing each of the six controversial speakers asked about in the survey — three conservative and three liberal — to speak on campus; and more than two-thirds of students said it was acceptable to shout down a speaker on campus. In calendar year 2025, FIRE recorded a record number of campus speech controversies — including deplatforming attempts and attempts to sanction scholars and students for their expression.[24] These included a record number of event disruptions and attempted disruptions,[25] and the termination of at least 20 professors for their social media posts commenting on Charlie Kirk’s assassination.[26]

The best- and worst-ranked schools for freedom of speech

For the third time in seven years, the University of Chicago is the top-ranked school for free speech, with an overall score of 81.42 and a speech climate grade of B-. Claremont McKenna College (80.13), last year’s top-ranked school, is ranked second. The Georgia Institute of Technology (78.98), Michigan Technological University (78.72), and Vanderbilt University (77.09) round out the top five. Like UChicago, CMC has a speech climate grade of B-, while the rest of the schools ranked in the top five have a C+.

At the other end of the spectrum, New York University ranks last, with an overall score of 44.97. This comes after finishing as the third-worst school last year. The University of Washington (47.50), Texas State University (47.82), Northeastern University (49.78), and Rutgers University (49.91) round out the bottom five. The University of Washington and Northeastern were both ranked in the bottom five last year as well. All of these schools receive a failing grade of F for their speech climate.

The contrasts between the best and worst schools are stark.

The average overall score of schools in the top five is 79.27, compared with an average of 47.99 among the bottom five schools — a difference of almost five standard deviations. Furthermore, the average ranking across the different survey-based components among the top five was 47 — including an average ranking of 12 on “Administrative Support” and 16 on “Political Tolerance.” The lowest average ranking among these schools on any of the survey-based components was 79 on “Disruptive Conduct.”

Things are considerably different at schools in the bottom five, whose average ranking on the survey-based components is 178 — including 210 on “Administrative Support” and 211 on “Disruptive Conduct.” The only survey-based component on which schools in the bottom five rank above the median is “Openness,” with an average ranking of 101.

The story is similar when comparing the top five and bottom five on the behavioral metrics of the rankings. All of the top five schools have a “green light” rating for their speech policies[27] and have endorsed the Chicago Statement issued in 2015.[28] Four of these five schools — Michigan Tech being the exception — have also adopted a commitment to institutional neutrality.[29] Additionally, none of them incurred any penalties for speech controversies on their campuses.

In contrast, the bottom five schools all lost points due to their speech policies and the outcomes of speech controversies that occurred on their campuses. None of the schools have endorsed the Chicago Statement or adopted a stance of institutional neutrality.

Northeastern’s speech policies earn a “red light” rating.[30] The remaining schools in the bottom five all have “yellow light” ratings.[31] Worse, these schools collectively lost 41 points due to the outcomes of different speech controversies on their campuses.[32]

The University of Chicago and Claremont McKenna College earned the two highest scores ever: 81.42 and 80.13, respectively. This is encouraging, but those scores are also equivalent to a grade of B-. The average score among the top five schools is a C+ (79.27), or slightly “above average.”

So, even the best schools for free speech have plenty of work still to do to create a truly open environment for expression on their campuses. Not all of that responsibility rests with colleges. Campus speech climates also reflect broader cultural forces, including political polarization, fear of social consequences, and declining tolerance for disagreement.

At the same time, the best schools are clearly doing a superior job compared with the worst ones. The bottom five have an average overall score of 47.99 — they all receive failing grades, and their overall scores are more than two standard deviations below the nationwide average.

The problem is not just the speech policies at these schools; it is also how they handle speech controversies on campus and how the students interpret those actions.

In the span of a few weeks this past spring, Rutgers — after multiple students attempted to disrupt a talk by Yossi Hertz, a former Israel Defense Forces soldier — first prominently declared its commitment to free speech, stating that it “does not cancel events or speakers based on disagreements with their messages, however heartfelt such disagreements may be.”[33]

Then, a week or so later, Rutgers rescinded the School of Engineering’s invitation to alumnus Rami Elghandour, chairman and CEO of Arcellx, to deliver the school’s commencement address. The university acted after some graduating students objected to Elghandour’s social media posts about the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and indicated they would skip the ceremony. This time, a university spokesperson said that the “decision keeps the focus on our engineering students and honors the celebratory spirit of the event to ensure that no graduate feels forced to choose between their personal convictions and a convocation ceremony.”[34]

Those two statements contradict each other and exemplify leadership that sends mixed messages to students and faculty on campus about which kinds of expression are welcome and which are not.

Other patterns are evident in how the bottom five schools handle speech controversies:[35]

  • Texas State University administrators apparently fear state and federal government crackdowns.
  • The University of Washington appears to struggle to ensure that campus events go off without a hitch.
  • New York University is particularly concerned with events that discuss the Israeli-Palestinian conflict. 

Leadership matters. Sending consistent messages to students and faculty matters.

This past year, NYU directed Jews Against Zionism, a Jewish student group that is critical of Israel’s actions in Gaza, to remove a social media post.[36] It also prevented students organizing an annual concert from inviting performers affiliated with the No Music For Genocide movement.[37] Last fall, it initially canceled an appearance by Ilya Shapiro, a law professor supportive of Israel’s actions in Gaza, that was organized by the university’s Federalist Society because it was scheduled for Oct. 7, 2025, and then reversed its decision following public backlash.[38]

There are clear reasons why some schools do relatively well in FIRE’s College Free Speech Rankings, and also clear reasons why other schools do not — and those reasons are reflected not only in a school’s behavioral metrics but also in its survey data assessing student perceptions.

The top five schools perform well on almost every student-perception metric, particularly on “Administrative Support.” The bottom five do not, and they score particularly poorly on “Administrative Support.”

Simply put: Dear administrators, the students notice.

Risers and fallers

DePauw University ranked last in the first two years of the College Free Speech Rankings.[39] The university’s president then reformed the school’s speech policies, and DePauw began to improve.[40] This year, it ranks 25.

The University of South Carolina ranked 246 out of 248 schools in the 2024 College Free Speech Rankings.[41] After a new chancellor initiated speech policy reform, endorsed the Chicago Statement, and refused to cancel a roast of Kamala Harris hosted by Milo Yiannopoulos and Gavin McInnes in fall 2024, student responses improved, and the university ranked 34 the following year.[42] This year, it ranks 14.

The story is similar at Vanderbilt University, which ranks 5, and Dartmouth College, which ranks 6.

In other words, improvement in the rankings — even rapid improvement — is possible. It starts with strong leadership on the issue of free expression.

This year, the schools with the largest increases in overall score were:

  • The University of California, Davis, whose score increased by almost 18 points primarily because of its adoption of the Chicago Statement, a commitment to institutional neutrality, obtaining a “green light” rating for its speech policies, and the decay of penalties imposed for older speech controversies. 
  • Harvard University, whose score increased by over 13 points due to significant improvements in student perceptions — particularly on “Comfort Expressing Ideas” and “Administrative Support” — and because penalties imposed for older speech controversies decayed, while not being supplemented with poor responses to fresh controversies.
  • Barnard College, whose score increased by over 12 points mostly due to policy reforms.
  • The Georgia Institute of Technology, whose score increased by almost 9 points because of improved student perceptions on “Disruptive Conduct” and “Administrative Support,” and its adoption of the Chicago Statement and a commitment to institutional neutrality.
  • Columbia University, whose score increased by over 8 points because of improved student perceptions on “Comfort Expressing Ideas,” “Self-Censorship,” and “Openness,” and because penalties imposed for older speech controversies decayed.

Other schools that improved their scores by more than one standard deviation this year, compared with last year, included: Utah State University, Dartmouth College, the University of Alabama, Furman University, Clark University, and Tufts University.

On the other hand, a number of schools had their overall scores decline considerably compared with last year’s rankings:

  • Texas State University, whose score decreased by over 11 points, primarily due to its poor handling of numerous campus speech controversies described in Table 1 of this report’s appendix.
  • Texas A&M University, whose score decreased by almost 7 points because student perceptions on “Administrative Support” declined significantly and the university incurred additional penalties for its handling of multiple speech controversies. Some of those controversies made national headlines, including the termination of Melissa McCoul, a senior lecturer in the Department of English at the university, who came under scrutiny after a student secretly recorded part of her Literature for Children class that included discussion of gender identity and a “Gender Unicorn” slide, and then challenged McCoul, claiming the material was “illegal” under state policy.[43]
  • The University of Arkansas, whose score decreased by almost 6 points because of three separate incidents in which the university sanctioned faculty speech, including its decision to rescind an offer to Emily Suski, a professor of law and associate dean for strategic and institutional priorities at the University of South Carolina’s Joseph F. Rice School of Law, to become the dean of the university’s School of Law.[44]
  • New York University, whose score decreased by almost 5 points primarily because of penalties incurred for its handling of a number of speech controversies, each of which is described in Table 1 of this report’s appendix. NYU’s "Administrative Support” also declined.
  • Clemson University, whose score decreased by almost 5 points due to its termination of two professors — Joshua Bregy and Melvin Earl Villaver Jr. — who made controversial social media posts in response to Charlie Kirk’s assassination.[45]

Other schools that experienced notable declines in their overall scores included the University of Minnesota, the University of Florida, California Polytechnic University, and the University of North Texas.

A chilling effect at Utah Valley University

Charlie Kirk was assassinated at a Turning Point USA event at Utah Valley University on Sept. 10, 2025. Later that day, UVU President Astrid S. Tuminez addressed the campus community through an Instagram post:[46]

We grieve with our students, faculty, and staff who bore witness to this unspeakable tragedy. He was invited by the student group Turning Point USA to speak on our campus. We firmly believe UVU is a place to share ideas and to debate openly and respectfully. Any attempt to infringe on those rights has no place here. We do not condone any form of violence at UVU and seek to make our campus a safe place for all.

The university then responded quickly. Classes were canceled for the rest of the week and counseling services were expanded. New student organizations promoting tolerance popped up, and the university later launched a civic dialogue program — “Our Better Selves for a Better America.”[47]

A FIRE survey of 2,028 students nationwide conducted in the month after the assassination included 204 UVU undergraduates and found that 94% of them said it was “never” acceptable for students to use violence to stop a campus speech, compared with 81% of UVU students who said this the previous spring. That same month, UVU also invited FIRE President and CEO Greg Lukianoff to deliver a lecture titled “Free Speech: An Antidote to Violence.” Lukianoff emphasized that words are not violence and that free speech is the greatest antidote humanity has to actual violence.[48]

Some students appeared to notice the administration’s efforts. This year, about half of UVU students (48%) said it was “very” or “extremely” clear that their administration protects free speech on campus, while just 12% said that it was “not very” or “not at all” clear. Last year, these percentages were 42% and 12%, respectively. Improvement was even more evident when students were asked how likely it was their administration would protect speech rights during a controversy. Last year, 29% of UVU students said it was “very” or “extremely” likely their administration would do so, while 16% said it was “not very” or “not at all” likely. This year, those percentages were 42% and 15%, respectively. In terms of the College Free Speech Rankings, UVU’s “Administrative Support” score increased compared with last year, and the university ranks 24 on this component. 

Yet, it is also evident that the assassination has had negative effects on the campus speech climate.

In that same survey conducted in the month following the assassination, UVU students said that after what happened to Charlie Kirk, they were less comfortable expressing views on a controversial political topic during a class discussion, in a common campus space, or to other students on social media than their peers nationwide. UVU students were also considerably less comfortable attending public events on campus and even attending class. The percentage of UVU students reporting self-censorship at least a few times a week also increased after Kirk’s assassination, compared with the percentage of UVU students who reported this in the spring of 2025.[49]

Then this past spring, the campus chapters of TPUSA and the College Republicans, state and federal politicians, and members of the general public all opposed the university’s announcement of Sharon McMahon, an author, educator, and podcaster, as its commencement speaker because of a series of social media posts she had made criticizing Charlie Kirk after his assassination.[50] In response to the backlash, UVU rescinded McMahon’s invitation to deliver the commencement address, citing “increased safety concerns.”[51]

Other student survey responses reinforced these experiences.

Charlie Kirk was the most difficult topic to discuss on UVU’s campus, with 62% of students identifying it as difficult to discuss; 44% of students nationally said this. Other topics identified by a notable portion of UVU students included: abortion (54%), Donald Trump (53%), transgender rights (53%), gun control (51%), gay rights (46%), and the Israeli-Palestinian conflict (42%). In this year’s College Free Speech Rankings, UVU declined noticeably on “Openness,” and ranks 251 on this component compared to 183 last year.

Additionally, the percentage of UVU students who said they self-censor frequently, at least a few times a week, also increased. Last year, roughly a quarter of UVU students said they self-censored frequently in conversations with other students (24%), in conversations with professors (27%), and during classroom discussions (24%). As with “Openness,” UVU’s score on “Self-Censorship” noticeably declined, and the university ranks 230 on this component compared to 96 last year. 

About half of UVU students (51%) surveyed this year reported that they were “somewhat” or “very” uncomfortable expressing their views on a controversial political topic during a class discussion, compared with 40% last year. The result was essentially identical for expressing views on a controversial political topic in a common campus space, with 52% of UVU students saying they were “somewhat” or “very” uncomfortable doing so this year, compared with 40% last year. Like its scores on “Openness” and “Self-Censorship,” UVU’s score on “Comfort Expressing Ideas” declined, and the university ranks 153 compared to 23 last year. 

Overall, UVU ranks 210 out of 261 schools — down from 71 out of 257 last year — and its overall score declined compared with last year. This placement is painful but understandable: A public figure was assassinated on campus in front of many students engaging in free speech and open debate. As several liberal and conservative UVU students told us (see student quotes above) — the chilling effect of this assassination persists. Charlie Kirk was the most difficult topic to discuss on campus. Comfort expressing views is down, self-censorship is up. 

There is no doubt that the speech climate at UVU is currently a challenging one. The administration has made some laudable efforts, but its decision to rescind Sharon McMahon’s commencement invitation was a mistake. Rather than deplatforming McMahon, UVU should respond to every instance of and every call for censorship with a clear defense of free expression. 

National trends: A chilling climate for campus speech

After seven years of surveying almost 350,000 college undergraduates nationwide, a sobering picture has emerged: Students are reluctant to speak their minds, especially on controversial political issues. Many report that they self-censor regularly, avoid certain topics entirely, and doubt their administrators would defend free expression if controversy struck.

This year, many students continue to report difficulty discussing a number of important social and political issues openly and honestly. Significant numbers also believe it’s acceptable to shout down a speaker, block access to events, or even resort to violence to silence campus speech. Many also show low tolerance for controversial speakers.

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict remains the most taboo topic on campuses across the country for the third year in a row. This year, half of students nationwide identified this topic as difficult to discuss, down from 53% last year and 55% two years ago. This year, students were also reluctant to talk about two public figures, Charlie Kirk and Donald Trump, and reported increased concern discussing immigration. As in prior years, abortion and transgender rights were identified as difficult topics.

Last year, we reported that the percentages of students who said it was acceptable for other students to shout down a speaker, block entry to an event, or use violence to stop a campus speech were all at record highs in the history of the College Free Speech Rankings.[52] This year, each of these percentages declined slightly — although they each remain at disturbingly high levels.

Finally, for the second year in a row, a majority of students opposed allowing each of the six controversial speakers included in the survey — three liberal and three conservative — to speak on campus.

Conclusion: Reasons for cautious optimism 

This year’s average overall score (61.36) is the highest ever recorded in seven years. Moreover, 140 schools received passing grades, compared to 91 last year. These results offer reasons for cautious optimism.

At the same time, the top two schools — the University of Chicago and Claremont McKenna College — earned the two highest scores ever, 81.42 and 80.13, respectively, and both scores correspond to a B-. The average score among the top five schools is a C+ (79.27). Thus, even the “best” schools for free speech still have plenty of work to do to create a truly open environment for expression on their campuses. When the scope is expanded to all of the 261 schools ranked this year, just 24 receive a C or better, and 121 of them still receive a failing grade.

These results are not solely a reflection of college policies or administrators. Campus speech climates are also shaped by broader cultural forces, including political polarization, intolerance of disagreement, and fear of social consequences. Colleges cannot fully control the attitudes students bring to campus, but their policies, leadership, and responses to controversy can either strengthen or weaken the culture of free expression.

In other words, while there have been improvements at a number of schools, the overall state of affairs for free speech on American college and university campuses remains very concerning. 

Over the past seven years, it has become apparent that strong leadership at colleges and universities is needed to establish at the very least the basic conditions of an open expressive environment. Schools that clearly and consistently support First Amendment protections and do the best they can to establish a campus environment that prioritizes open expression perform better in FIRE’s College Free Speech Rankings. This comes not just from sending messages to students and faculty on campus that the school values free and open expression, but also from consistently demonstrating that they mean what they say on this issue when controversy inevitably erupts.

We do not think Harvard’s improvement this year — it ranked 245 last year after finishing dead last in the 2024 and 2025 rankings — is a coincidence.[53] Its new leadership has made its support of the First Amendment clear.[54] The school’s rise in the rankings is the result of a significant improvement in its score on “Administrative Support,” the decay of penalties imposed for older campus speech controversies, and the absence of new penalties for new controversies.

In many ways, Harvard’s story is similar to those of other schools over the past seven years. DePauw University, the University of South Carolina, Vanderbilt University, Dartmouth College, and others have reformed their policies and then, when faced with controversy, continued to stand firm in their reinvigorated support for the First Amendment.

This is the way. 

We hope more colleges and universities start to agree with us in the coming years.

FIRE

The Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression (FIRE) is a nonpartisan, nonprofit organization dedicated to defending and sustaining the individual rights of all Americans to free speech and free thought. These rights include freedom of speech, freedom of association, due process, legal equality, religious liberty, and sanctity of conscience — the most essential qualities of liberty. FIRE also recognizes that colleges and universities play a vital role in preserving free thought within a free society. To this end, we place a special emphasis on defending these rights of students and faculty members on our nation’s campuses.
