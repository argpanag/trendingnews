Trump and leaders of Denmark and Greenland announce security pact to be signed next week

Donald Trump’s threatened annexation of Greenland appears to be off the table, as the US president and the leaders of Denmark and Greenland announced on Friday that they expect to sign an Arctic security pact next week during the United Nations general assembly in New York City.

Trump cast the deal as a personal triumph, claiming on social media that the agreement “gives the United States permanent control over security, and all other needs, in Greenland, completely addressing ALL of our many U.S. concerns”.

“We will immediately begin the process of developing a large Military presence in the appropriate part of Greenland,” Trump added without mentioning that the US already has a presence on the island, and the right to expand bases, through Nato.

Statements from Denmark’s prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, and the leader of Greenland’s government, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, posted online late Friday, emphasized that the agreement would fall far short of Trump’s stated desire for “the acquisition of Greenland by the United States”. The island is a self-governing territory of the Kingdom of Denmark.

Frederiksen said that what she called “a great agreement” for all three parties, “recognizes the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom and the Greenlandic people’s right to self-determination”.

Nielsen said it was “joyous” that the agreement “ensures and strengthens the security of Greenland, the Kingdom of Denmark, the United States and the Western alliance”, suggesting that the deal might possibly just reiterate the existing US rights through Nato to use the island as a security base.

Trump to meet Venezuelan leader Delcy Rodríguez next week

Donald Trump plans to meet next week with Delcy Rodríguez, who became president after a US military raid in January captured then-leader Nicolás Maduro and brought him to the United States to face drug trafficking charges.

Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the United Nations, told reporters that Trump and Rodriguez will take part in what’s known as a “pull-aside” meeting – a more casual meeting than a formal bilateral sit-down – during the United Nations general assembly in New York City.

Trump will meet with the new UK prime minister, Andy Burnham, for the first time, according to Waltz, who as Trump’s national security adviser until he mistakenly added the editor of the Atlantic magazine to a private Signal group chat in which the defense secretary, Pete Hegseth, briefed senior officials, including the vice-president, JD Vance, on planned strikes on Houthi rebels in Yemen.

The Iran-backed rebels Yemeni fighters recently seized control of the Bab al-Mandab strait – the other major regional energy chokepoint after the strait of Hormuz.

Trump also plans to meet the leaders of Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait and Bahrain, which are on the front lines of the war he started with Iran nearly seven months ago.

The first meeting between Trump and Rodríguez, who had been Maduro’s vice-president, comes after the two countries announced an agreement last month to give the US a stake in Venezuela’s oil reserves.

Donald Trump on Friday signed legislation enabling sweeping sanctions on Russia aimed at pressing it into ending its war with Ukraine, and also giving him the power to impose additional tariffs on countries breaking the embargo.

The US president signed the Lindsey O Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act into law two days after it passed the House of Representatives in a bipartisan 262-159 vote, amid concerns the legislation gives him too much power.

The legislation imposes new sanctions on Russian political and military officials and foreign networks helping to supply Russia’s war machine and evade existing sanctions.

In addition, it targets Russia’s fleet of “shadow tankers”, which have allowed the country to continue to ship crude around the world despite existing sanctions on its energy exports.

But it also gives Trump wide leeway over its implementation, including which countries could face tariffs, and which tariff rates could be imposed. It also gives him sweeping discretion over whether sanctions provisions can be waived against certain countries.

Controversially, it allows Trump to impose tariffs of up to 100% on buyers of Russian energy, including China, and on other countries that help Moscow to evade sanctions.

The bill’s broad provisions were criticized by many Democrats, who complained that it gave Trump excessive authority to resort to tariffs, which he has already imposed on multiple countries despite a US supreme court ruling prohibiting him from doing so under the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act.

In chaotic news conference, Trump refers to barring CNN, Politico and MS Now as his 'ban on the free press'

Near the end of a chaotic news conference in the Oval Office on Friday, when a reporter tried to bring Donald Trump back to the subject of his move to ban CNN, Politico and MS Now from the White House, over unflattering coverage he referred to as “fake”, the president seemed to admit that what he is attempting to do is a violation of the freedom of the press enshrined in the US constitution.

“On the ban, what does that mean in practice?” the reporter asked. “Are you taking away their badges? Are you banning them from the pool? What does this actually mean?”

“When you say ‘the ban’, which ban?” Trump replied. “Do you know how many bans I’m involved in? I’ve got more bans going than any human being!”

“On the three news outlets, sir, what does that mean in practice?” the reporter clarified.

Looking genuinely confused, Trump said: “Wh- I still don’t know what she’s”, before turning for help to those around him.

NBC News correspondent Kelly O’Donnell then rephrased the question, asking: “What do you envision the bans would look like on the media outlets?” Trump said: “Oh, the ban on the free press, right?”

“Uh”, Trump said, “a very simple ban. I don’t want them in my office. I don’t want them here. I would say the ban would go as far as you can. And if they could straighten themselves out, you know, if they could straighten themselves out, I’d love it. Look, they’re very dishonest people. They write in a very dishonest way.”

He then went on to give an inaccurate account of the settlements he extracted from ABC News and CBS News after suing them over coverage he objected to, and, not for the first time, falsely claimed that the BBC documentary on the January 6 2021 Capitol riot he has sued the corporation over used technology to put words in his mouth that he never actually said. “They put horrible fighting words into my mouth,” Trump claimed, incorrectly. “BBC actually changed my language and they put words in my mouth.”

“We’ll be suing these people too,” he added, in apparent reference to the three outlets he banned from the White House on Friday.

Trump says that his son paid back Kremlin-linked Russian who covered wedding party costs

Asked by a reporter in the Oval Office on Friday if it was appropriate for Umar Kremlev, a Russian linked to the Kremlin, to have paid for part of Donald Trump Jr’s wedding celebration in the Bahamas, Donald Trump said “it’s totally allowed. But as I understand it, he paid him back.”

“A person that he knows pretty, I guess a friend of his, gave him a wedding party. That’s very common, I’ve heard of many people giving wedding parties,” Trump added.

When the reporter seemed to suggest that a Putin-connected boxing official was not just a routine friend to make such a gift, Trump replied: “Well, I don’t know, maybe he didn’t do a background check. But I’ve given wedding parties to numerous people.”

“With all of that being said, I understand that Don’s paid him back,” Trump added.

Trump defends banning CNN, MS Now and Politico from White House: 'I don't think a court should allow fake news'

At an Oval Office event to promote changes he says he’s making to the US healthcare system, including pressing for lower drug prices and reducing vaccine doses for children, Donald Trump defended his decision to ban CNN, MS Now and Politico from the White House.

Reminded by the NBC News correspondent Kelly O’Donnell, a past president of the White House Correspondents’ Association, that “every president takes an oath to uphold the constitution, which includes the first amendment,” the president said, that he does, “much more so than you,” before adding: “you get so tired of reading and seeing fake news.”

“There may be others to join them,” Trump warned, before hinting a short time later that the New York Times and the Washington Post are on his list.

“Our country has to have honest news,” the president said.

“You need a free press,” Trump added. “You need a fair press and an honest press.”

When another reporter reminded Trump that he had lost in court in 2018 when he tried to ban CNN’s Jim Acosta from the White House, Trump tried to rewrite history by claiming that “we didn’t have to take it too far because the reporter that you’re talking about discredited himself.” In fact, after a judge ordered the White House to restore Acosta’s access, the first Trump administration relented immediately.

“I don’t think that a court should allow fake news to be written day after day after day,” Trump went on. “I think that somebody has a right to keep them away if they’re going to write false stories all the time,” he insisted, apparently failing to understand a fundamental part of the US constitution.

Trump then went on to reveal that what he is mainly upset about is the tenor of coverage of him not being positive enough from non-partisan media outlets, while praising partisan, rightwing outlets.

“I get I don’t get fair press. I get it from Breitbart. I get it from a little bit from Fox,” Trump said. “But what I do ask is just a semblance of truth.”
