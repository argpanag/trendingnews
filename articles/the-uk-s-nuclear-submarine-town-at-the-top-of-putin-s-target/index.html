The UK’s nuclear submarine town at the top of Putin’s target list

TheTools · 07.09.2026 11:00 · source · permalink

Helensburgh residents living next to nuclear submarines find rising tensions ‘worrying’, but hope upgrades at Faslane can bring an economic boost

Russia’s veiled threats against the UK feel more tangible in Helensburgh, the Scottish coastal town nestled close by one of Britain’s most important military sites.

Residents walking along the seafront are well aware that the UK’s nuclear-armed submarines slide silently up and down its open waters.

HMNB Clyde, better known as Faslane, is home to the submarines that carry the Trident weapons system, the nation’s nuclear deterrent.

BBC presenter Maryam Moshiri reveals blood cancer diagnosis

BBC News presenter Maryam Moshiri has revealed she has been diagnosed with an incurable type of blood cancer.

What happened?

In an interview with The Times, Moshiri said she has been diagnosed with an incurable type of blood cancer.

The presenter said she has been undergoing treatment for polycythaemia vera since her diagnosis in November 2024.

What is polycythaemia vera (PV)

PV thickens the blood by causing bone marrow to continuously produce red blood cells.

What are the risks?

This increases the risk of blood clots, which can cause stroke, heart attack or deep vein thrombosis.

The symptoms

Symptoms can include headaches, confusion, dizziness and blurred vision.

What treatment did she receive?

Her treatment involved a procedure called a venesection, where almost a pint of blood is removed through a vein about twice a month to reduce the red blood cell concentration.

Immunotherapy treatment reduced the frequency of Moshiri’s venesections but initially came with “horrendous” side effects including insomnia, headaches and itching.

How UK’s first ‘super-university’ can tackle Neets crisis

The head of the UK’s first “super-university” has said that merging two universities can help reduce the number of people not in employment, education or training (Neet).

A new ‘super-university’

What is it?

The newly formed London and South East University Group (Lase) is now the third largest university in the UK.

How big is it?

It contains over 50,000 students across four campuses in London, Medway and Kent.

Which universities merged?

The universities of Kent and Greenwich legally merged to form Lase last month.

What is the Neets crisis?

  • 1In April to June, an estimated 981,000 people aged 16-24 in the UK were classed as Neets.
  • 2This represented a fall of 30,000 compared with the first quarter of the year.
  • 3However, the figure is still 30,000 higher than a year ago, according to the Office for National Statistics.

The role of ‘super-unis’

It’s a real opportunity for us to address the very people that the Government’s worried about

It’s a real opportunity for us to address the very people that the Government’s worried about

Lase chief executive Professor Jane Harrington said the merger will help the universities deal with growing operational costs and provide more accessible learning pathways to better address the needs of the regions in a way they could not on their own.

How will Lase work?

  • Kent and Greenwich universities will still operate as distinct academic entities within the group.
  • They retain their current names and students still apply to and graduate from the institution they choose.
  • The group will now be able to offer subjects that they could not before when they were separate entities.
  • Students will also be able to use libraries and access wellbeing services across campuses.

What strain are universities facing?

Higher education providers in the UK have been facing increasing financial pressures, with MPs on the Education Committee warning earlier this year that there is a “real risk” a university in England could close due to insolvency for the first time.

The Office for Students told the committee it feared 24 providers were at risk of becoming insolvent and closing in the 12 months from November last year.

More ‘super-universities’ to come?

Lase is writing up a “blueprint” for the Department for Education to work as a “model” for a group structure which other universities would be able to use.

In May, King’s College London and Bedford-based Cranfield University announced plans to merge in August 2027.

EES checks resume – three ways to avoid border delays

A loophole allowing countries to pause the EU’s new border checks has ended, sparking concern that the travel chaos reported earlier in the summer may return.

EES border checks set to resume

EU border checks are set to start again in full force from Monday, 7 September, as member states’ rights to waive Entry/Exit System (EES) checks during times of maximum stress comes to an end.

While EES is unavoidable, and there is no way to skip the queue entirely, there are some ways to alleviate the stress of border check delays.

What is the EES?

  • EES requires British passport holders and other third-country nationals to register biometric data when entering or exiting the Schengen area.
  • Applying to 29 countries across Europe, excluding Ireland and Cyprus, the system caused has widespread delays since its introduction in April.
  • Until now, the registration could be temporarily suspended by border officials when queues were too long to manage.
  • But despite objections from some member states, this loophole has now expired, leaving holidaymakers facing the possibility of more significant delays.

How can you avoid delays

  • 1Travel outside peak times – even the busiest airports, like Palma and Lisbon, were quieter later in the summer.
  • 2Skip duty free – going straight to the border control desk will allow you to see how busy it is.
  • 3Pre-register for Sweden and Portugal – currently applying only to Sweden and Portugal, the Travel to Europe app allows users to pre-register, reducing the information needed at border control to just fingerprint scans.

Go to passport control ASAP

A spokesperson for Abta, a UK travel agent trade association, recommended passengers go to passport control as soon as possible after getting through security.

It follows reports earlier this summer of long delays and some flights setting off while several passengers were still in the long border control queues.

The huge naval base is just five miles along the road from Helensburgh, a town containing around 15,000 people, including military families and retired personnel.

Anti-nuclear campaigners argue that Faslane makes this part of Scotland – just 25 miles away from Glasgow – a target for attacks.

However, the majority of people in Helensburgh who spoke to The i Paper are supportive about the need for the nuclear naval base. There is enthusiasm about £15bn worth of planned upgrades to Faslane over the next 10 years, with the hope that the town can get a knock-on economic boost.

‘I assume the military’s on top of anything suspicious’

When it comes to the heightened threat from Russia, there is mix of feelings among local people, ranging from calm to defiant to uneasy.

Billy McBain, 58-year-old Helensburgh resident, worked at Faslane for many years. He retired as a Royal Navy engineer in 2013.

“It is worrying, the increase threat from Russia,” McBain said. “But there’s only a minute chance of attack against us. The whole point of the nuclear deterrent is that it keeps the whole country safe.”

“I enjoyed my years there,” McBain added on Faslane. “The expansion is good [for Helensburgh] – it would be a bit of ghost town if it wasn’t for the base.”

Sharon Harvey, a 67-year-old Helensburgh resident, said: “The stability of the leaders we have now, Putin and Trump, is worrying, I suppose.

“But I don’t think about it [the Trident nuclear deterrent] as a big threat. It has to go somewhere – so I’m happy for it to be here.”

“When I walk the dog along the seafront, you’re aware of visitors that are talking lots of photos,” added Harvey.

“I sometimes joke with friends, ‘Do you think the military are aware of this?’ I just assume they’re on top of anything really suspicious.”

Faslane ‘absolutely not off limits’ to Russia

Vladimir Putin’s veiled threat against the UK has heightened concerns that Russia could target British factories creating drones for Ukraine.

The possibility that Moscow might disrupt sites in Britain is a “military secret”, said the Russian leader.

Lord West of Spithead, the former First Sea Lord, said drone facilities are currently “more at risk” of cyber attacks, sabotage and espionage than major military bases like Faslane.

“But we have to be wary of grey zone hybrid activity,” the ex-Navy chief told The i Paper.

“Russia is always prodding and probing, and Faslane is one of the key places they might want to prod and probe at. If Putin thinks he could get away with some sabotage there, I’m sure he love to do that.”

Ed Arnold, defence expert and senior associate fellow at Royal United Services Institute, said: “The key military sites [like Faslane] are absolutely not off limits. The Russians would be interested in any way they can to prevent the site from operating as it should.

“If there’s a higher risk for sabotage or espionage, there’s an argument that people in Helensburgh need to be quite vigilant or suspicious of odd behaviour,” Arnold added.

The number of security incidents at Faslane rose from 16 in 2021 to 149 last year, according to Freedom of Information data obtained by LBC earlier in 2026.

The Ministry of Defence (MoD) has not revealed details. Security incidents can include lost ID and other documentation issues, The i Paper understands.

Philip Ingram, a former senior British military intelligence officer, said he hoped the increase was a “reflection of the heightened state of alert” as more security checks are done.

“If tensions increased substantially, if it came to a Russian operation against Nato forces, Faslane would be one of the number one targets,” said Ingram.

“We have to be wary of cyber attacks, sabotage and good old-fashioned espionage and spies that could compromise people working at Faslane,” he added.

Ingram urged people in Helensburgh to be “wary”, adding: “If there’s new people behaving suspiciously, they should report it. The threat is very real.”

Faslane would “likely be on a high-priority target list” in the event of a conflict between Russia and Nato, according to a report from the Nato-accredited think-tank, the Combined Joint Operations from the Sea Centre of Excellence.

‘Terrifying to think nuclear subs are there’

Mark Stewart, a 57-year-old anti-war campaigner who lives opposite Faslane’s security gates, thinks about the nuclear threat every day.

He is one of a handful of activists who have been living at Faslane Peace Camp, a collection of caravans and shacks across the road from the base, which have been occupied since 1982.

The former engineer from Newcastle came about a year ago to help revive the camp after numbers dwindled. “I’d had enough of the system,” he said.

“It’s a strange part of the world – like heaven and hell,” Stewart added. “It’s beautiful, but it’s also terrifying to think nuclear submarines are there.

“All the Russia talk makes things feels scarier than it has for a while. It makes Faslane more of a risk than ever.”

Siobhan Donald, 25, moved to the camp about six months ago. “I don’t like the nukes being there at all,” she said.

“I don’t think they should be in Scotland – it makes us a target. But I don’t want them anywhere. The Russia stuff is frightening, that the two sides are creating this heightened threat.”

Nuclear naval base ‘adds a bit of excitement’

Some Helensburgh residents are relaxed about the apparent rise in tensions. Cathy, a retiree in her early sixties, is not worried about Moscow-directed dirty tricks.

“You’d have to be a pretty stupid Russian spy to come here to try to break into Faslane.”

Cathy moved to the area with her husband about six years ago. “It didn’t put us off. It’s a beautiful part of the world, and it’s interesting to be close to the submarines.

“It adds a bit of excitement,” she joked. “I’ve always thought that if Russia was to bomb us, we’d all be gone in an instant, we’d all be doomed anyway. So, I don’t worry about it.”

The SNP has said it would seek to negotiate for the removal of Trident from Faslane in an independent Scotland, while still supporting Nato’s mission.

Math Campbell-Sturgess, SNP councillor for Helensburgh and Lomond South, said that the Faslane naval base remains “very much part and parcel of the community”.

And councillors want local businesses “to get as much of an economic boost as possible” from the coming modernisation of the base.

“Since the Russian invasion of Ukraine, people are concerned – you can’t not be,” he added.

“But no more concerned than anywhere else. People are so accustomed to the stand-off since the Cold War, so they’re not alarmed by everything Putin says.”

Alex Gallacher, 68, who retired to Helensburgh a couple of years ago, is not alarmed at all. “I’m very cynical about all this Russia threat stuff.

There’s a lot of sabre-rattling going on, but not much substance to any of it. It’s bullshit,” he added. “I’m more worried about the cost of living, about homelessness, about poverty.”

An MoD spokesperson said the security of Faslane “is of the utmost importance and security measures are continually reviewed”, adding: “Due to the sensitive nature of the site we do not comment on specific measures or incidents.”
