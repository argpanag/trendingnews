‘I can kneel down and pray again’: Mary Berry, 91, on the one ‘essential’ exercise that restored her mobility

TheTools · 21.11.2026 00:00 · source · permalink

After breaking her hip, Mary Berry took the plunge with a form of exercise she doesn’t like at all

‘It was my doctor who first said if you sit in a chair, you most likely won’t be here in a few years’ time,’ says Dame Mary Berry. In 2021, she suffered a broken hip after tripping up while picking sweet peas in her garden. Surgeons operated to repair the hip, before delivering this sobering news. But why, if the surgery was successful, was their warning so stark?

Why are hip fractures so dangerous?

A 2017 study found that one in three adults over the age of 50 dies within 12 months of fracturing their hip. Another found that older adults have a five- to eight-fold increased risk of death during the first three months after hip fracture.

‘After a hip break, many people are suddenly unable to walk, stand or even sit comfortably. This immobility can quickly lead to complications such as blood clots, pneumonia and muscle wasting. Unlike a wrist or ankle fracture, which can be managed with relative independence, a hip fracture often requires surgery and intensive rehabilitation and even then, many people don’t regain full function,’ explains GP Dr Vikram Murthy.

Swimming for recovery

Faced with this news, ‘I was determined to get back into shape,’ says Dame Mary. Her strategy? ‘You’ve got to keep going,’ she says. ‘You’ve got to push yourself. Start with short walks, taking great care.’

Exercise, Dame Mary explains, ‘was essential’. She had physiotherapy and also took the plunge, engaging in a sport she had no great love for. ‘I went swimming,’ she explains. ‘I don’t like swimming, but of course you can move better in the water and you’ve got to work at it.’

In 2008, scientists at the University of South Carolina published results of a study following over 40,000 men, age 20 to 90, for an average of 13 years. The swimmers among them, they found, were 50% less likely to die from all causes than runners or walkers. Land-based exercise, they concluded, was less effective at reducing artery stiffness.

What to read next

There’s more. ‘Swimming is excellent for older adults because it’s low-impact,’ explains physiotherapist Sam Bhide. ‘The buoyancy of the water offloads the joints, significantly reducing the impact and compressive forces compared with land-based exercise.’

Water also provides 360-degree resistance, she explains, ‘which helps build muscular endurance and tone. This resistance encourages a full range of motion and helps maintain muscle mass.’

All this makes swimming great for your whole body, but it is also particularly ‘brilliant for hips,’ she explains, ‘because it promotes mobility and strengthens the surrounding musculature – specifically the glutes and hip stabilisers. It is a very effective way to keep the joint supple.’

Which strokes are best?

For those recovering from hip surgery, Sam recommends starting very gently, with a mix of strokes: ‘Try front crawl for 10 lengths and then 10 lengths of backstroke, for example, to encourage a good range of motion. Water walking or simple leg kicks holding on to the side of the pool are also effective, controlled ways to gain strength.’

It may take time and it may not be easy but, says Mary, ‘It’s very rewarding because now I can kneel down and pray again. You wouldn’t know that I’ve had a new knee and a new hip.’
