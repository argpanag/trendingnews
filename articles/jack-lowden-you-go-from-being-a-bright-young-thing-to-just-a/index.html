Jack Lowden: “You go from being ‘a bright young thing’ to just ‘a thing’”

TheTools · 02.11.2026 06:00 · source · permalink

Jack Lowden cycles down a traffic-choked street in Shoreditch, past chugging cabs and a van unloading sacks of coffee beans. He enters from the right: Lime bike coasting, body still in the saddle. He shoots me a pointed finger, and for a moment, it looks like he’s miming a Walther PPK. He is, in fact, shooting me the international hand signal for “one sec”, as he slips past the entrance of the café to the nearest amenable lamppost.

It’s a big week. Tomorrow, he flies to LA for the Emmys, where Slow Horses, the great Apple TV series about C-tier spies, is up for nine awards – one of them his. And three days ago, he became the odds-on favourite to be the next James Bond, swivelling the eyes of the world his way.

We move inside and find a corner table. Lowden asks for an orange, carrot and ginger juice; they don’t have it, so he settles for a smoothie and grumbles about the September sun. “I’m a creature of the cold,” he says. “What is this now, 20-odd degrees? This is my peak – fuck, man, I’m struggling.”

Shirt by Graphpaper. Trousers by Ami. Tie by Giorgio Armani. Glasses by Tom Ford.

Then he turns the questions on me, with the chatty discomfort of someone who’d much rather be asking than answering.

“How old are you?” A gust of comradely weariness, delivered in a soft Scottish Borders burr.

“I’m 30.”

“I’m 36, man. You definitely start feeling older.”

He first noticed it – not maturity, but fatigue – “at 30,” he says. “When you get into your 30s, something just starts to tick… I measure myself against footballers. You know, when they get to 30 and it’s like, ‘Oh, he’s still got the legs’ – because he’s only 30.”

But actors endure their fair share of scrutiny about ageing. “I remember being ‘promising’ for ages,” says Lowden. He won an Olivier at 23, and an EE Rising Star award, then spent a decade doing serious work in big films – a Spitfire pilot in Christopher Nolan’s Dunkirk (2017), Lord Darnley, opposite Saoirse Ronan, in Mary Queen of Scots (2018) – without quite becoming the name above the marquee.

Now, he’s reaching the age when a promising actor becomes a leading man, or doesn’t. “I count myself lucky, in that I’ve had a relatively gradual build,” he says. “So I’ve been allowed to develop as a man, as a person, outside the spotlight.” Only lately has he noticed it swinging his way. “It’s funny – you go from being ‘a bright young thing’ to just ‘a thing’.”

Slow Horses has a lot to do with that. His hapless nepo-spy, River Cartwright, has carried the show’s momentum for six series. Gary Oldman’s Jackson Lamb, a flatulent, foul-mouthed spymaster, may get the laughs, but it’s Lowden – all puppy-dog eyes and self-inflicted injuries – who lingers. “Jack is great at the action, he’s great at the comedy, but he’s also really, really great at the messy emotional stuff,” says show creator Will Smith. “He truly is the full package.”

In this mostly empty café, Lowden is still, for now, a civilian, unspotted, in the uniform of the famous at rest: a beige jacket and blue corduroy cap, both unusually nice and entirely undistinctive. “I’m in a bit of a time-off state at the moment,” he says. But he can’t leave the cap alone, tugging and resettling it every few minutes – the one tell that he’s still on the clock.

Which brings us to the reason we’re here, and his biggest role to date – a part with a habit of turning actors into fixtures of the national imagination. Colin Firth had the wet shirt. Matthew Macfadyen had the dawn mist. In Netflix’s new version of Pride and Prejudice, it’s Lowden’s turn as Mr Darcy.

Dolly Alderton, who wrote the adaptation, saw her Darcy way before anyone else did. “He had been at the top of our list for a long time when we were scripting, long before we started casting,” she tells me. Alderton and director Euros Lyn thought the part was a trap. “We always knew that Darcy is a difficult part to play – he is unknowable to the audience as well as Elizabeth for a large chunk of the story, and there are layers of vulnerability and complexity that he has to play underneath his cold and rude façade,” she says. “Of course we needed someone handsome, romantic, masculine, distinguished – all the things you associate with Mr Darcy, but what we also needed was a really, really accomplished actor.” When Emma Corrin came aboard as the heroine, Elizabeth Bennet, they agreed on the spot. “Jack Lowden was our dream Darcy,” Corrin says. “Dolly and I hoped and prayed he would do it.”

Lowden wasn’t so sure. “Honestly, the idea of it did make me stop and go, ‘Hold on,’” he says. “It’s very precious to a lot of people, Jane Austen, so it made me think about it for a bit.” He chatted with Alderton, Corrin and Lyn, and read a few scenes. Unlike Alderton, he didn’t see himself as an immediate Darcy, but that made the role all the more tantalising. “When I take jobs, or I’m offered them, I look at what I can get away with – I don’t buy myself in 99 per cent of the roles I do, but if other people do, I get a kick out of that.” He likes being the unlikely pick. “I’m not everybody’s first choice for most of the roles I’ve played, and I quite enjoy that.”

Lyn also directed Heartstopper, and it shows: this is a sunny, fast-moving Austen, aimed squarely at younger viewers. It’s also funnier than its predecessors, most of all in Jamie Demetriou’s sleazy, uneasy Mr Collins. “I think he’s the comic genius of our generation,” Lowden says. He cites a scene in which Collins forces a clumsy introduction on Darcy at a ball. Demetriou suggested smacking his head directly into the small of Lowden’s back, like a drinking bird. “He said, ‘I’ve got this idea, I might do this.’ I said, ‘Do it,’ and he did, and I had to turn around and look at him and it was so hard not to laugh.” It’s the kind of thing Lowden lives for. “I love a bit of physical business, and he’s still committed to it, which feels like a dying art.”

But Lowden has strong comedy chops, too. “Jack brought the humour to Darcy’s awkwardness, which surprised me,” Alderton says. “His confidence and his tenderness are in perfect balance. He can play both heroic and very human.” Corrin puts it more simply: “The amazing thing about Jack, and what I think makes our Lizzy and Darcy different, is his funny bones.”

“Physical business”, as Lowden calls it, isn’t just pratfalls and stunts, but the root of every good performance. “It took me years to realise that’s how I act – it’s not through feeling, it’s timing, the score and musicality of a scene,” he says. His Darcy is no action man, especially next to some of his other roles, but Lowden still started with the physicality. “I found him very shy. And I can relate to that. There’s a lot of him in the book, and in the previous adaptations, that’s just him standing in rooms, and I loved that. He’s always a bit over there; he’d rather not be there. For me, it’s about him moving, from beginning to end, from not wanting to be in the room to wanting to be in the room.”

For 46 free-flowing minutes, we talk about Darcy, The Rest is Entertainment, lockdown jogging and Scotland at the World Cup (“The night we qualified… I just lost myself”). Anything to ignore the black-tie-clad elephant, just out of view to our right.

The Tuesday before, Gary Oldman told a press junket two things: first, that he’d heard the new 007 had been cast; second, that he was hoping it’d be Lowden. Through a sinister confluence of news headlines, content aggregation and WhatsApp groups, this soon morphed into thousands of thumbs-upped texts between fathers and sons: Jack Lowden as Bond? A spy show behind him, an Emmy nomination, a co-star of Oldman’s standing in his corner – it’s hardly a stretch.

Well, I’ve gotta ask –

“Oh, Christ…” Lowden’s previously animated face settles into a frozen, half-agape smile of practised unreadability.

What was it like, when Oldman’s comments came out?

A suck of the teeth precedes the official statement. “On this… It’s all very flattering. It’s nice to be mentioned – for any role – and I’m just not really going to add to the noise,” he says. It isn’t a denial; it’s a bucket of cold water, poured with care. “It’s very nice, and Gary’s a sweetheart – it’s particularly lovely, coming from someone like him. But I don’t really listen to it.” It comes, Lowden reckons, with the outfit: “As a young male actor, generally, the minute you’re not playing a zany artist type, the minute you’re in a suit, people fling it at you.” We move on to the show that has put him in the conversation.

Six series deep, Slow Horses has become one of those rare long-running collaborations where actor and character grow together in plain sight. It began with a tape. “The great Nina Gold got Jack to tape an audition,” recalls Smith. (Gold, for what it’s worth, is now leading the search for the next 007.) “I knew he was River in seconds. You could have put it on screen.”

River has since become a showcase for both sides of Lowden. As co-star Kadiff Kirwan puts it, “He gets to play the hero and the clown.” The clowning comes from a childhood obsession with 1970s British sitcoms, and above all, Arthur Lowe’s Captain Mainwaring in Dad’s Army. “The way they’d push him over,” Lowden says, pushing his own cap and glasses skewwhiff, “and you could see him composing himself, adjusting his glasses, his hat” – he sets each back in place, inches from my face – “because he knew it made everyone laugh, and you could see the workings of it.”

Kristin Scott Thomas, who first worked with Lowden at the Old Vic in 2014 and now plays steely MI5 boss Diana Taverner, has watched the change up close. “She actually said something to me at one point in season six… I looked up, and she went, ‘Oh, you look like a man now,’” says Lowden. He talks about her, Oldman and his screen grandfather, Jonathan Pryce, with real admiration, mostly for how little fuss they make. Oldman is “routinely the most prepared actor, walks onto set knowing every line, and then goes home, has a good time, and doesn’t take himself too seriously”. Lowden works the same way. “There’s no mystique; it’s very workmanlike, and that’s what I love.”

The heroics have escalated with each series, and the sixth is the most perilous yet: Russian agents pick off the slow horses one by one in reprisal for an off-the-books MI6 hit, with River’s own father, Frank Harkness (Hugo Weaving), leading the cull. The series’ centrepiece is a shootout in a bowling alley, more John Wick than James Bond: a hail of bullets, bathroom brawls and broken light fittings.

The best part of the show, for Lowden, is simpler: “It’s really nice being part of something people properly love.” Slow Horses has already been renewed for a seventh and eighth series, despite a cliffhanger at the end of the sixth, which leaves it unclear how long River, and with him Lowden, will be sticking around.

What comes next, Lowden suspects over the dregs of his smoothie, won’t happen in front of a camera at all. “I don’t think it’s in acting, really,” he says. “I think it’s in making something of my own.”

Not that the roles are drying up. Next is Berlin Noir, Apple TV’s adaptation of Philip Kerr’s crime novels, written by Peter Straughan, the Oscar-winning writer of Conclave. Lowden plays Bernie Gunther, a sardonic, world-weary investigator with his own moral code, picking his way through a Berlin thick with crime – a character not a million miles from a certain other action hero. Colin Firth is in the cast, “which was fascinating, having just finished playing [Darcy] myself,” says Lowden. But the real revelation was Straughan. “I loved doing that job, playing that part, but the kick I got was realising how bloody good Peter was. I was envious of him some days. I wished I was on [his] side of the camera.”

Directing, he says, is the end game, and if it meant giving up acting, “I’d do it, no problem.” He has made a start. He co-produced the 2020 thriller Kindred, which he also starred in, as well as The Outrun, the BAFTA-nominated 2024 drama in which Ronan played a recovering alcoholic returning to her hometown in the Orkney Islands. Now, there’s another project in development. “Something I’ve always wanted to tell, one of the only stories I really want to tell,” Lowden says, “working with a writer I think is phenomenally talented. Hopefully we’ll have a first draft in a couple of months.”

The rest is a matter of nerve: “There’s a little voice under my arse telling me to just get on and do my own thing, and it probably takes more balls than I’ve currently got. But it’s coming.”

The price of starting so young – acting since he was 10, working since he was barely out of his teens, climbing steadily ever since – is that a lot got left behind. “Career-wise, my biggest regret is not going to university. I went straight to drama school,” says Lowden. “That three years of running about with a satchel on a bike…” In lockdown, he tried to make up for it, with a short course in Anglo-Scottish relations at Edinburgh University. “At the end they said, ‘Right, go off and write a dissertation,’ and I thought, No, I just want to know and learn stuff,” he says. The piano, which he took to grade five at school, went the same way. “I wish I’d stuck at it. Whenever I see a piano now, I just sit down and play the same piece – ‘Achilles Heel’ by Toploader – over and over.”

Not that Lowden’s life hasn’t worked out exactly as he’d have wanted it to. This summer, Ronan was shooting a film in Italy, and he went along. “I’m going to sound a bit soppy, but getting to watch her act, in the flesh – that’s how we met – is really wonderful. She’s so at home doing it,” he says. Last year, they had their first child. “Since I was a kid, I always knew I wanted to be a dad, and now I am. You become aware that you’ll watch your kid grow up – sounds morbid – and you think, Oh my God, I’m in charge of a kid, and I’m still a kid myself.’”

There is one role he is chasing. It’s another figure to whom many men feel a psychological connection: Sir Alex Ferguson, in his Aberdeen days. “I swear to God, I think he’s cited Michael Collins, the Republican fighter, and how he ran his intelligence operation,” he says. “I find it amazing that a football manager would look to how someone ran a guerrilla campaign for lessons.”

Football carries weight for Lowden. He used to go to games with his dad, in towns where “clubs are the only thing that’s still there” – and turns up on the touchline when he can at his beloved Broxburn Athletic, a semi-professional side in the fifth tier of Scottish football. It’s the amateur world – of Sunday leagues, community choirs and parish councils – that makes him talk fastest.

“I grew up in amateur operatics,” he says, “and I think that old-school way of thinking about what we do comes from that: people doing it for the love of it.”
