Report: Blacklisted IEIT Used 6 Newer AMD Firmware Versions - FourWeekMBA

TheTools · 19.11.2026 15:56 · source · permalink

Hunterbrook Media reported on Thursday that blacklisted Chinese server maker IEIT Systems has put “at least six newer versions of AMD’s firmware” into its products since the US added it to the Entity List. AMD did not answer Hunterbrook’s questions, the outlet says.

Hunterbrook discloses that its affiliate, Hunterbrook Capital, is short AMD and long a basket of comparable securities. The investigation says the firmware is the startup code that lets AMD chips run in servers.

The key insight: Hunterbrook Media, whose affiliate is short AMD, reports that Entity-Listed IEIT Systems used at least six newer AMD firmware versions after its March 2025 listing; AMD did not answer its questions, and whether a licence exists is not public.

Update, 19 November 2026, 17:40 UTC: AMD responds. AMD says Hunterbrook’s claims “misrepresent AMD’s practices,” according to a Bloomberg headline relayed by Walter Bloomberg at 17:03 UTC and Wall St Engine. Earlier, Hunterbrook had said AMD did not answer its questions.

AMD’s investor-relations press-release page showed no statement dated 19 November when we checked at about 17:35 UTC, so we have not seen the full text. AMD traded at $490.00 at 12:38 p.m. ET, down 7.57% on the day, Nasdaq quote data show. Not investment advice.

Update, 19 November 2026, 16:06 UTC: AMD shares fell on the day of the report. AMD traded at $489.84 at 11:03 a.m. ET, down $40.30 or 7.6% from the $530.14 prior close, Nasdaq quote data show. The day’s low was $470.56. Earlier, Walter Bloomberg posted that the decline had extended to 8.6%.

By our arithmetic, a $40.30 drop on the 1,640,087,251 shares AMD reported outstanding in its latest 10-Q is about $66.1 billion of market value. At the day’s low, the drop was about 11.2%, or roughly $97.7 billion.

China matters to AMD’s sales. In fiscal 2025, revenue billed to customers in China (including Hong Kong) was $7,751 million of $34,639 million, about 22.4% (our arithmetic on the 10-K geographic table), matching a figure circulating on X. Billing location is not where chips end up.

AMD’s investor-relations press-release page showed nothing dated 19 November when we checked at 16:05 UTC, and Hunterbrook says AMD did not answer its questions. Not investment advice.

Update, 19 November 2026, 16:25 UTC: what Hunterbrook did and did not say. Posts on X with wide reach said Hunterbrook believes AMD “illegally sold chips” into China (one post had about 132,000 views; another said Hunterbrook “accuses the company of illegally selling chips”). We did not find that claim in either AMD article Hunterbrook published on 19 November.

Its main report says the records it found “don’t establish on their own a violation of export laws,” and that AMD’s payments from Chinese ventures “do not establish that AMD violated U.S. export controls.” It adds that “selling chips there is legal.” Its one use of the word “illegal” describes a Commerce settlement with a different company, Lambda Research.

A second Hunterbrook piece, under The Bear Cave name, says The Bear Cave believes investors are “trading one cousin’s problems for the other’s” and makes no illegality claim. Both disclose Hunterbrook Capital is short AMD. We could not read Hunterbrook’s X accounts, so a separate post can’t be ruled out. Hunterbrook says AMD did not answer its questions. What Hunterbrook Says It Found

IEIT is listed under Inspur Electronic Information Industry, with IEIT Systems as an alias, in a Federal Register rule effective 25 March 2025. Its licence review policy is “Policy of denial”.

Hunterbrook says it downloaded an IEIT software update posted on 19 October 2026 and found AMD’s startup firmware inside, “in a version issued after the blacklisting”.

That update came 573 days after the listing took effect (our arithmetic). Where AMD’s release dates were public, IEIT’s builds followed “about one to two months later”, Hunterbrook says.

Hunterbrook also says three companies it ties to IEIT, Maginfra, Kaytus and Aivres, offer firmware “nearly identical” to IEIT’s. None of the three is on the Entity List, it notes.

What Is Not Established

Hunterbrook says the dates “concern software, not shipments of processors”. It does not establish how IEIT obtained the firmware.

A former Commerce official told Hunterbrook not to rule out that AMD holds a licence, and that the department keeps that information from the public. AMD did not say whether it holds one, Hunterbrook reports.

Why the Timing Matters

Hunterbrook points to the US rule that would extend restrictions to companies at least 50% owned by listed entities. Its pause expires on 10 January 2027, 52 days from 19 November (our arithmetic).

It also cites AMD’s Singapore billing, “$4.28 billion” in fiscal 2025, “over 10% of its sales”. A new rule effective today requires licences for advanced chips shipped to Singapore and four other countries.
