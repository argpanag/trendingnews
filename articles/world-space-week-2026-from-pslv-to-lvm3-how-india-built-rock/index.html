India's path to the Moon was not accompanied by just one rocket. It is a product of learning over years and years to launch heavier spacecraft into space. India's launch vehicles have grown with the expansion of the country's increasingly ambitious space programmes, from the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle (PSLV) to the Launch Vehicle Mark-3 (LVM3).

PSLV emerged as India's workhorse

PSLV was launched for the first time in 1993 and is one of India's important launchers. It is designed to fly four times with solid and liquid propulsion, being capable of injecting satellites into a variety of Earth orbits.

PSLV, over the years, became synonymous with Earth-observation, navigation satellites and commercial satellites. It also contributed significantly to India's interplanetary aspirations with missions like Chandrayaan-1 and Mars Orbiter Mission.

But India's ambitions were expanding into a rocket of medium lift's capacity didn't seem to stretch very far.

Why India needed a bigger rocket?

For communication satellites and deep space missions, a satellite capable of carrying much more payload was needed, requiring a heavier lifting vehicle. This resulted in LVM3, which was then called Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle Mark III (GSLV Mk III).

LVM3 was a quite different rocket from PSLV as, apart from the two big solid boosters, a liquid-fuel core stage and a cryogenic upper stage. The mass off-the-road of the vehicle is approximately 640 tonnes and the height is approximately 43.5 metres.

LVM3 made history for the Indian lunar aspirations

The importance of LVM3 became clear with Chandrayaan-2. During 2019 the rocket launched the second Indian lunar mission towards the moon. On July 14, 2023, Chandrayaan-3 was successfully launched on LVMH—4 at Sriharikota. Eventually the spacecraft landed softly near the south pole of the Moon in a historic event.

The mission, which showcased India's heavier launch ability, was a step towards more complex moon explorations.

Satellites to the Moon and beyond!

With PSLV to LVM3, it is not just a switch of rocket size; it's a gradual change. It shows the progress India has made in the field of propulsion, launch technology and mission design.

The rocket revolution is far from over, as India is preparing for heavier satellites and future human spaceflight and lunar missions. And the next chapter in the world of rockets might involve more powerful rockets than LVM3.
