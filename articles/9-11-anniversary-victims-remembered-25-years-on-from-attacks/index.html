  7. 'I survived the epicentre of 9/11, but years later it's left me terminally ill'published at 10:49 BST
    10:49 BST

    Amy Oakden and Seb Cheer
    BBC Tees and BBC Yorkshire

    Warning: This story contains graphic details of the aftermath from the 9/11 attacks.

    Twenty-five years on from 9/11, Paul Berriff is still feeling its impact.

    The photographer and documentary filmmaker has become one of thousands of survivors to be diagnosed with terminal illnesses connected with the long-term effects of that day.

    Now aged 80 and living near Northallerton in North Yorkshire, Berriff recalls being left unconscious for approximately 35 minutes, during which time the second tower also collapsed.

    After regaining consciousness, Berriff said his "mouth was full of all this cement gunge stuff".

    "I could hardly breathe. I was hot, I was sweating, my trousers were burning. Total blackness, I've never seen blackness like it."

    "All of us that escaped that day thought we'd done well, then about three or four years ago, we started developing these illnesses," he said.

    "All of us had ingested the cloud which contained body parts, glass, aviation fuel, you name it. It had laid dormant for 20 years and we've all developed these fatal diseases."

    He was diagnosed three years ago with pulmonary fibrosis.

    "My consultant said I've only got five years to live," he said.

  8. New Yorkers were 'lied' to about toxic air after 9/11, says Mamdanipublished at 10:35 BST
    10:35 BST

    Madeline Halpert and Kayla Epstein
    Reporting from New York City

    Toxic air quality was being recorded in Manhattan soon after the 9/11 attacks even as officials played down health risks to the public, according to a trove of newly released files.

    Asbestos was found "nearly everywhere" tested near Ground Zero, where the World Trade Center towers collapsed in 2001, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani said earlier this week.

    "People got sick because the leaders they trusted lied and told them they were safe to breathe in toxic air," he said, announcing the release of 170,000 documents related to the attacks.

    Illnesses related to dust and smoke from the towers' collapse have since claimed more than threefold the nearly 3,000 lives lost in the attacks, US health officials say.

    The records show asbestos was found near Ground Zero for months after the attacks at the same time people were being advised it was safe to go there.

    The files, which include air-quality reports and communications among city officials, include October 2001 correspondence known as the "Harding Memo".

  16. 'We owe 9/11 victims preparation, resilience and courage' - MI5 bosspublished at 08:32 BST

    The horrors of 9/11 will never leave me, writes Director General of MI5 Sir Ken McCallum in the Independent this morning., external

  17. I watched as a hijacked plane flew over my home - moments later my wife was killed on 9/11published at 08:19 BST
    08:19 BST

    Brendon Williams
    BBC News

    Charles Wolf knew immediately that something was wrong when a plane flew low over his New York City apartment on a sunny morning 25 years ago.

    Seconds later, the plane slammed into the North Tower of the World Trade Center - where his wife Katherine, from Swansea, was working.

    Katherine died instantly, becoming one of 2,977 people killed in the al-Qaeda terrorist attacks on the USA on 11 September 2001.

    "I knew instantly when it flew over my building - our apartment building - that it was a twin engine jet at full throttle," said Charles, a trained pilot. "I knew it so well that I called the FBI and told them... 'That was no accident, that was deliberate'."

    A year later, when Charles met the FBI agent who took that call, he said the agent told him: "You were the first person to get us on to the fact that that was not an accident."

    Charles subsequently led a campaign to ensure the victims' families and dependants were fairly compensated, and said his "wonderful" wife would be proud of what he fought for.

    Unhappy with the US-government run September 11th Victim Compensation Fund, he successfully pressured the authorities into allocating funds more fairly and to more people.

    His fight later became the subject of a Netflix movie called Worth, in which he was portrayed by the actor Stanley Tucci.

  18. 'Horror film scream lives in my head,' says 9/11 survivorpublished at 08:00 BST
    08:00 BST

    Warning: This post contains details that some may find distressing

    Janice Brooks had only moved to New York a couple of weeks before 9/11 to begin what she calls "the biggest adventure of [her] life" - working for a company called Euro Brokers on the 84th floor of the South Tower.

    On the morning of the attacks, she was in the office when she heard a bang and saw "paper and dust swirling" as the first plane hit the North Tower, she tells BBC Breakfast.

    While she was evacuating, the second plane hit with an "almighty thud". Brooks heard "one of those horror film screams" as a woman was hit by debris, her eyes covered in blood.

    She says the scream "lives in my head".

    When she finally reached the ground floor and looked up at the building, there was a "big gaping hole" where her floor should have been.

    Sixty-one people died in her office. "I only went to 23 funerals," Brooks says. "I should have tried harder, I’ll take it to my grave that I let them down."

    She says she's given herself "one job" since that day: "To have a good life".

I watched as a hijacked plane flew over my home - moments later my wife was killed on 9/11published at 08:19 BST
08:19 BST

Brendon Williams
BBC News

Charles Wolf knew immediately that something was wrong when a plane flew low over his New York City apartment on a sunny morning 25 years ago.

Seconds later, the plane slammed into the North Tower of the World Trade Center - where his wife Katherine, from Swansea, was working.

Katherine died instantly, becoming one of 2,977 people killed in the al-Qaeda terrorist attacks on the USA on 11 September 2001.

"I knew instantly when it flew over my building - our apartment building - that it was a twin engine jet at full throttle," said Charles, a trained pilot. "I knew it so well that I called the FBI and told them... 'That was no accident, that was deliberate'."

A year later, when Charles met the FBI agent who took that call, he said the agent told him: "You were the first person to get us on to the fact that that was not an accident."

Charles subsequently led a campaign to ensure the victims' families and dependants were fairly compensated, and said his "wonderful" wife would be proud of what he fought for.

Unhappy with the US-government run September 11th Victim Compensation Fund, he successfully pressured the authorities into allocating funds more fairly and to more people.

His fight later became the subject of a Netflix movie called Worth, in which he was portrayed by the actor Stanley Tucci.

'Horror film scream lives in my head,' says 9/11 survivorpublished at 08:00 BST
08:00 BST

Warning: This post contains details that some may find distressing

Janice Brooks had only moved to New York a couple of weeks before 9/11 to begin what she calls "the biggest adventure of [her] life" - working for a company called Euro Brokers on the 84th floor of the South Tower.

On the morning of the attacks, she was in the office when she heard a bang and saw "paper and dust swirling" as the first plane hit the North Tower, she tells BBC Breakfast.

While she was evacuating, the second plane hit with an "almighty thud". Brooks heard "one of those horror film screams" as a woman was hit by debris, her eyes covered in blood.

She says the scream "lives in my head".

When she finally reached the ground floor and looked up at the building, there was a "big gaping hole" where her floor should have been.

Sixty-one people died in her office. "I only went to 23 funerals," Brooks says. "I should have tried harder, I’ll take it to my grave that I let them down."

She says she's given herself "one job" since that day: "To have a good life".
