Gold's 25% fall in 2026 among sharpest since 1980: What history says about recoveries

TheTools · 19.11.2026 11:30 · source · permalink

If you bought gold near its peak this year, the recent fall is hard to ignore. And if you're considering buying now, there's another question: Could prices fall further?

But here's what history tells us. Gold has seen double-digit corrections several times in the past, even in years when it eventually delivered positive returns.

The weakness continued on Thursday (November 19), with domestic gold futures for December 4 delivery opening 0.75 percent lower at Rs 1,28,842 per 10 grams. MCX spot gold stood at Rs 1,30,921 per 10 grams.

So, is the current correction really unusual, or is this something gold investors have seen many times before?

According to FundsIndia Research, gold recorded a 25 percent intra-year decline in 2026 through October 31, making it one of the sharpest corrections in its historical data going back to 1980.

Representative Image_Golds History of Declines and Recoveries

How common are sharp falls in gold?

Gold has experienced an average intra-year decline of around 13 percent since 1980, according to the FundsIndia analysis.

An intra-year decline, or drawdown, measures how far prices fall from an earlier high during a particular year, regardless of where they finish in December.

The historical data show that corrections of 10–15 percent are far from unusual.

For instance, gold fell as much as 15 percent during 2020. Similar declines occurred in 2022, while 2023 witnessed a 10 percent correction.

However, the 2026 decline stands out for its severity.

The steepest fall in the study occurred in 1980, when gold plunged 40 percent from its high. In 1981, prices corrected 25 percent, matching the drawdown recorded through October 2026.

But falling prices don't always mean a losing year

Perhaps the more interesting finding is what happened by the end of these years.

Despite repeated corrections, gold delivered positive calendar-year returns around 78 percent of the time over the historical period studied.

Consider 1980. Gold witnessed a 40 percent intra-year decline but still finished the calendar year with a 4 percent gain.

A similar pattern emerged in 2008, when gold corrected 20 percent during the year but ended with an 28 percent return.

More recently, gold fell 15 percent at one point in 2020, yet finished the year up 28 percent.

However, the pattern doesn't hold every time.

In 1981, a 25 percent drawdown was accompanied by a 22 percent calendar-year loss. Similarly, gold corrected 24 percent in 2013 and ended that year down 18 percent.

For 2026, FundsIndia's data showed a 5 percent negative return through October 31. This is a year-to-date figure, not a full-year return, and does not capture subsequent price movements.

What should investors take away?

The historical numbers highlight an important distinction: a sharp correction from a high does not necessarily determine how an investment performs over the entire year.

But the reverse is equally important. Just because gold has recovered from several past corrections does not mean the current decline will follow the same pattern.

For investors, the bigger consideration is their investment horizon and how much gold they already hold. Those buying after a sharp correction should be prepared for further volatility rather than assume prices must recover quickly.

Bottom line

Gold's history shows that temporary declines are common, but a 25 percent fall is considerably deeper than the historical average. Past recoveries offer perspective, not a guarantee of what happens next.
