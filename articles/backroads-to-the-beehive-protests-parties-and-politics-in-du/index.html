Backroads to the Beehive: Protests, parties and politics in Dunedin's studentville

TheTools · 23.10.2026 19:01 · source · permalink

RNZ / News / Politics / Election 2026

There are less than three days until enrolment closes and just over half of Dunedin's young people have enrolled to vote. RNZ's South Island Correspondent Tim Brown and camera operator Louis Dunham have toured Te Waipounamu to hear from the people where the political spotlight does not often shine. They take readers on a journey from the Backroads to the Beehive, this time from studentville.

Otago University's student quarter evokes a certain stereotype - streets littered with shattered glass, burnt-out couches and parties that tread a fine line between youthful fun and wild riot - but there is far more to student life than a drinking culture.

Scarfies, or breathers as they are now known, have a proud history of protest.

Otago University students have been taking to the streets since at least the 1950s when young political activists were leaders in the anti-nuclear movement.

By the 1990s thousands were marching against rapidly rising and recently introduced study fees, a tradition continued by hundreds of students who rallied in May against the government's decision to scrap the fees-free scheme that paid for a year of study.

The decision is still a sore point for students like Darcy Dougherty, who condemns the move as "pretty s***".

"I thought my third year would be free and I'd only be paying two years, so it's an extra $9000," he says.

Some students have access to the Bank of Mum and Dad but others rely on scholarships, loans and any other money they can scrimp and save.

On a quiet Tuesday night a notorious Dunedin party street is mostly shrouded in darkness, apart from the soft glow of light and pulse of bass from the back of one flat.

Hyde St is notorious for its parties.

RNZ / Louis Dunham

RNZ discovers six young men celebrating a flatmate's birthday with a mid-week party but even at home among friends, fees-free is a marker of the divide between those with a student loan and those without.

Five are students and the sixth is a full-time tradesman living with "the bros".

They are all enrolled to vote and intend to exercise their democratic right, including Eli Banks, who exclaims "bring back fees-free" to cheers from his flamates.

"It's pretty tight, you've got to work over the summer. If you didn't have any savings you'd be pretty cooked," he says.

"I have worked over summer every year, so I have got a few savings, but if you didn't have that I don't know if you'd be able to cut it without the bank of the parents."

Tradie Thomson Wright feels "almost the complete opposite" about Banks' enthusiasm for the scheme, arguing university students' tuition fees are not a priority in the current economic climate.

"I've got to buy my own tools out of my own pocket and I'm building the houses they'll live in, but as a flatmate, I still love him. We're not beefin'," he says.

The coalition government's decision to scrap the fees-free scheme is unpopular among students in the Dunedin electorate.

RNZ / Louis Dunham

'It's a basic human right to live in a warm environment and be fed'

On nearby Dundas Street, student Caley Tee and a group of friends describe how the rising cost of living is making it hard to afford necessities.

"There are weeks where people are choosing between eating food or staying warm and I don't think anyone should have to choose between those things. It's a basic human right to live in a warm environment and be fed," she says.

"It's a bit grim and the job market's pretty tough. No one's hiring. We've applied for plenty of places. We've got jobs for next year but no contract so far."

Tee's friend Alyssa Paton is in a similar bind.

"There are a lot of weeks where we are living week-to-week and having to dip into our savings to pay bills, but when you're surrounded by the right people it makes it worth it," she says.

Zara Stephens tries to avoid checking her student loan balance.

"I personally don't really like to look at it a lot because of how high it gets, but you're only a student once and you've got the rest of your life to recover from it," she says.

At the 'Corner Store' flat on Leith Street, some of its six young flatmates tell RNZ they each pay $260 per week in rent, which will rise to $285 per week for next year's tenants.

The student allowance and maximum student loan living costs are capped at $333 per week.

'Enrol, it takes two minutes. Everyone should vote'

Just over half of Dunedin's 18-24-year-olds are enrolled to vote.

Some of Dunedin's students are registered in their home electorates, complicating the numbers, but nationwide just over two-thirds of 18-24-year-olds are enrolled.

Otago University Students' Association president Daniel Leamy says there are fewer policies pitched at students because of young people's failure to vote in large numbers.

RNZ / Louis Dunham

That age group makes up almost half of the estimated unenrolled population and more than 100,000 young people across the country will not be able to vote in the election, including thousands in Dunedin.

Otago University Students' Association president Daniel Leamy says young people's failure to vote in large numbers has resulted in fewer party policy pitches targeting students.

"It is self-reinforcing. It's a constant cycle but we're working to counter it," he says.

"How you have your say is to go and vote. It's actually a pretty good system.

"We're trying to really push that message of, 'this is how you have your say, this is what you can do and it's actually really easy'."

Leamy enrolled on polling day at the last election, although that option is not available in November.

"That's a big barrier for students," he says.

"Most students here, this will be the first election they'll vote in. There's actually probably a lack of knowledge that it's even happening and that they need to get organised ahead of time this time around.

"We're running pretty big campaigns on social media to say 'hey, this time you have to be organised, you've got to be enrolled two weeks before Election Day'.

"Those 50 percent who are enrolled, they see it, but we're trying to get to that other 50 percent, which is a challenge."

Back on Hyde Street, Tradie Thomson Wright, while not at university, has a message for every New Zealand student ahead of enrolment closing at midnight on Sunday.

"Enrol, it takes about two minutes, and then everyone should have their say. Everyone should vote, no matter what."
