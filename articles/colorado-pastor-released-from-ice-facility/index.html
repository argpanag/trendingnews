Colorado pastor released from ICE facility

TheTools · 21.10.2026 01:37 · source · permalink

Provided by Philip Eberhart

  • Aurora
  • Immigration

Audio By Carbonatix

A 79-year-old retired Ugandan pastor has been fighting for a year to stay in the United States. For now, he can take a pause.

The U.S. District Court of Colorado officially granted the petition for a writ of habeas corpus for Edward Nalwamba — an immigrant from Uganda who has lived and preached in the U.S. since 2002 — on Oct. 19, ordering Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) to release the man from detention at the Aurora detention center. He was released today, Oct. 20.

“It has been a long, hard fight. It’s very exciting. He’s just thrilled,” says Joy Athanasiou, Nalwamba’s immigration attorney. “We were so concerned that if he stayed in detention a little longer, he wouldn’t make it. I am hopeful he will be able to recover some strength.”

The battle started in September 2025 when ICE detained him “out of the blue,” according to Reverend Philip Eberhart, a pastor at Greenwood’s Resurrection Anglican Fellowship and Nalwamba’s close friend. “He left two developmentally disabled men and a house full of goods. ICE just picked him up and left them there.”

Nalwamba was initially supposed to be deported on June 30, but his lawyer and church community fought tooth and nail to stop his deportation and reopen his asylum case, which has been in legal limbo for over 20 years.

Nalwamba initially came to the U.S. for a religious conference in 2002. He traveled from a country under the authoritarian rule of President Yoweri Museveni. Because Nalwamba openly fought for democracy throughout his life in Uganda, he became a target on the president’s radar, his friends say, adding that colleagues back home sent him messages while he was in the U.S., warning him of trouble back home if he returned.

During Museveni’s reign, many outspoken citizens have gone missing, including some of Nalwamba’s colleagues, according to Athanasiou.

Eberhart has even claimed that strange men recently approached Nalwamba’s daughter while she was attending college in Uganda, asking where her father was. His colleagues believe he would likely be arrested as soon as he landed in his home country.

Nalwamba applied for asylum in 2002, but his attorney never filed the application and disappeared with the money. He applied on his own in 2003 and was denied. He was denied again in 2010 because he had not been tortured, but only feared being tortured, according to his current attorney, Athanasiou.

Nalwamba was eventually detained by ICE in 2010 and placed in El Paso County Jail (where the department was holding detained immigrants at the time). He was there for around nine months before being released with an “order of supervision,” meaning he had a deportation order but could not be immediately moved to Uganda.

Then he was left alone for over a decade, and resumed working with people with disabilities and preaching at Resurrection Anglican Fellowship before being detained in 2025. Over the past year, the pastor has experienced “severe medical neglect” by ICE and a respiratory illness, which has left him wheelchair-bound and weak, according to Athanasiou. Nalwamba has been ill with an upper respiratory illness since June 8, 2026, unable to get adequate medical treatment, therapy or testing for contagious diseases, his representatives say.

There have been several claims of tuberculosis rampaging through GEO Group’s private detention center, which has ultimately led to multiple lawsuits by the state.

“His health is still bad. I am very worried. He still has to be able to get to the doctor and get treatment and get testing,” Athanasiou says.

While the release is a triumph for Nalwamba, it’s only the start of the next phase. His motion to reopen his asylum case is pending with the Board of Immigration Appeals, and that could take months or years, according to Athanasiou. Nalwamba could technically be detained again on the deportation order, as well — something he and his friends still worry about.

“You cross your fingers and hope that they’re not going to try to pick him up again,” Athanasiou says. “If they were to pick him up again, in a normal time, the stay would be granted. But we’re just in this alternate universe right now.”

For now, Nalwamba will focus on recovering his health and fighting to have his asylum case reopened. He and his attorneys will then have to prove that his life would be in danger if he returned to Uganda.
