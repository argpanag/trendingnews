That was properly impressive from Zverev, who was barely threatened in the match. He broke in the first game, establishing control that he never relinquished. Shelton improved in the second set and actually won the ace battle 15-14, but it only seemed close because he served well; from the back, he was outclassed to the point of embarrassment, and there was no sense that he could win a breaker.

Er, I spent some time this afternoon researching my earlier optimism we’d see a contest, and this is what I found:

I didn’t see Zverev’s opening match as I was on the football, but he got 85% his first serves in. If he does that again, at the pace he sends them down, there’s not much Shelton can do.

Shelton is considered to have a big forehand, but the metrics say otherwise. At 78mph, he’s level with the tour average on speed and his average spin is just 43rpm above tour average.

On return, he was, just after the US Open, 85th on return metrics taken from the top 100 in the world.

Which, given his well documented issues on the backhand – he gets it in but can’t do much with it – doesn’t leave much beyond his serve. Talking of which, if his serve +1 is a forehand, he has an 80% better chance of winning the point than if it’s a backhand so, even with a decent serve, if he’s then made to his a backhand, he tends to lose the rally.

Preamble

“Achieving the world No 1 status is something every player dreams of, but I feel like I’m the only world No 1 in tennis history that has to explain why it’s OK for me to be world No 1. Which I find quite difficult, because I think I deserve to be where I am”.

So said Alexander Zverev after becoming the first world No 1 to lose in straight sets immediately after establishing that status. He does, though, know why it’s like this. First of all, not everyone agrees with his assertion that “the accusations” against him of domestic violence “have been proven false”, so not everyone is happy with his new status. But there are sporting reasons too: the man unable to win majors when Jannik Sinner and Carlos Alcaraz are healthy has pirated two in their absence, beating no one ranked higher than 14 to take the first and no one ranked higher than nine to take the second. A such, he sits inside a tautological paradox: a win is a win, yet also, there are wins and there are wins.

Zverev has, though, collected this trophy twice – hard court, best of three sets, is probably his sweet spot – and will expect to beat Ben Shelton tonight, his serve and backhand, two of the best shots in the game, likely to be definitive. Shelton, though, is growing, his win over Alcaraz in New York the biggest of his career, and though he was easily despatched in the final by Zverev, who leads their head-to-head 6-0, there’s a sense a move is being made. He’s taken a new coach in Juan Carlos Ferrero and with it a new confidence that the right things are being done, improvement imminent.

Whether he’s changed quickly enough is uncertain. But his lefty angle, correctly deployed, might hamper Zverev’s backhand, just as the flat forehand down the line might test his weaker flank – and, if he’s to rise beyond his current station, at 24 these are the matches he needs to start winning, and soon.
