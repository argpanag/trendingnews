by Alex Pry

West Palm Beach, Fla. (WPEC) — The National Hurricane Center is monitoring two areas for possible tropical development in the Atlantic basin, including Potential Tropical Cyclone Five in the northern Gulf of America and the remnants of former Tropical Storm Dolly near the Caribbean.

Area #1: Remnants of Tropical Storm Dolly

The remnants of former Tropical Storm Dolly are producing showers and thunderstorms from Hispaniola eastward to the northern Leeward Islands.

Strong upper-level winds are expected to prevent redevelopment over the next couple of days as the system races west to west-northwest at 20 to 25 mph.

Conditions may become a little more favorable by the middle of the week as Dolly’s remnants approach the southern Bahamas or Florida Straits.

The National Hurricane Center gives the system a near-zero chance of redevelopment over the next 48 hours and a low, 20% chance through the next seven days.

For South Florida, Dolly’s remnants bear watching because they could help reinforce an already wet pattern later this week.

Area #2: Potential Tropical Cyclone Five in the Northern Gulf

PTC Five is a broad area of low pressure in the northern Gulf, located more than 100 miles south of the southern Louisiana coast.

The disturbance continues to produce a concentrated area of showers and thunderstorms, and the National Hurricane Center says additional development is likely as it moves slowly west-northwest.

A short-lived tropical depression or tropical storm could form before the system reaches the upper Texas or southwestern Louisiana coast late Monday or early Tuesday.

The National Hurricane Center gives it a 80% chance of development over both the next two and seven days.

The next name on the list to claim is Edouard.

Regardless of whether it receives a name, the system is expected to bring locally heavy rain to portions of the Texas and Louisiana Gulf Coast.

It is too soon to determine exactly how close the moisture and any associated disturbance may get, but residents should stay tuned for forecast updates.

The Atlantic hurricane season is moving into its historical peak, which arrives around September 10th.

The most active stretch of the season runs from mid-August through mid-October, when ocean water is at its warmest and the atmosphere is often more favorable for tropical development.

So far this season, the Atlantic has produced four named storms — Arthur, Bertha, Cristobal and Dolly — but no hurricanes.

By the end of August, the climatological average season has typically reached its sixth named storm, and the first hurricane usually forms around August 11th.

That puts 2026 a little behind the typical pace in both named storms and hurricane activity.

That quieter start does not determine how active the rest of the season will be.

We are now entering the portion of the calendar when tropical activity can increase quickly, and it only takes one storm to make it a "bad" season, even despite the lack of activity thus far.
