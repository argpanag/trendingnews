Questions linger about lead pollution from Iowa blast, despite officials’ assurances

TheTools · 26.11.2026 01:32 · source · permalink

As federal officials offered reassurances Wednesday that the Army’s detonation of a cache of lead azide in Iowa produced a minimal plume of lead dust, independent scientists and local residents said questions remain about how much contamination the explosion caused, and where — though they were hopeful about the initial results.

Some people with homes closest to the blast who had heeded the call to evacuate said they were still unsure when it will be safe to return.

In a post on X around 5:30 p.m. local time, the Army said that the Federal Emergency Management Agency had issued an “all clear” around the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, where the detonation occurred, but provided little detail beyond that. “Post-detonation surface sampling is underway, and results will be released once complete,” it said.

Sean Byers, who lives across the street from the plant, said he plans to stay away from his home for a few days with his children.

“Although the Army has supposedly not detected lead in the air, I think it is in their best interest to make that claim,” he said. “I think that a lot of people in general are downplaying the potential long-term effects of what may be to come simply because they may have perceived the lead plume as less boisterous” than some had feared.

The Environmental Protection Agency said that initial, real-time air monitoring data — from four sites — had not detected any lead in the atmosphere; however, outside experts said it will take days to begin to understand whether there are hotspots that require cleanup.

Several experts said they were cautiously optimistic that the Army had averted the worst possible version of events in Middletown, Iowa.

The planned explosion, which occurred at 9 a.m. local time, had sparked an intense public outcry over the last two weeks. Local residents complained of poor communication from the Army and insufficient time to prepare — especially given the Thanksgiving holiday — which led officials to delay the detonation. Army leaders have said that detonating the 9,700 pounds of lead azide was the only option because the chemical had become so degraded and unstable that it was at risk of blowing up on its own.

Lead is a highly dangerous neurotoxin, and no level of exposure is considered safe. To assess where lead dust might have dispersed and how much contamination possibly occurred, academic and EPA scientists have spread out across Iowa to collect samples and begin testing.

A few more days of uncertainty about the risks lie ahead.

Dr. Joel Kaufman, a professor of medicine, epidemiology and environmental health sciences at the University of Washington who studies air pollution, said the EPA’s initial, real-time air monitoring was a promising sign but that it must be backed up with lab-based chemistry results from dust collected on the ground.

“This is a reasonable way to test for real-time lead in the environment,” he said, adding that it will be crucial to analyze samples from a wide variety of locations.

“Is it possible that they are right that the amount of lead that’s being put into the environment is virtually zero? Yes,” Kaufman said. “Is there reason to be suspicious about it? Yes.”

Andrew Whelton, a professor of civil, environmental and ecological engineering at Purdue University in Indiana, agreed that a study of physical samples is essential.

“You have to collect particles that deposit and send them to a lab for chemical analysis,” he said.

The EPA said Wednesday that it had 300 employees and contractors on the ground in Iowa and that they had taken 1,200 air, land and water samples before the detonation.

“We are very diligently working to immediately send the new post-event samples back to EPA labs,” the agency said on X.

Garrett Anthony, a Burlington resident who said his home is about 7 miles from the blast site, evacuated earlier this week.

“I’m really thrilled to see that the live monitoring by the EPA is showing zero lead in the air, but the ground testing is still very, very important to me, and I want to see what happens next,” he said. “I definitely care about the lasting effects.”

Anthony said he stored some soil in advance to use as a baseline in tests if necessary.

In a letter to Iowa’s governor on Friday, Jim Macy, an EPA administrator for the region, said the Army’s permit required it to update and distribute a plume estimate within six hours of the detonation. The Army published deposition and air dispersal maps at 3 p.m. local time, but they contained few immediate details. The air dispersal map showed no plume, as no lead was detected by sensors.

“Post detonation sampling underway,” one map said. “Results will be published when available.”

Macy said the EPA expected to receive initial results from dust wipe samples “in the days following the detonation,” but that a full data review would take longer.

The Army and the EPA are also supposed to determine which bodies of water could have been affected by the plume and reach out immediately to public water systems to offer lead sampling resources, drinking water evaluations and filter equipment for homes.

In the days before the detonation, Whelton had been critical of the EPA and the Army for not publicly sharing more details about their lead testing plan. In the permit documents that the Army submitted to the EPA, he said, “there is no plan for applying an evidence-based approach to determine if people’s homes, schools and businesses are safe.”

Academic scientists had been gearing up for more than a week to provide their own air and soil sampling.

Patrick O’Shaughnessy, a professor in the department of occupational and environmental health at the University of Iowa, said he planned to collect dust particles in a specialized pump that pulls air through a filter.

“Then you have to send it off to a lab using a sophisticated analysis machine to get at the lead content of it,” O’Shaughnessy said on Friday.

Others at the University of Iowa had planned to sample surfaces with dust wipes and have those tested.

“We’re going to do as many as we can,” O’Shaughnessy said, adding that researchers from Oregon and New York had flown to Iowa to help.

The group hoped to have some preliminary data to share Thursday morning, he added.

Anthony said he wanted to make sure an “independent review” from outside scientists corroborated Army and EPA data.

“Hearing that multiple sources all confirm would be great,” he said. “I know that’s going to take a little time.”

Meanwhile, at the site of the detonation, a complicated cleanup and remediation effort by Army personnel and contractors is expected to take three months.

According to the permitting documents, the detonation was expected to leave a crater 38 feet wide and about 5 feet deep. The documents suggested large chunks of concrete could fly as far as half a mile. Cleanup workers — who will wear full-body Tyvek safety suits and respirators — might have to move as much as 13 tons of debris to a hazardous waste facility, according to the permit.

The public anger over the Army’s detonation plan prompted it to add last-minute protective measures that were not listed in the permit documents. The extra measures, intended to help absorb debris and prevent contamination from spreading, included gel blocks, aqueous foam and a wall of water containers. It’s not clear if those added protections will alter the cleanup timeline.
