The stakes couldn't be higher in the fourth season of The Diplomat — with a Russian nuclear missile having been secretly hijacked by the U.S. while in British waters. So headaches abound for ambassador to the U.K. Kate Wyler (Keri Russell), who is juggling her marriage to Vice President Hal Wyler (Rufus Sewell) with her professional duties to President Grace Penn (Allison Janney), who has marital problems of her own (her husband is played by Bradley Whitford). But showrunner Debora Cahn has delivered yet another "thrillingly riveting" season, say critics, who've given the Netflix drama a rating of 100% on Rotten Tomatoes and 92 on Metacritic.

TV Insider's Matt Roush calls this season "rip-roaring, thrillingly riveting, and sensationally entertaining," praising Cahn for balancing international intrigue with "some of the wittiest dialogue that will have you giggling until the next dizzying shock stifles laughter into an 'OMG' gasp." His grade: five stars.

"In Season 4, The Diplomat continues to be a crackling, pulpy thriller with superb writing, gorgeous cinematography, and top-tier performances," says IGN's Michael Peyton. He praises Sewell and Russell's onscreen chemistry — "they continue to be dynamite," he writes" — as well as Whitford, who "takes full advantage of the show's darkly comedic writing," calling a scene with Chief of Staff Billie Appiah (Nana Mensah) "one of those two-handers that highlights the show's tour de force storytelling and brilliant acting."

But he singles out Janney in particular, saying the recent Emmy winner has never been better. "Intricately drawn and perfectly performed, Grace Penn is a revelation of a character," he writes. "As Penn, Janney is at once flighty and megalomaniacal, grounded and borderline mad, caring and absolutely unforgiving. ... Grace Penn is the most complex, well-drawn president ever put on screen and Janney hits a grand slam in every scene she's in."

"Nothing else on TV is as entertaining as this exquisitely stressful show," writes Sarah Dempster in The Guardian, awarding the season five stars. "Several big things happen involving snipers, security breaches and globular Russians smushing their chins into their chest while mumbling about the fall of the west. ... But, as always, it's the small stuff that proves most captivating: the perpetual microshifts in power; the twinges of affection in the interminable game of 4D chess that is the Wylers' marriage." Dempster calls out the "fantastic" cast: "Not least Whitford as the terminally uninterested Penn and Sewell as the self-serving Hal, the latter's performance a succession of fleeting tics conducted behind cheekbones that could dislodge the cobwebs from a proscenium arch."

But it's not all positive. IndieWire's Ben Travers argues that the series has lost its bite, given Kate's efforts to make peace with Hal. "As healthy as that might be for their marriage, it's a disaster for The Diplomat," he writes. "Seeing the Wylers work as a team may be a nice, cozy idea, but it neuters the drama, shifting all the weight onto political maneuverings that are simultaneously too close to reality to offer effective escapism and too far from reality to provide cathartic insight. ... Without the usual marriage drama, the political drama edges too close to a polite simulation of what's really going on in Washington, which spoils any hope of suspending disbelief." Ultimately he grades the season a C.
