‘Why William’s rewriting of the royal rule book is not as radical as it sounds’

TheTools · 24.11.2026 18:00 · source · permalink
  • Analysis
  • Lifestyle
  • Royals
  • European royals

November 24, 2026 - 6.00pm

Analysis: Prince William’s rumoured plans to strip his brother, and other relatives, of their titles when he becomes King has provoked renewed scrutiny around privilege, status and who should benefit from their royal connections.

The Prince of Wales is considering a radical shakeup of the monarchy, hoping to establish clearer rules about what it means to be a prince or princess and the role of working royals.

Prince William’s rumoured plans to remove the royal titles of his relatives when he’s King would be in line with other major royal houses across Europe. Samir Hussein - Pool/Getty Images

That’s according to a new book titled Dukes Of Hazard by Tom Sykes, the Daily Beast’s European editor-at-large.

Kensington Palace has refused to comment on the claims in line with their policy of not providing commentary on books about the royal family.

The claims made by Sykes have drawn intense interest over the past couple of days, with news coverage including debates from historians, royal commentators and general observers.

But William’s reported rewriting of the royal rule book is not as radical as it first sounds.

If the changes were to happen − and it’s very likely they will eventuate after the change of throne − it would bring the British monarchy in line with some of the other big royal houses of Europe who are leading the way in modernising their own families in the name of progress and freedom.

Prince William would not be the first to strip his relatives of their titles, with many high-profile royals having lost theirs in recent years. Some have accepted the changes graciously while others have spoken openly about their hurt in losing their royal birthright.

According to Sykes, William’s family restructure would see him take aim at his estranged brother Prince Harry and his wife Meghan.

Prince Harry and Meghan, and their two children, would lose their titles and status under William’s rumoured changes. Getty Images

They would lose their Duke and Duchess of Sussex titles, which they have retained despite stepping back as working royals in 2020.

The couple would also lose their His/Her Royal Highness styling, an honorific currently in abeyance and not able to be used following the Sandringham Summit just before their departure six years ago.

Prince Archie and Princess Lilibet, the couple’s two children, would also lose their prince and princess titles.

The titles, and the right to be called His/Her Royal Highness, would apply to only those working in service of the crown; that is, those royals who actively carry out official duty representing the monarch.

It would also clear up the rules around who receives public funding and grace-and-favour homes (free accommodation as a ‘thank you’ for service).

A source is quoted in the book as saying: “If you are a working royal − going out, touring the country, doing diplomatic work, doing government work, having a public role, pressing the flesh, getting out of bed on a wet Wednesday to go to Sheffield − then you will be an HRH and you will have a prince or princess title. You will also get an allowance, a salary and a place to live. One place to live.

Prince William wants to establish clearer rules about what it means to be a prince or princess and the role of working royals, a new book claims. Getty

“If you are not doing those things and you are not a working royal, you will not get to be an HRH, you will not get a free or subsidised place to live and you will not get any special favours. You will, however, be a private citizen free to get on with your life and make money any way you choose.”

William’s cousins Princess Beatrice and Princess Eugenie, the daughters of Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor, would also have their titles cut.

Sykes told Newsweek:  “It’s absolutely essential. In terms of Harry and Meghan, it should have been done at the Sandringham Summit and instead of saying to them ‘your titles are going into abeyance’ they should have said, ‘for clarity, let’s just remove the HRH.’ At that stage, it would have been enough if they had just done it then”.

He added: “The best time to change the rules was six years ago, the second best time was when Charles became king, the third best time is now but Charles isn’t going to do it because he has cancer and a lot on his plate and he doesn’t want to have a big fight with his son, so William’s going to have to do it.”

William’s changes would also have implications for his two younger children Princess Charlotte and Prince Louis.

They would be offered the opportunity to relinquish their own titles and forge careers outside the royal family, affording them a freedom usually only available to lower-ranked royals.

This group photo, taken at Buckingham Palace in April, shows the 11 working members of the British royal family. Instagram/TheRoyalFamily

They would also be fully aware of the costs of losing their status: no public income and taxpayer-funded police protection − two issues which Prince Harry admitted to being shocked by. He is still battling with the British government to reinstate his own security.

Being transparent about what it would mean to give up a royal title would prevent family disagreements, Sykes said.

“Louis is the one they’re worried about,” he told Newsweek. “That’s what this is all about. It’s about the next generation and ensuring the same problem doesn’t happen again.

Prince William and Catherine’s children, except for heir Prince George, would be able to choose if they want to retain their royal titles, author Tom Sykes says. Getty Images

"It's about two things. It's about creating an off ramp for someone who wants to leave the royal family so its clear what's going on. And it's about when someone leaves the royal family they cannot set up a rival court calling themselves a prince of the United Kingdom."

Prince George, as heir to the throne, would not be affected.

The royals who’ve been stripped of their titles

While the House of Windsor remains the most prominent of Europe’s main royal families, they remain one of the slowest to modernise.

The royal houses of Denmark and Sweden, two of the oldest monarchies in the region, have both made major changes in the past decade.

Others, including Norway, the Netherlands and Spain, have very few working royals with the bulk of official duties handled by their respective kings and queens and their children.

Queen Margrethe with her eight grandchildren in April 2023, just months after she decided to downgrade four of them to the lesser ranks of count and countess. Patrick van Katwijk/Getty Images

In September 2022, Denmark’s former monarch Queen Margrethe II announced the four children of her youngest son Prince Joachim − her grandchildren − would no longer be able to use their prince and princess titles.

The decree not only surprised regular Danes but also foreign observers, who were caught off-guard by the move.

At the time Queen Margrethe said the decision was made so Prince Joachim’s children would be “able to shape their own lives to a much greater extent without being limited by” their royal titles... and their “formal affiliation with the Royal House of Denmark as an institution”.

Margrethe also said it was “a necessary safeguard for the future of the monarchy”.

Some saw the decision as a step towards her eventual abdication in early 2024, sparing her son − the now-King Frederik X − from making the tough choice to strip his nieces and nephews of their titles in the future.

In 2022, Count Nikolai (third from left) and his siblings lost their princely titles and were downgraded to the status of count and countess following a controversial decision by their grandmother Queen Margrethe II.  Zahra Khadir

Margrethe said as much in an interview to Danish newspaper Weekendavisen several months later.

"For me, it has been important that it should not be Frederik's lot to make such a decision. It was better that it was me," she said.

"Because then it is the old woman who has decided it."

In an extraordinary turn of events, Margrethe later issued an apology for causing her family any distress, admitting she “underestimated” how much they would “feel affected”.

Prince Joachim’s four children were downgraded from prince and princess to the lesser-rank of count and countess and also lost their HRH status. Their places in the line of succession were unchanged.

Margrethe’s decree was swiftly met with anger and shock from Prince Joachim and his family, who all claimed to have been surprised by the move.

King Frederik and Queen Mary have previously said the titles, and resposibilities, of their three youngest children would be looked at one day. The Danish Royal House

Prince Joachim said they were all "very sad", adding, "It's never fun to see your children being harmed. They have been put in a situation they do not understand".

Princess Marie said their daughter, Athena, has been "bullied at school": "They come and say: 'Is it you who is no longer a princess?'"

“We would have liked to have had time to talk about it,” Princess Marie said.

Count Nikolai, the eldest of the grandchildren affected, said he was “very confused as to why it has to happen like this”.

"My whole family and I are of course very sad. We are, as my parents have also stated, in shock at this decision and at how quickly it has actually gone," he told Ekstra Bladet.

In an interview with nine.com.au a year after the change, Nikolai admitted it was “still quite a touchy subject”.

“I mean, I am the same person as I’ve always been,” he said. “It’s more just a formality or sort of change of belonging, in a way.

“Not that I had any formal responsibilities before, but now I am officially free of them anyway.”

His mother, and Joachim’s ex-wife, Countess Alexandra, had previously said the family was “saddened and in shock”, adding her children could not “understand why their identity is being taken away”.

Count Nikolai and his brother Count Felix joined their grandmother, and the royal family, at Fredensborg Palace in April last year for her birthday. Getty Images

"This comes like a bolt from the blue. The children feel ostracised."

Four years on, Count Nikolai and his three siblings Count Felix, Count Henrik and Countess Athena, remain much-loved members of the Danish royal family.

While they do not perform official duties, they are frequently included at family-centric events.

The four children of King Frederik and Queen Mary remain unaffected, however they have previously said the titles of Princess Isabella, Prince Vincent and Princess Josephine − and their own allowances − would be looked at in the future. Isabella and her brother, heir Crown Prince Christian, are both serving in the Armed Forces and have turned down their military pensions.

In Sweden, King Carl XVI Gustaf’s similar decision was met with much less controversy than in neighbouring Denmark.

The four children of Prince Carl Philip and Princess Sofia were affected by changes made by Sweden’s king in 2019. Getty

The royal children of Sweden made headlines in 2019 when the king revoked the royal status of five of his grandchildren, stripping them of the titles of ‘royal highness’ and freeing them from future royal duties.

The children also cannot claim the taxpayer-funded ‘appanage’, or allowance, that is given to members of the royal house.

The decision applied to the children Prince Carl Philip and Princess Sofia (they had two at the time and have since welcomed two more) and Princess Madeleine’s three children, but not those of Crown Princess Victoria as they are in the direct line of succession.

Princess Madeleine’s three children also lost their royal status but have retained the right to be known as prince and princess. Michael Campanella/Getty Images

However, all of King Carl XVI Gustaf’s grandchildren are considered part of the royal house and have been allowed to keep their princely titles but do not have HRH status.

King Carl XVI Gustaf's grandchildren all hold duchies, as do their parents.

In a previous interview, Prince Carl Philip said the lack of an HRH title for his children was "positive".

Princess Madeleine also agreed with her father, saying it would give the children “a greater opportunity to shape their own lives”.
