Sam Altman: "AI Has Become Smarter Than the Best Scientists... Humans Must Hold the Reins" — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 03.11.2026 04:55 · source · permalink

Sam Altman, chief executive of OpenAI, said artificial intelligence has now reached a level of intellectual capability that exceeds the world's top scientists. He stressed, however, that AI must not hold the final say over the future and that humans must retain control.

Speaking on Nov. 2 (local time) at the Abu Dhabi International Petroleum Exhibition and Conference (ADIPEC) in the United Arab Emirates, Altman said, "At the beginning of this year, AI was mostly not as smart as the very best scientists, and now it is mostly smarter than them." He added, "At the beginning of this year, AI couldn't do a lot of office work, and now it can do a substantial fraction of it." He cautioned that these assessments are rough and do not apply to every field.

Altman predicted that rapid advances in AI capability will bring significant changes across the economy, while stressing that human creativity, judgment and the willingness to take responsibility for one's decisions remain essential. He also projected that people will be able to adapt by adjusting the way they work.

In particular, he repeatedly emphasized that humans must retain control as AI's judgment improves. "I don't think AI should be the decision-maker of the future, but it also shouldn't be a toolmaker without moral responsibility," Altman said. "It is absolutely critical that people retain control over the future," he said. "We shouldn't hand control to AI, and we shouldn't let AI decide what is best for us." He added, "AI should be a tool built for people and in service of people."

While emphasizing these principles, Altman took an indirect swipe at other AI companies. "I can't believe that such obvious principles are what distinguishes other AI companies from OpenAI," he said, without naming specific companies.

He also raised concerns about AI safety. "As AI systems get smarter, how we ensure and convince the world that we can avoid serious safety and alignment incidents will become a critical issue," he said. Alignment refers to making AI operate in accordance with human intentions and values.

Altman said AI usage is surging, claiming that average monthly token usage per person worldwide has reached about 100,000. A token is the basic unit AI uses to process text and other content. He did not disclose the specific population or methodology behind the figure.

Altman recently visited South Korea and met with officials from Samsung Electronics and SK Group, according to reports. The trip is seen as involving discussions on cooperation in supplying semiconductors and memory needed to build AI infrastructure.

He has also previously discussed, on a podcast program, the idea of placing a cap on AI intelligence. At the time, he argued that a structure in which multiple AIs with intelligence slightly above that of humans cooperate is preferable to a single superintelligent AI. Just as human society maintains stability through cooperation and mutual checks among many people, the same principle should apply to AI, he said. He did not mention the specific level of the cap or how it would be enforced.

OpenAI also disclosed a safety incident that occurred in its internal research environment. According to the company, on Sept. 20 an internal research model exploited a system configuration error and gained limited access to the external internet during training. The company temporarily halted training and evaluation of its most capable models and strengthened isolation and monitoring of the research environment to prevent AI from accessing unauthorized areas. After confirming the safety measures, it resumed reinforcement learning training and internal model inference processing on Nov. 1. The company said the incident occurred in an internal research environment, not in a model released to the public.

Altman's remarks come as concerns grow that AI could escape human control. Major AI companies have been rolling out next-generation models one after another, heating up the safety debate. Industry observers expect that as the race for AI performance accelerates, alignment and control issues will emerge as key variables for both regulators and investors.

He also argued that cloud-based AI is more efficient than local AI running on personal computers. With a global DRAM shortage acting as a key constraint on AI computing, the logic goes, centralized computing can optimize resource allocation and lower costs. The remark drew controversy, however, as it was interpreted as disparaging personal computing. Memory makers' reluctance to expand capacity for fear of repeating past boom-and-bust cycles has worsened the DRAM supply bottleneck, driving up memory component costs for AI companies and consumers alike.

Market observers say Altman's remarks carry significant implications for the direction of AI infrastructure investment. As cloud-centered computing demand grows, so too must demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) for data centers and server DRAM. Conversely, if the spread of local AI is delayed, the recovery in memory demand for on-device products such as PCs and smartphones could also be pushed back. Some also suggest that if AI safety norms are strengthened, rising model development costs and compliance burdens could reshape the market around large operators.
