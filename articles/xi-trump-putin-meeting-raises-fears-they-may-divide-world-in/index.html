Xi-Trump-Putin meeting raises fears they may divide world into ‘spheres of influence’

TheTools · 16.11.2026 21:00 · source · permalink

Twenty-one leaders are set to descend Tuesday on the Chinese city of Shenzhen for this year’s Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit. But all eyes will be on the trio of Xi Jinping, Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin.

The Chinese Foreign Affairs Ministry said a trilateral meeting of the Chinese, U.S. and Russian presidents will be held Thursday, after the conclusion of the two-day APEC summit, and there are already growing concerns that the world’s three largest nuclear powers could reach broad understandings that ignore the interests of smaller countries.

Mao Ning, a spokeswoman for the ministry, said Monday that the three leaders would discuss “safeguarding world peace and promoting strategic stability in major-power relations.” She said Thursday’s meeting would be “of groundbreaking significance.”

It has drawn nervous comparisons to a tripartite gathering in February 1945 in the Crimean resort city of Yalta. There, U.S. president Franklin Roosevelt, Soviet leader Joseph Stalin, and British prime minister Winston Churchill met to draw up the postwar order.

The comparison is welcomed in Moscow, which has called for new European security arrangements to be agreed upon as part of U.S.-led negotiations aimed at ending the war in Ukraine. “The task is to create a comprehensive system of comprehensive security in Europe that will become a new, modern Yalta,” Russia’s chief negotiator, Vladimir Medinsky, said last week, following the latest round of peace talks in Abu Dhabi.

But talk of another Yalta rings alarm bells in Kyiv and other capitals. The 1945 summit left half of Europe under Soviet domination, setting the stage for the Cold War, which lasted for the next four decades.

“It’s dangerous when Russia wants to begin these discussions with a new security infrastructure in Europe,” Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said in an interview with CNN after the Abu Dhabi meeting. “We don’t want to have Yalta 2.0. We don’t want to be divided by Russia.”

Opinion: A Trump-Xi-Putin summit would be trouble for the world at large

European leaders, who will not be present at the APEC summit, are concerned that Mr. Xi, Mr. Trump and Mr. Putin will discuss dividing the world up once more into “spheres of influence.” Such an arrangement could give Russia free rein to continue its war in Ukraine, while the U.S. and China would agree not to interfere in each other’s regions of interest.

“It’s pretty obvious that they want to create an image of a tripolar world, where you have the U.S., China, Russia – and that’s it when it comes to big issues,” said Sergey Utkin, a resident research fellow at the Institute for Peace Research and Security Policy at the University of Hamburg.

While Russia under Mr. Putin has sought for the past 25 years to restore its dominance over neighbouring countries that were once part of the Soviet Union, what’s new is that a sitting U.S. president sees the world in similar terms. Mr. Trump has revived the Monroe Doctrine – a 19th-century idea that calls for U.S. hegemony over all of North and South America – and rebranded it the “Donroe Doctrine.”

In agreeing to host the Shenzhen meeting, Mr. Xi has signalled that he also sees merit in discussing major issues exclusively between the three powers.

The consequences of any agreements the trio reach could be extensive. In addition to seeking Kyiv’s capitulation, Russian negotiators have also sought a withdrawal of NATO soldiers and military equipment from Poland and the Baltic states, including from Latvia, where Canada leads a multinational brigade of NATO troops. Russia has also rejected the idea of European peacekeepers being deployed to Ukraine, even as Britain and France are currently leading an 11-nation military exercise in Poland aimed at bolstering readiness for a potential deployment to Ukraine.

In Asia, U.S. allies such as Taiwan, South Korea and Japan are wary of being abandoned as part of any deal. The Middle East, where the three powers have overlapping interests, could also be up for discussion.

Experts say it’s unlikely that such large issues will be decided at Shenzhen, given how little preparation appears to have gone into the meeting. As of this weekend, the three sides hadn’t yet agreed on an agenda or even a format for the three-way talks.

“I think that the most important thing for them is the photo − the picture of the three of them together,” said Carmen Claudin, a non-resident senior researcher at the Barcelona Centre for International Affairs. “I don’t think there would be any substantial content to the discussions.”

Ms. Claudin said European leaders will nonetheless be squirming as they watch events in Shenzhen from afar. “Where is Europe? Where are we? I mean, we’re not there, and we’re not at the negotiating table regarding the war against Ukraine. I mean, it’s very sad.”

With its limited agenda, Thursday’s meeting in Shenzhen may wind up having more in common with the 1943 Tehran Conference between Mr. Roosevelt, Mr. Churchill and Stalin that preceded the much more consequential Yalta gathering two years later.

One potentially positive outcome from Shenzhen could be a resumption of nuclear arms-control talks. Mr. Trump has walked away from a series of U.S.-Russia pacts that restricted the number of nuclear weapons each side could have, arguing that any new arrangements had to include China, which has the world’s third-largest arsenal.

Beijing, with an estimated 620 warheads, has thus far refused to sign on to nuclear arms-control treaties, saying it is up to Russia and the U.S. – which both have upwards of 5,000 warheads – to reduce their arsenals before any caps are placed on China.

“To agree to be part of some trilateral format to discuss nuclear issues, if this is achieved, would be at least a small step in terms of strategic stability on the global level,” Mr. Utkin said. “But it’s not necessarily something that would make the end of the war in Ukraine any closer.”
