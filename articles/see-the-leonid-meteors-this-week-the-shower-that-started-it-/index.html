See the Leonid meteors this week — ‘the shower that started it all’ | CNN

TheTools · 16.11.2026 11:00 · source · permalink

Each November, Earth passes through a trail of debris left behind by comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, creating the annual celestial display known as the Leonid meteor shower.

As tiny pieces of the comet fizzle in the atmosphere, they create meteors that can be seen blazing across the night sky.

This year, the shower is expected to peak overnight from Monday through Tuesday, when Earth passes through the densest part of the debris trail, according to the American Meteor Society. Under clear conditions, sky-gazers could see up to 15 meteors per hour, said Bill Cooke, lead of NASA’s Meteoroid Environment Office.

“You’ll need a really dark sky,” Cooke said. “Leonids are faint, so you’re not going to see any Leonids from the suburb.”

He recommends finding a spot away from artificial lights and avoiding looking at your phone, which can interfere with your eyes’ ability to adjust to the darkness.

The best time to spot these fleeting meteors will be in the early morning hours, around 2 or 3 a.m., in any time zone, Cooke said. That’s when the constellation Leo, from where the meteors appear to originate, is higher in the sky.

The moon, which will be in its waxing crescent phase and 39% illuminated, will also have set by that time, leaving darker skies for viewing.

The Leonids will be visible from both the Northern and Southern hemispheres, although the constellation Leo appears lower in the sky south of the equator, potentially limiting the number of visible meteors in that region, according to the American Meteor Society.

Special equipment isn’t needed to enjoy the celestial show. “Lie on your back and take in as much sky as you can. No telescopes, no binoculars, just your eyes,” Cooke said. “It’s cold out there, but you know, to see the Leonids, you got to undergo a little discomfort.”

Meteor storms from the Leonids

The Leonids are known for producing spectacular meteor storms, which occur when at least 1,000 meteors streak across the sky per hour, according to NASA. One of the most famous meteor storms happened in 1833 when observers reportedly witnessed tens of thousands of meteors per hour.

“People thought the world was ending. There are all sorts of stories in the old newspapers from that time,” Cooke said. “That really rocked people’s boat back then.”

That unusually large flurry of meteors also helped spark the scientific study of the celestial objects as astronomers began investigating what caused the streaks of light.

“It was the Leonids that gave birth to meteor science back in the early 19th century, so without the Leonids, we wouldn’t know nearly as much about meteors as we do now,” Cooke said. “They are the shower that started it all.”

The Leonids can produce meteor storms roughly every 33 years when the shower’s parent comet nears the sun. As the comet approaches the sun, it heats up and releases more debris, which Earth can later pass through, said Meredith MacGregor, an associate professor in the department of physics and astronomy at Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore.

However, meteor storms aren’t guaranteed every three decades as the gravitational pull of Jupiter and other planets can alter the path of the comet’s debris. Scientists expect the next Leonid meteor storm to occur in 2033, Cooke said.

A meteor storm isn’t expected this year, but MacGregor said watching the annual shower Monday through Tuesday still offers a unique opportunity to connect with the cosmos.

“You’re going out and you’re seeing material coming into our atmosphere that originated in the tail of a comet that came from the outer parts of our solar system,” MacGregor said, “and you actually get to see that with your own eyes.”

Upcoming meteor showers

Meteors from the Leonids are expected to be seen flaring in the sky until the shower’s finality on November 30, according to the American Meteor Society. If you are eager to see more, here are the remaining meteor showers that peak in 2026:

  • Geminids: December 13-14
  • Ursids: December 21-22

Full moons

There are two full moons remaining in 2026, according to the Farmers’ Almanac. Both are supermoons, when the full moon appears larger and brighter than usual since it will be closest to Earth in its orbit.

  • November 24: Beaver moon
  • December 23: Cold moon
