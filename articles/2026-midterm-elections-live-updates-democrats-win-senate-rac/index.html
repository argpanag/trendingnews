Here’s the latest.

Democrats got off to a strong start in their quest to regain control of Congress on Tuesday, securing victories in Senate races in Georgia, North Carolina and New Hampshire as the party’s candidates appeared to be outperforming expectations according to early returns.

Senator Jon Ossoff of Georgia, a Democrat, fended off his challenger in a crucial battleground race, according to The Associated Press, while Roy Cooper, the former governor of North Carolina, flipped a Republican-held seat there. Representative Chris Pappas, a Democrat, held a Senate seat for his party in New Hampshire, a purple state that both parties viewed as critical.

Polls have closed in a number of states with tense Senate races, including Texas, Iowa, Kansas and Michigan. Turnout appeared strong across the country, as millions of people cast votes that will deliver a verdict on an unpopular president who has implored the country to act as if he were on the ballot.

Democratic candidates have blamed President Trump, and the war he started this year in Iran, for the economic hardships that have driven a current of frustration across the nation. Republicans have focused on portraying their Democratic opponents as extreme on social issues and the economy as they played defense in reliably red states.

Democrats flipped a House seat in North Carolina’s 11th Congressional District, where Jamie Ager, a fourth-generation farmer, buoyed their hopes of taking control of the chamber. In Florida, they turned back an attempt by Republicans to flip two seats via redistricting, with Representative Jared Moskowitz and Representative Kathy Castor winning re-election in districts that Republicans redrew in an effort to oust them.

Florida Republicans fended off a significant challenge in a Senate race: Senator Ashley Moody defeated Angie Nixon, a progressive Democrat, in a tight contest to complete the term of Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

In South Carolina, Senator Darline Graham has been filling the seat long occupied by her late brother, Lindsey, and Democrats have hoped that Dr. Annie Andrews, a pediatrician, could flip the seat. In Ohio — which also features an acrimonious race for governor — former Senator Sherrod Brown, a Democrat, is vying for a comeback as he tries to oust Senator Jon Husted, a Republican.

The last polling places will not close until 1 a.m. Eastern on Wednesday, in Alaska. Full results won’t be known until well into the evening — and maybe not for days or longer, particularly if many of the races are close — but it’s clear that voters were eager to have their say. In each of the major Senate battlegrounds, including Texas, North Carolina and Michigan, early turnout eclipsed that of the last midterm cycle.

In Texas, overall turnout could exceed 10 million votes, which would top the total from each of the last two midterms. In Georgia, Election Day turnout was on track to beat that of the 2024 presidential election, the Georgia secretary of state said.

Gov. Kathy Hochul of New York fends off a Trump ally to capture a second full term.

Gov. Kathy Hochul, a Democrat whose folksy, centrist approach has helped her forge relationships with leaders as disparate as President Trump and Mayor Zohran Mamdani, won a second full term as governor of New York on Tuesday, according to The Associated Press.

Ms. Hochul, the state’s first female governor, defeated Bruce Blakeman, the Nassau County executive, a Republican who is a longtime friend of Mr. Trump’s. Their alliance proved auspicious for Ms. Hochul, a Buffalo native who spent months bashing her opponent for supporting the Trump administration’s policies.

But Ms. Hochul, 68, has also displayed an ability to work with the president, despite their profound differences. She worked with the Trump administration on new energy projects, including the jump-starting of several stalled natural gas pipeline projects, and has looked for moments to influence the administration’s immigration enforcement in the state.

At the same time, the governor fought the Trump administration’s efforts to kill the congestion pricing program, which is on track to raise $15 billion for transit improvements by charging vehicles to enter the Manhattan business district.

At one point, Mr. Trump posted on social media, “CONGESTION PRICING IS DEAD. Manhattan, and all of New York, is SAVED. LONG LIVE THE KING!” In response Ms. Hochul said: “We are a nation of laws, not ruled by a king. We’ll see you in court.” (New York ended up prevailing in court, and the toll remained in effect).

The Associated Press called the race just as the polls closed at 9 p.m.

Ms. Hochul’s re-election bid became as much a referendum on the actions coming from the White House as on policies flowing from the State Capitol in Albany, N.Y. The governor’s campaign focused on Mr. Blakeman’s fidelity to Mr. Trump in its tens of millions of dollars in attack ads against her rival.

“We have a real record of accomplishment,” she said on Sunday at a Queens sports bar while campaigning with Mr. Mamdani and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez.

“When you’re delivering universal child care, when you’re delivering record investments in education, when you’re delivering health care despite all the Trump administration cutbacks, when you’re focusing on affordability and what we can do to help lift up New Yorkers,” Ms. Hochul said, “that is the message that is resonating.”

Despite his best efforts, Mr. Blakeman, 71, a longtime player in Republican politics on Long Island, struggled to shake loose from his association with Mr. Trump.

After winning re-election as county executive last fall, Mr. Blakeman entered the governor’s race, setting up a primary against Representative Elise Stefanik, a well-known staunch defender of Mr. Trump. But Ms. Stefanik abruptly dropped out and announced that she would not seek re-election to the House.

Mr. Blakeman used his campaign messaging to try to attract disaffected Democrats and independent voters who have been burdened by rising energy costs and high taxes.

But he was dogged by fund-raising challenges and temporarily waylaid by irregularities in his required filings to the state. At one point he was removed from the campaign finance matching program and had to sue to regain access.

In the end he spent around $24 million on ads, according to AdImpact, just a bit shy of what Ms. Hochul spent, and benefited from help from a group that was seeded with donations from James Dolan, the owner of the New York Knicks.

For Ms. Hochul, winning a second term strengthens her own legacy, and opens the door for her to match two recent predecessors, Mario M. Cuomo and George E. Pataki, who each served three full terms.

She entered office in August 2021, moving up from lieutenant governor after Andrew M. Cuomo resigned amid sexual harassment allegations that he denies. Her first year in office was defined by efforts to stabilize state government, which was still dealing with the pandemic and the fallout of scandals related to Mr. Cuomo.

Her election to a full term in 2022 proved to be closer than expected, leading Nancy Pelosi, the former House speaker, to accuse Ms. Hochul of dragging down the Democratic ticket when the party lost five House seats in the state. She struggled initially to pass big-ticket policy items related to housing and to win approval for her preferred pick for the state’s top judge.

She also drew criticism for initially withdrawing her support of congestion pricing, only to reverse her decision shortly after her election, while lowering the charge from $15 to $9 for most vehicles.

Throughout much of her first full term, Ms. Hochul, a former Erie County clerk, was viewed warily by legions of progressive voters in New York City. She began to find her footing after Mr. Trump’s re-election in 2024, when she became a vocal bulwark against Washington.

Still, faced with middling approval numbers and numerous rivals from both parties preparing to challenge her, she seemed far from certain to win a year ago. But several primary challenges from Democrats either sputtered or never materialized.

One such bid came from her lieutenant governor, Antonio Delgado, who had hoped to challenge her from the left. His campaign was undermined by Ms. Hochul’s relationship with Mr. Mamdani, who endorsed the governor last winter. Mr. Delgado dropped out after the state party convention and was replaced on the ticket with Adrienne Adams, the former speaker of the New York City Council.

Mr. Mamdani’s support followed a similar gesture from Ms. Hochul, who became one of the most prominent Democrats to endorse him during his mayoral run. Since his victory, the two have built a surprisingly fruitful governing partnership despite profound ideological differences on a number of fronts, including raising taxes and support for Israel.

Ultimately, Mr. Mamdani’s support for Ms. Hochul appeared to be crucial, helping her to excite the younger, more progressive coalition of voters that propelled his mayoral campaign to victory last year. Over the last month they appeared together repeatedly; Mr. Mamdani also filmed several videos highlighting their work together to expand child care and resolve the city’s budgetary woes.

Trump officials pledge to arrest noncitizen voters.

As polls closed in several states on Tuesday evening, there were no signs that federal immigration agents had been deployed in large numbers to voting sites, a widespread concern among civil rights groups and immigrant advocates leading up to the election.

But Trump administration officials made clear in a flurry of social media posts on Tuesday that noncitizens who illegally voted would be hunted down. Markwayne Mullin, the homeland security secretary, also indicated that federal agents would scour voter records after the midterm elections to try to find unlawful voters.

“If you vote illegally, we will find you,” Immigration and Customs Enforcement said in one post. “We will track you down, arrest you, and prosecute you. There will be no escape.”

ICE’s account also promoted about a half dozen arrests throughout the day, posting photos depicting handcuffed immigrants accused of illegally voting, mostly in past elections. “ANOTHER VOTER FRAUD ARREST!” the agency wrote in one post.

The remarks signaled that the administration planned to continue its search for noncitizens who illegally registered to vote or actually voted, an effort that it has already dedicated massive resources to. Many studies have found noncitizen voting to be rare, but President Trump has falsely called the issue rampant. Mr. Mullin and other administration officials have said that any amount of noncitizen voting is unacceptable.

In a Fox News interview on Tuesday, Mr. Mullin said he had “made it very clear that ICE agents aren’t going to be around polling places unless there’s a threat.” But he indicated that his department would scrutinize voter data after the election.

“After this election we’ll be gathering all the data. We’ll be gathering the voting logs, and we’ll be going through those and making sure that no illegals voted,” Mr. Mullin said.

The number of people who have been charged so far make up a small share of the more than 158 million ballots cast in the 2024 general election. The administration has charged about 240 people with voting fraud-related offenses since Mr. Trump returned to office, according to the Justice Department.

As of Oct. 15, 25 people had been found guilty or pleaded guilty, a New York Times review of court records found. Two had been acquitted, and charges were dropped or cases were dismissed in four other instances.

Several election experts and immigrant advocacy groups have denounced the efforts, saying that the administration’s intense focus on the issue was not producing evidence that noncitizen voting was widespread, and that it instead could be intimidating voters.

The Senate will get its first openly gay male senator.

Representative Chris Pappas, Democrat of New Hampshire, will be the Senate’s first openly gay male senator with his victory over former Senator John E. Sununu, a Republican, on Tuesday.

Like other candidates on the precipice of breaking a barrier, he did not spend much time emphasizing the milestone on the campaign trail. But it is a notable breakthrough in the Senate, as L.G.B.T.Q. rights have faced pushback in some states and some gay rights activists have warned about a decline in public support.

While his husband was in attendance at some of his campaign events, Mr. Pappas largely focused his message on the themes that most Democrats highlighted: a pledge to deal with high prices and health care costs, and opposition to Washington corruption, among others.

“I think people, regardless of their politics, really just want their neighbors to be able to be themselves,” Mr. Pappas told The New York Times in October.

Representative Barney Frank, Democrat of Massachusetts, voluntarily came out as gay in 1987, after six years of service in the House. Decades later, Tammy Baldwin, Democrat of Wisconsin, in 2012 became the first openly gay person elected to the Senate.

Before his career in politics took off, Mr. Pappas, 46, was probably most associated with his family’s restaurant in Manchester, N.H., the Puritan Backroom. His grandfather is said to have invented the chicken tender there.

At 22, Mr. Pappas ran for state representative in New Hampshire, winning the race. That set off a winding political career in the state, before he joined the wave of Democrats in 2018 that capitalized on anger against President Trump during his first term.

Mr. Pappas has served for four terms in the House and is a chair of the Congressional Equality Caucus. He will replace Senator Jeanne Shaheen, who did not seek re-election after three terms.

Ashley Moody will complete Marco Rubio’s term in the Senate in Florida.

Senator Ashley Moody of Florida, the Republican appointed last year to replace Marco Rubio, won a special election on Tuesday to complete his term, according to The Associated Press. She fended off a challenge from State Representative Angie Nixon, the Democratic nominee, who had mounted a grass-roots campaign that kept the race closer than expected.

Ms. Moody’s victory cemented Republicans’ dominance in recent Florida Senate races. Republican Senate candidates have won every time they have been on the ballot in the state since 2018 as the former political battleground has become more reliably Republican.

Ms. Moody, 51, will now get to serve two more years in the Senate. She will be able to seek re-election to a full term in 2028.

That she faced so much of a contest this year in a state that President Trump won by 13 percentage points two years ago was surprising. Florida Republicans won Senate races by double-digit margins in 2022 and 2024, and Ms. Moody was expected to continue that streak when Gov. Ron DeSantis picked her for the job last year. She was serving a second term as the state’s attorney general at the time.

But Ms. Nixon’s shocking win in the Democratic Senate primary in August jolted the race. She deployed her skills as a former union organizer to score an upset despite having been at a vast fund-raising disadvantage compared with a rival favored by the party establishment.

In the general election, Ms. Nixon, 42, bet that she would be more successful by leaning into progressive policies, such as single-payer health care and universal child care, than by moving to the political center like past Democratic Senate nominees in the state had done. Ms. Nixon would have been Florida’s first Black senator.

She joined the Democratic Socialists of America in June. That party did not endorse her campaign, but Ms. Moody used the democratic socialist label to cast Ms. Nixon as much too radical for the “free state of Florida.”

Ms. Moody had endorsements from Mr. DeSantis and from Mr. Trump, whose unpopularity dragged down Republican candidates across the country. The president rallied twice in Florida in the last days of the campaign, getting loyal supporters to the polls.

Ms. Moody ran as something of a generic Republican, little known to many Florida voters because she had never run for the Senate before — and because her Senate campaign was not very visible, especially compared with Ms. Nixon’s.

But Ms. Nixon was not well known, either, and had much less money to introduce herself to Florida’s big and diverse electorate.

In win for Democrats, Roy Cooper flips a North Carolina Senate seat.

Roy Cooper, a popular former North Carolina governor and a Democrat, won the state’s Senate race on Tuesday, according to The Associated Press. The victory gives Democrats their first flip of the night and an essential victory as the party tries to gain Senate control.

Mr. Cooper, who left the governor’s office last year after two terms, defeated Michael Whatley, the former Republican National Committee chairman. President Trump had handpicked Mr. Whatley to try to hold the Senate seat in a battleground state that has voted for him three times.

But Mr. Whatley proved no match for Mr. Cooper, who has never lost an election in his nearly four decades in North Carolina politics. He is the first Democrat elected to the Senate in North Carolina since 2008.

Mr. Cooper will succeed Senator Thom Tillis, a Republican who had clashed with Mr. Trump and chose to retire after the president threatened him with a primary challenger.

Both parties expected an expensive and contested race between Mr. Cooper, 69, and Mr. Whatley, 58. The two sides spent significantly on political advertising until about a month ago, when Mr. Cooper’s consistent lead in polls began to look insurmountable.

Still, Democrats feared complacency in a state where they have fallen short in past Senate races. Mr. Trump and Vice President JD Vance traveled to North Carolina several times in recent weeks to rally for Mr. Whatley and Republicans in competitive House races.

A longtime Republican political operative, Mr. Whatley campaigned as a loyal Trump supporter and tried to portray Mr. Cooper, who served 16 years as the North Carolina attorney general, as soft on crime. That attack line was ineffective in a year in which voters were focused on the economy.

Mr. Cooper campaigned under the slogan “Make Stuff Cost Less.”

Republicans also tried to paint Mr. Cooper as a radical leftist, but it was difficult to redefine him after he had built a reputation as a moderate over 38 years in elective office.

As governor, Mr. Cooper guided the state through the coronavirus pandemic and three major hurricanes. His signature accomplishment was expanding Medicaid coverage to more than 600,000 people.
