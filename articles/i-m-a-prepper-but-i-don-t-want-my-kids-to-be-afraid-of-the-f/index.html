I’m a prepper. But I don’t want my kids to be afraid of the future

TheTools · 21.09.2026 00:02 · source · permalink

Subscribe to listen

TVNZ documentary Meet The Preppers follows Kiwis preparing for chaos that may or may not come from a major disaster, war or economic collapse. Wellington father Richard Hovey is one of them.

Rich Hovey reckons he owns the perfect car for a prepper: a Prius.

“My neighbour used to have this massive four-wheel drive which everyone assumed was mine,” he laughs.

The Wellingtonian isn’t one for a Mad Max apocalyptic-style war machine, but rather something that will get him from A to B without contributing to the climate crisis.

“I’m, in some ways, the most boring prepper there is.”

According to the Merriam-Webster dictionary, a prepper is someone who “gathers materials and makes plans in preparation for surviving a major disaster or cataclysm, such as worldwide economic collapse or war”.

Hovey first heard of climate change around 1993 and was interested in systems and preparedness for natural disasters. Living in quake-prone Wellington prompted a need to be prepared.

“It wasn’t like something happened in life that probably suddenly set me on the path so much as I was probably going to end up here somehow.”

The industrial designer is analytical, measured and forward-thinking: not exactly the frantic, zombie apocalypse survivor-style prepper often portrayed in film and TV.

Hovey was an outdoorsy kid, with a creative streak. Joining the prepper community came slowly, as he gradually accumulated skills and knowledge over the years.

He takes a practical approach to prepping in everyday life, which he shares in a new TVNZ documentary, Meet The Preppers.

Each morning, he tries to get up in the dark and move through the house without turning on the lights to gain familiarity with his surroundings.

While he’s not keen on the concept of a bunker, there’s plenty of non-perishable food like rice and beans on hand, and a stockpile of chilli flakes because “they’re cheap, and you can buy in bulk, and they make everything better”.

About 100kg of flour is stashed across the house – under the couch, in the shed out back – and to keep morale up if everything turns to custard, there’s a big bag of lollipops. A grab bag with first aid, water and other essentials is never too far away.

Then there’s the selection of knives, swords and hatchets.

Hovey’s teenage children, Otis and Lilly, have been learning self-defence with knives since they were 5. At times, their bedtime stories sounded less like “There once was a princess living in a castle” and more like “Choose three weapons, here’s the scenario, what do you do?”

For as long as they can remember, tactics, diversion and flanking have been part of their life.

“It’s, to a large extent, so they don’t freeze,” Hovey says.

He doesn’t want them to fear the future. He’s trying “not to freak them out”, instead giving them tools he thinks could be important.

Teaching them about self-defence is as much about instilling them with confidence as it is making sure they “know when to just keep walking”.

He has a policy of 100% honesty with his children, even when he doesn’t want to be.

“My kids know that they can ask anything and they’ll always get an answer, which might be, ‘I genuinely don’t know’,” he says.

Otis, 15, and Lilly, 17, recently told their dad, “We’re just screwed, aren’t we? Is climate change so bad now we’re just screwed?”

“I don’t want them to be all like grim and, you know, bunker mentality, everyone’s out to get them. But I want them to be realistic too.

“I have no idea what’s going to pay off,” Hovey admits.

“But at least there’s [the chance] to buckle down and focus on something and accepting that might change.”

People curious about prepping often want to know where to start. For Hovey, it’s building a basic level of capability within yourself, and thinking about your relationships with others.

“There’s a little bit of ‘having your own oxygen mask on first’. [And] you’re no good to anyone if you aren’t good to anyone,” he says.

With Wellington prone to natural disasters such as earthquakes, and the Covid pandemic changing the way many of us live, he believes more people are starting to think the way he does.

“I think on one hand, it’s really good that people are starting to go, ‘We had Covid, or there was a storm, or there was a power cut’. But I also think the cost of entry is getting higher as well. Stuff used to be in the future, whereas getting ready for climate change is [something we need to do] now.”

Many in New Zealand’s prepper community keep to themselves and avoid going public at the risk of being chastised or stereotyped. Hovey is one of the few who has chosen to be public with his lifestyle, referring to his prepping as “bugging in” rather than “bugging out”.

His partner is more private due to job-related restrictions, and doesn’t feature in the documentary, though Hovey says “she’s happy to be known as part of the household”.

There’s no set guide to being a prepper, he says. Some stay secluded, others prefer to face things head-on when and if they happen.

“I’d much rather be around people. [But some people] bunker down, because dealing with people can be painful, as can dealing with people in stressful situations. On the other hand, there are some amazing stories of survival and resilience and community as well.”

Hovey is part of a Wellington group called Dirt, which stands for disaster, innovation, research and teaching.

Here, people of all backgrounds come together to discuss disaster preparedness and simulate scenarios they may come across in a crisis. Theatre director Oscar Arnold helps make the role-playing come to life, with the intention of making those “scary scenarios” a little easier to talk about.

“Part of the reason I’m involved and open is that it’s probably easy to go down a rabbit hole,” Hovey says.

“I don’t even know if there are misconceptions. Like, maybe, actually everyone’s perception is right, and it’s a combination of ‘Whoa, that’s crazy,’ or ‘I kind of feel bad that I don’t want to engage because actually they might be smart’.”

But Hovey and his family are ready to face the future head-on, whatever life throws their way.

“I have no idea what that looks like. Maybe it’s with a backpack and a crossbow. Maybe it’s living in this house while things just kind of get a bit crappier … I don’t know, but I want to find out.”

Meet The Preppers airs Monday, 8.30pm on TVNZ 1 and TVNZ+ as part of the Documentary NZ season.
