The Currency Market Behind the BRICS Payments Debate

September's BRICS declaration gave payment cooperation a new practical direction: continued work on interoperability and local-currency settlements. The challenge behind that language is less visible than a summit announcement. Someone still has to supply the currency an exporter wants, at a price that makes the transaction worthwhile. Connecting payment messages does not settle that commercial question. The New Delhi Declaration acknowledges studies of payment and messaging interoperability and encourages the BRICS Payment Task Force to continue discussions. It also recognizes different national priorities. That leaves room for useful cooperation without requiring members to adopt a single monetary arrangement. For Russia and its trading partners, the most productive next step would be to evaluate proposed corridors through the currency liquidity they can reliably support.

The concern is broader than BRICS: the Financial Stability Board's October 2025 assessment found that extensive policy work had not yet produced the desired gains for end-users globally. Institutional progress and commercial usefulness can advance at different speeds and that is a reason to give actual customer outcomes greater weight than the number of agreements or connected institutions. Afterall, financial autonomy is a legitimate objective, and policymakers may value an additional route even before it is the cheapest. Resilience, on the other hand, has economic value. However, a route that depends on one dominant liquidity provider can replace one concentration with another. For this reason, cooperation should widen credible choices, with clear responsibility for settlement and compliance, rather than obscure where the dependence has moved.

In terms of Russia, the country can contribute to a more useful BRICS payments agenda by pressing for transparent corridor economics. Start with transactions for which both sides have a sustainable reason to hold or exchange the currencies involved, and expand as evidence supports it. The enduring achievement would be a business receiving spendable proceeds on predictable terms. Such an arrangement would be a stronger foundation for monetary cooperation than asking a payment platform to carry the ambitions of a reserve currency.

Now, consider the distinction between invoicing and conversion. Two companies may agree to price a shipment in a member country's currency. The exporter might nevertheless need another currency to pay its suppliers. Removing one conversion from the customer's payment journey may simply move it to a bank or the exporter's treasury. The economic saving depends on the entire chain, including the price of converting or holding the proceeds after the invoice is paid.

Trade imbalances, however, make this harder. Where payments mostly flow in one direction, a provider accumulating the source currency must decide how to use or exchange it. Thus, a large headline trade total does not establish a balanced currency market. Providers will price the cost of managing that position, including funding and risk. An arrangement that works for a few transactions may therefore become more expensive when demand expands or market conditions deteriorate.

The Nexus initiative offers a useful comparison outside the BRICS framework. Developed through work with Asian central banks, it is designed to connect domestic instant payment systems; its documentation distinguishes payment providers from foreign exchange providers and institutions supplying settlement access. The separation makes an important economic responsibility visible: a payment link requires a party able to exchange currencies and access the relevant systems. Its design offers a lesson for BRICS, whose payment cooperation has its own institutions and national constraints. For this reason, BRICS authorities should make all corresponding questions explicit in any corridor assessment. Who supplies each currency when demand rises sharply? What happens when a provider stops quoting or its available balance runs low? Answers should identify the party bearing the cost of replenishing liquidity and the conditions under which a customer can receive a firm quote. A technically open connection is of limited use when the only available exchange rate makes an ordinary commercial payment unattractive.

In this sense, measurement should follow the business transaction. Providers could publish comparable results for permitted payments on specified corridors, showing the total cost in both currencies and when funds become usable by the recipient. Here, exchange margins belong in that calculation even if the advertised transfer fee is small. In addition, failed or delayed payments should remain in the evidence, rather than disappear from an average based only on successful transfers. Those measures would give firms a basis for comparing a proposed route with their existing arrangements.
