Singapore's labour force participation rate dips for 5th year to 67.8% due to ageing population: MOM

TheTools · 27.11.2026 03:00 · source · permalink

The overall proportion of working-age Singapore residents who are either employed or actively seeking employment has dipped further to 67.8 per cent in 2026, as the country officially became a "super-aged" society.

It is the fifth year in a row that the labour force participation rate (LFPR) has decreased, according to Ministry of Manpower (MOM) data published Friday (Nov 27).

In 2025, the rate was 67.9 per cent and in 2024, the rate was 68.2 per cent.

MOM, in its Labour Force in Singapore Advance Release for 2026, attributed the lowering participation rate to the ageing population rather than weaker economic conditions.

"Population ageing is likely to continue to have a dampening effect on our labour force participation rate," said Ang Boon Heng, director of the manpower research and statistics department.

"Our labour force participation rate, when compared with other economies, remains high, and this is testament of the various measures that we have been putting in place to encourage employers to hire, and to encourage people to stay employed.

"We will continue to do more of this."

Across most age groups, the LFPR remained broadly stable or improved when compared to a decade ago.

The ministry noted that only residents aged 15 to 24 had lower participation as more youths pursued further studies and entered the labour force later.

In total, the number of residents outside the labour force rose to 1.18 million in 2026.

Low unemployment rate

The resident unemployment rates remained low overall.

From 2025 to 2026, the professionals, managers, executives and technicians (PMET) unemployment rate was broadly stable at 2.7 per cent, but the non-PMET rate rose from 2.8 per cent to 3.5 per cent, returning to the levels seen in 2023 and 2024.

The long-term unemployment (LTU) rate for non-PMETs increased slightly from 0.5 per cent to 0.7 per cent, driven by residents aged above 50.

"This may partly reflect a general increase in retrenchments among residents in their 50s, and the continued challenges they faced in re-entering employment," the ministry added.

Across the workforce, 88.2 per cent are employees, while the remaining 11.8 per cent are self-employed.

Of the employees, a record high of 91 per cent hold permanent jobs, a slight increase from 90.8 per cent in 2025, according to data.

The proportion of employees on fixed-term contracts remained stable at 7.1 per cent, while casual/on-call employment declined further to 2 per cent.

Those switching jobs more likely to experience income gains

Fewer residents also changed jobs in 2026, with the rate continuing to fall to 5.7 per cent, from 6.2 per cent in 2025.

MOM noted that the decline in job switching was most pronounced among younger residents aged below 30, with job stability cited as the most common reason.

Job switching remained the lowest among older workers aged 50 and over, with about half of older job stayers having been in their current jobs for at least a decade.

But those who switched jobs are more likely to experience real income gains, which refers to income adjusted for inflation. 

Among full-time resident employees aged 25 to 59, 66.5 per cent of those who changed jobs in the past year saw real income gains from 2025 to 2026.

In comparison, 61.4 per cent of those who did not change jobs reported real income gains.

Over a longer five-year period, from 2021 to 2026, it was observed that 70.2 per cent of full-time workers who switched jobs received a higher income as compared to 63.2 per cent among workers who remained at their jobs.

Of the total job switches over the past year, 94.4 per cent were voluntary rather than due to retrenchments, said MOM.

Residents who switched jobs involuntarily were less likely to see real income gains, and less likely to surpass their pre-switch earnings.

According to data, 65.7 per cent of workers who switched jobs involuntarily in 2023 had real incomes above their pre-job change levels, compared with 76.9 per cent among voluntary job switchers.

Rise in median income

For the first time, the median gross monthly income of full-time employed residents crossed $6,000 in 2026.

The nominal median gross monthly income increased by 4.3 per cent over the year, from $5,775 in 2025 to $6,021 in 2026.

At the 20th percentile, nominal income grew by 3.4 per cent from $3,164 to $3,270.

Income growth in 2026 was at a slower pace compared to 2025, with the ministry suggesting that the moderation partly reflected more cautious wage increases.

Broadly, MOM said that forward-looking indicators suggest that firms remained cautious about expanding headcounts and raising wages.

"The Government will continue to support employers and workers," it added.
