Reflecting on 2026 with the year's best political cartoons

  • Topic:Art

David Pope (centre) is announced as 2026's Political Cartoonist of the Year. (ABC News: Adam Kennedy)

The events shaping this year, and no doubt those to come, have been distilled by Australia's top cartoonists and showcased in this year's Behind the Lines exhibition.

The annual exhibition unveils 2026's wittiest and most thought-provoking political cartoons, with artists covering topics from the global oil crisis and the rapid rise of artificial intelligence (AI) to the cost of living.

And of course, the iconic moments from Australian politicians.

The AI Tamer, by Harry Bruce, published in the Cairns Post and Townsville Bulletin in July 2026. (Supplied: Old Parliament House)

Hobart Mercury cartoonist David "Macca" MacArthur's Last Supper 2026 was published on April 2. (Supplied: Old Parliament House)

Mark Knight's cartoon for The Herald Sun, titled What Took You So Long? (Supplied: Old Parliament House)

Pope takes out top award

Canberra-based David Pope was named Political Cartoonist of the Year, his third time winning the award.

Reflecting on the past year, he said today's fast-paced world, with the added challenge of AI and a 24-hour news cycle, made it difficult to pick a single moment to depict.

"There's the inflation story, and how expensive it is for people struggling with everyday bills; there's the rise of AI, the hype of that, the grift that comes with that, the threat that people feel.

"And then there's the ongoing background radiation of the climate, and the climate policy.

David Pope's cartoon, published on June 18, was in response to One Nation leader Pauline Hanson's address to the National Press Club. (ABC News: Adam Kennedy)

"It constantly feels the ground is shifting all the time," Mr Pope said.

"You've got to try and, in those moments, focus on the kind of broader currents rather than the things that were said up on the hill today."

He said cartoonists often tried to speak to a common experience or response to big issues affecting the world, by "trying to find a different way into a story".

"Or to make them sometimes just feel that they're not alone in having the response they're having to the political news.

"The challenge is to find a way that is a human response to stuff that doesn't reinforce passivity or grief."

Climate Breakdown, by David Pope for the Canberra Times. (Supplied: Old Parliament House )

Tributes flow

This year's series also spotlights the legacies of veteran cartoonists, the late Jon Kudelka, and the retiring Cathy Wilcox, who closes out a 37-year career at the Sydney Morning Herald.

After 37 years at The Sydney Morning Herald, Cathy Wilcox published her last cartoon for the outlet on August 28. (Supplied: Old Parliament House)

Behind the Lines curator Matthew Jones said it has been a significant year for the cartooning community.

"This year we lost one of Australia's finest political cartoonists, Jon Kudelka. His work was admired not only by readers, but by fellow cartoonists," Mr Jones said.

He said Ms Wilcox was a "giant of Australian political cartooning".

"We appreciate our cartoonists and their contribution to our democracy, now more than ever."

The exhibition is open at Canberra's Old Parliament House until November 2027.
