Four months, one surgeon in Finland, and hard questions for Sinner's inner circle

TheTools · 26.11.2026 09:53 · source · permalink

Share this article

Jannik Sinner flew to Turku, Finland on Tuesday, handed himself over to knee specialist Lasse Lempainen at the Pihlajalinna Turku Kupittaa clinic, and emerged from surgery with his iliotibial band repaired and his Australian Open defence already over. Within hours of that confirmation from Tennis Australia, the conversation inside Italian tennis had shifted from sympathy to something sharper: had the people around Sinner let him down?

The bluntest answer came from Diego Nargiso, a former Italian professional now working as a television commentator who covered the ATP Finals in Turin last week. Speaking to Fanpage, he left no room for ambiguity. “It was totally underestimated, absolutely,” Nargiso told Fanpage, then laid out exactly where he thought the failure had occurred. “When from July such a decision is made in November, after four months, it is a defeat for everyone: from the player to the staff, to the doctors who followed the conservative path.”

Nargiso was careful not to target any single person. The first to misjudge the severity, he suggested, was probably Sinner himself. The camp around him then followed that lead, and a succession of medical consultants, some of them sought out specifically because Sinner said he wanted “the best in the world”, failed to find a clinical solution that made surgery unnecessary. Four months of that cycle, Nargiso concluded, was a collective failure.

Sinner said he tried everything, but critics ask why that took until November

Sinner has consistently framed the operation as a last resort rather than an avoidable outcome. “Over the past few months, together with my team, we’ve tried everything to treat my injury without surgery,” he wrote on social media on Tuesday. “After some very encouraging progress early on, my knee unfortunately didn’t respond as we’d all hoped.” Conservative treatment nearly worked, then didn’t. Surgery became unavoidable.

Set that account beside Nargiso’s reading and a gap opens. The promising early progress Sinner describes is precisely the detail that frustrates his critics. If the knee showed encouraging signs and then slipped back, repeatedly, over four months, the question becomes why no one drew a harder line earlier. Conservative management of soft-tissue injuries around the knee is legitimate medicine. Extending it for the better part of half a year, while a world-class athlete misses the US Open, the indoor hardcourt swing and now Melbourne, is harder to defend.

Greg Rusedski had already flagged unease before the surgery news broke, noting the absence of encouraging signs on the practice court and the information vacuum around Sinner’s condition. TennisWatcher reported this month that medical opinion on Sinner’s options appeared divided, with one Italian source suggesting his doctors there had not been considering surgery at all when he began consulting specialists abroad.

When Sinner won Wimbledon in July, nobody imagined his year was over

He was 25, defending his title on the grass, beating Alexander Zverev in four sets for his second successive Wimbledon crown. He had just won five Masters 1000 titles on hard courts and clay in the first half of the season. In October he was still talking about targeting the Australian Open, still insisting he would not rush his recovery.

What followed was a painful loop: rest, return to hitting, pain, rest again. Experts watching from outside noted that an iliotibial band problem which refused to settle after weeks of conservative care was raising structural questions, not just inflammatory ones. Nargiso had pointed to Sinner’s distinctive court movement and lean physique as a possible factor months ago, long before surgery became the conclusion. The signs were there for months; the people around him kept hoping circumstances would spare them from acting on those signs.

Now they have been connected in an operating theatre in Finland, and Sinner is expected to miss all competition until at least April or May 2027. That is another four or five months on the sideline for a player who was already absent through the second half of 2026 and watched Alexander Zverev climb past him to world number one in the meantime. By the time Sinner walks onto a clay court in spring, it will have been the better part of ten months since he last competed.

Nargiso insisted he was not pointing a finger at any individual, and his tone throughout was one of genuine frustration rather than score-settling. “It’s a shame,” he said, “and you can’t say that it hasn’t been underestimated.” He is not alleging negligence. He is making a simpler, harder-to-dismiss argument: that the outcome, surgery in late November after an injury sustained in July, is itself the evidence that something went wrong earlier. Carlos Alcaraz, speaking from Turin after winning the ATP Finals, said he was sure Sinner would “come back even stronger.”
