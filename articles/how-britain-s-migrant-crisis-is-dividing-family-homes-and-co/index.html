How Britain’s migrant crisis is dividing family homes – and communities

TheTools · 12.10.2026 07:04 · source · permalink

As asylum seekers are moved from hotels to HMOs, residents say their close-knit streets are falling victim to crime and squalor

Windsor Road in Tuebrook, north-east Liverpool, was once a tidy, friendly street of distinctive red-brick Victorian terraced houses. It was never posh – this is an area proud of its strong, working-class roots – but when Billy Lake, a local councillor, moved to Tuebrook in the mid-1980s with his wife and young children, it was seen as an “affordable aspirational area, somewhere a young family could buy a starter home”.

Now, it is at the sharp end of one of the fastest-growing social problems in modern Britain. Local councillors estimate there are 18 houses in multiple occupation (HMO) dwellings where three or more people live separately, sharing kitchen and bathroom facilities, on this one street alone. The number of HMOs in Liverpool has more than tripled in a decade, from 4,850 to 16,494 last year. 

In part, this will have been fuelled by the expansion of mandatory HMO licensing in 2018, removing a previous stipulation that only buildings with three or more storeys would be included. But the rise in Liverpool has been bigger than in any other local authority.

HMOs are popular with landlords for the simple fact that they can make much more money from a larger group of unrelated adults than from renting to a single family. The prospect has become more appealing as buy-to-let returns have been squeezed by increased borrowing costs, tax rises and, more recently, renters’ rights legislation.

They are, however, almost universally unpopular with neighbours for their unique ability to decimate the character of an area, stripping it of family housing and opening the door to higher rates of antisocial behaviour and crime. In many areas, landlords can convert a house into an HMO without planning permission under permitted development rights, although a licence is required by most councils.

Many HMOs are student flats or let out to young professionals. Some are used for supported living or as halfway houses for ex-offenders and people with drug or alcohol addictions. But it is the increasing number used to house asylum seekers – operated by private companies such as the outsourcing firm, Serco – that has become a growing source of angst for those living near them. As the Government has sought to empty migrant hotels amid a public backlash against them, many asylum seekers have been placed in so-called “dispersal accommodation” around the country, the majority of which are HMOs.

Lake is walking around Tuebrook with Steve Radford, the councillor for the neighbouring ward of Stoneycroft. Along with another local councillor, Joe Dunne, who represents the ward of Tuebrook Breckside Park, they have been fighting a proliferation of HMOs.

All three represent the Liberal Party, originally founded in 1989 by opponents of the original Liberal Party’s merger with the SDP. They call themselves “pavement politicians”, those who are out and about on the streets, speaking to local people. What they have found is a community pushed to breaking point by an explosion of HMOs and the transient, often unsettled tenants they house.

“I moved to Tuebrook 42 years ago,” says Lake. “For many years, it was not a troublesome neighbourhood. It’s always been a very settled community made up of the upwardly mobile working classes.” Now, it is decidedly down on its luck.

On Windsor Road, the HMOs are easily identifiable by the rows of doorbells and key boxes by each front door, and overflowing bins outside. The outliers – those homes still occupied by their owners or rented in single occupancy, even if they are sandwiched between HMOs – are also easy to spot. They have landscaped front gardens and carefully tended hanging baskets of flowers, or signs up in the window bearing slogans such as “SEND THEM BACK” in black capital letters.

“Every Serco house is identifiable by squalor,” says Radford. Behind one such house he points out on a nearby street, rotting food and rubbish spill out of split bin bags. A passing young man greets us surprisingly cheerfully as he steps over a dead rat on the pavement. (A Serco spokesman said the company “does not recognise allegations that our properties are not being appropriately managed or maintained”. The firm has “robust processes in place to ensure our accommodation remains compliant, safe, and properly managed”.)

“The activity of Serco is building resentment where there never was any before,” says Lake. “This has never been that kind of area. Neighbours here weren’t hostile to each other, whoever you were. But this is now building that kind of ill-feeling... You can understand why there’s so much alarm.”

While Tuebrook’s problem with HMOs is particularly acute, this is a sorry story that is unfolding in urban areas nationwide. The number of properties with HMO licences has more than doubled in a decade, from 38,530 in 2014-15 to 95,217 in 2024-25. The true number of HMOs is likely to be far higher, as these figures don’t account for unlicensed HMOs. Based on government analysis of council data for the year 2024-25, the true number of HMOs (including those that are unlicensed) could be as high as 458,855.

HMOs are a necessity, especially in cities with sky-high property prices, such as London (which has the highest number in the country), and in areas with a large student population. But they are also changing the fabric of many urban neighbourhoods.

In smaller towns, such as Thetford in Norfolk, even a small increase in the number of HMOs can have a marked influence on the stability of a community. Tensions there have become so inflamed that, in August, there were three consecutive nights of protests over a reported increase in the number of asylum seekers the Home Office planned to house in HMOs in the district. 

Buildings believed to be housing asylum seekers were vandalised, and police made more than 20 arrests. Residents have said the occupants are largely “middle-aged men”, and that they don’t feel safe having “undocumented illegal immigrants” in the area. Recently, there has been a steady stream of criminal cases involving convictions of Channel boat migrants for violent crimes.

“I carry a can of Deep Heat spray with me now [for self-defence],” says Wendy, 60, who has lived in Thetford for two decades. An HMO on her road was occupied from spring of this year until it was targeted by protesters in August. “The elderly woman who lived next door to the HMO became a prisoner in her own home because she was so frightened,” she adds. “This has always been a nice area, but lately we’ve been talking about moving. But where do you move when there are HMOs everywhere? They’re all over Thetford.”

Lorraine Dean, 73, a retired NHS worker who lives on another estate in Thetford where she says there are two HMOs, describes how this has “changed the fabric of the street [and] devalued our houses”. “I’ve been here 27-odd years. It was a lovely little community when I first moved, and to some extent it still is, but it does make you feel uneasy,” she says. “Everybody is on edge now.”

In Tuebrook, as in Thetford, many of the complaints from locals centre on the unease of not knowing your neighbours and the sense that families are being pushed out in favour of a much larger number of HMO tenants, often single men. Others are the quotidian stuff of parking, bins and noise complaints.

“If there are seven people [in one HMO], there could be seven bins,” says one Tuebrook resident, a retiree in his 70s. “You get frustrated. There’s not much you can do about it. The thing is, you walk up and down the street, and look in the front gardens at all the rubbish. And you think to yourself, ‘Why am I even bothering to try and keep my place neat and tidy?’”

In the face of growing anger from residents, Liverpool’s Labour council introduced an Article 4 direction – which removes permitted development rights and means landlords must apply for planning permission to convert a family dwelling into an HMO – in many inner-city wards in 2021. Breckland’s council, which covers Thetford and is run by the Conservative Party, took a similar step last month. However, it does not appear to have had the desired effect in Tuebrook, as new HMOs are converted more quickly than enforcement action can be taken.

“We’ve now got to a situation where we’ve got [landlords] coming into the area, just buying the property and doing it anyway,” says Lake. “Because they know it won’t be enforced. We have councillors all over the city now clamouring to have their area designated Article 4, but in our experience, it means nothing, because it hasn’t stopped people from doing it anyway.”

Lake, Radford and Dunne claim that, in some cases, the council is paying private landlords who haven’t got the correct planning permission with taxpayers’ money. On a nearby road, for instance, one department of Liverpool’s council had moved people into an HMO, despite the very same council’s planning department having rejected an application for planning permission for that property.

In another case this summer, it emerged that a nine-bed property in Anfield had been operating as an HMO, under Serco’s management, for several years without planning permission. (Serco has pointed out that responsibility for planning disputes lies with the owner or landlord of the property and the local authority, not Serco.)

In a statement, Hetty Wood, Liverpool’s cabinet member for housing, said: “We share our residents’ concerns about the impact of HMOs in parts of the city, and we are taking action to address those concerns. Liverpool’s council is committed to ensuring HMOs are properly regulated, safe for occupants and managed in a way that respects local communities.”

There is no published nationwide figure for how many HMOs are used as so-called “dispersal accommodation” for asylum seekers, although as a percentage, it is still likely to be relatively small. Some local data does exist and varies widely. In Portsmouth, for instance, the percentage of HMOs let to asylum seekers was 1.6 per cent in 2025, but in Wigan, there were 200 Serco-operated HMOs used for asylum-seeker accommodation out of a total of 900 HMOs overall (around 22 per cent).

However, the share is likely to be growing. The latest Home Office immigration statistics covering the year to June 2026 show 93,293 asylum seekers in receipt of support – either accommodation or cash, or both – down from 106,075 a year earlier. Of these, 16,021 are housed in hotels, a dramatic reduction from 32,059 a year earlier. 

It is clear that dispersal accommodation is picking up the slack. The share of the total number of asylum seekers housed in dispersal accommodation has risen from 62.5 per cent to 70.5 per cent in the past year alone (as the share housed in hotels has dropped from 30.3 per cent to 21.4 per cent).

In Tuebrook, the problem started, ironically, with the area’s ample supply of large, relatively affordable family houses. “One of the things we have in Tuebrook – and it is a strength that has become a weakness – is that there are a great number of big houses,” says Lake. “But people don’t really have seven kids in a family anymore. They’re not really attractive if you’re looking for a family home to start out with.

“So the only people they really attract are speculators, who aren’t usually from the area and have no understanding of the community. They just see pound signs. They see a seven-bedroom house and think, ‘I’ll put 10 blokes in there.’”

Over time, this became a self-fulfilling prophecy. “The balance of privately-owned terraces has dramatically swung to privately-rented properties,” says Radford. “While you could understand the conversion of larger properties into good quality flats, we’re now getting ordinary terraced houses, effectively two-and-a-half-bedroom houses, being targeted for HMOs. The impact on the neighbours and on the streetscape is far more profound. It’s not only changed the current composition of residents, it’s also changed the ability of younger families to buy into the area.”

Steve Iafrati, an assistant professor in social and public policy at the University of Nottingham, studies issues relating to housing vulnerable tenants. Most of the problems that emerge with HMOs, he says, are because of the fact that their tenants are by nature transient and often disengaged from the local community.

“You’ve got to be careful about overgeneralising, but what we might expect to see [in areas with a high number of HMOs] is a higher turnover of people,” he says. “If you think about what makes a community, it’s knowing your neighbours, it’s a street WhatsApp group or Facebook group, it’s coming together at various times. People who are on a rapid turnover don’t integrate into that sort of thing. So those people are sort of largely out of the community.”

Moreover, once one or two properties have been converted, as seen in Tuebrook, it can become a vicious circle. “What you can also see is a knock-on effect where the neighbours might go, ‘Well, I don’t like this, I’m going to move out,’ which obviously creates an opportunity for more people to come and go, ‘Well, the house is up for sale, I could convert it into an HMO.’”

As private sector pressures have increased, a guaranteed long-term lease with a company such as Serco or the council has become even more attractive to landlords. Iafrati says that “supported exempt accommodation” – specialised housing for vulnerable adults, such as prison leavers and people with substance abuse issues, which is exempt from standard benefit caps – is on the rise, as those kinds of HMOs are “absolute moneymakers”. “If I open up an HMO, I can probably charge £80 a week, something like that,” he says. “If I open up supported exempt accommodation, I can probably charge double that.”

In turn, renting an HMO out to firms like Serco as dispersal accommodation for asylum seekers is appealing because it is “guaranteed regular income” with none of the hassle of finding or managing private tenants. “The landlord does not have to worry about voids or problem tenants or the hassle of tenant turnover, and Serco will manage the property,” says Iafrati.

If the landlords are the winners, it is clear who loses out. One Tuebrook resident, a mother-of-four, appears on the street after spotting The Telegraph interviewing other residents. She has rented here for a year. “It has been terrible,” she says. “We are inundated with rats. The number of times I’ve had to have people out. I’ve had to pay privately to get someone out because it was that bad.”

Her home, which is privately rented, has ended up sandwiched between a recently converted HMO and a now-empty house that was vandalised after its former occupant was arrested for child sex offences. “My kids are scared. They won’t go out, and I won’t let them out,” she says. “I want out anyway. My landlord’s selling.” 

The online listing for the house paints a different picture, describing the property as an “appealing opportunity for investors”.
