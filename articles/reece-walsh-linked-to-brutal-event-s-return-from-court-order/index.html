NRL superstar Reece Walsh is set to attend a controversial and brutal sporting event’s return in Sydney this weekend, two months after it was initially shut down in the NSW Supreme Court.

The Run Nation Championship was called off just two days before its September date when Justice Andrew Coleman ruled it was a combat sport under state law, which required a different form of regulation.

“The wording in the code describing the contact as a ‘tackle’ cannot alter the fact RNC (Run Nation Championship) is a collision sport,” Justice Coleman said in his judgment.

A combat sport is one where “the primary objective of each combatant ... is to strike, kick, hit, grapple with, throw or punch one or more other combatants”.

Organisers attempted to argue the so-called “run-it-straight” event — in which competitors have to run directly at each other and try to “win” the collision — was not required to be regulated by the NSW Combat Sports Authority.

Run Nation last month announced the Randwick show would finally take place on November 28.

The Combat Sports Authority, whose decision to knock it back sparked the legal challenge, approved the event two weeks ago.

“Every athlete will undergo full medical and neurological assessments before competing, and doctors and paramedics will be on site throughout the event,” organisers said.

“Independent officials have the authority to stop a contest at any time, and all athletes will complete mandatory medical checks and observe stand-down periods afterwards.”

The card was announced this week with Broncos superstar Walsh’s brother Zac named to compete in a Run Nation event for the second time.

Walsh appeared in a Run Nation video announcing Zac’s match-up and organisers have promoted the Rugby League World Cup’s appearance at the event.

They said more ‘high-profile guests and NRL players’ are expected to attend.

Former NRL player Sandor Earl is a key figure at Run Nation while former Sydney Roosters forward Dylan Napa and ex-Canterbury-Bankstown Bulldogs enforcer Sam Kasiano have previously been connected to the event.

Run-it-straight contests have grown in popularity in recent years with contests being widely viewed by young rugby league fans on social media.

But it has come under heavy scrutiny since Ryan Satterthwaite, a 19-year-old from New Zealand, died of a serious head injury sustained during an impromptu game of run-it-straight with friends in 2019.

Rugby league and union players’ links to various competitions have continued despite clubs and governing bodies, including Queensland Rugby League and New Zealand Rugby, condemning the sport.

Former NRL player Kevin Proctor was knocked out in a match-up last year.

A NSW parliamentary committee has been investigating how to better regulate emerging combat sports.

Bare-knuckle boxing and power slap events, along with collision contests like Run Nation, were some of the sports rising in popularity requiring appropriate monitoring and safeguards, the authority said in its submission to the committee.

Backyard and underground contests are a particular concern for both the regulator and brain trauma experts due to the significant dangers the sports pose for participants.

“This ‘super-stimulus’ form (of) violence cannot be tolerated in our society, nor should we risk parental and social media exposure to children,” cognitive neurologist Rowena Mobbs submitted to the inquiry.

Run Nation chief executive Tremaine Fernandez had promised the September ruling would be merely a speed bump in the sport’s progress.

“RNC is here to stay and ... we’re excited to work with the Combat Sports Authority and the opportunities that may bring,” Fernandez said.
