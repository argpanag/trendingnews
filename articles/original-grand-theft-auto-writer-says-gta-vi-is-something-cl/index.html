Original Grand Theft Auto writer says GTA VI is ‘something close to a miracle’ | CBC Radio

TheTools · 21.11.2026 01:13 · source · permalink

Brian Baglow talks about the evolution of the powerhouse series that started in mid-'90s Scotland

When Brian Baglow was working on the first Grand Theft Auto in the '90s, he had no idea it would spawn an iconic, decades long series, culminating in a blockbuster sixth installation that's shattering sales records and inspiring people to stay home from work.

"We were making it up as we went along," Baglow, writer and publisher for 1997's Grand Theft Auto, told As It Happens host Nil Kӧksal.

"All we really had was some serious technical and budgetary constraints, and a really irreverent and anti-authoritarian approach to things, which is: If it worked, if it made us laugh and it caused people joy, we were quite happy, and it went in."

Three decades later, Grand Theft Auto VI, which had a budget north of $1.5 billion US, has launched to rave reviews, earning $2 billion US  in estimated first-day sales for developer, Rockstar Games.

The game's Florida-inspired setting of Leonida is more realized than ever, as is its storyline and cast, centred on dual protagonists Jason and Lucia, a Bonnie and Clyde-esque couple who fall into a life of crime as they try to stay afloat in what is essentially a parody of modern America. 

"I think they've pulled off something close to a miracle. They have created an entire U.S. state virtually that is going to be more popular with people than the actual Florida," Baglow said.

"I think it's going to have a huge number of visitors from all over the world, maybe for the next decade."

Vice City, Leonida, modelled after Miami, is the fictional setting of Grand Theft Auto. (Rockstar Games/Take-Two Interactive)

How it started

Grand Theft Auto, despite its U.S. setting and themes, was originally the creation of a small group of developers at DMA Design in Dundee, Scotland. 

The idea, Baglow says, was to create a parody of the United States, from an outsider's perspective. 

"We didn't start with a script. We started with a tech demo, a way of moving around a city and the buildings," said Baglow, now the founder and CEO of Scottish Games Network.

"I came in to actually add the story, add the script, after the levels had been built, after the city had been built. And it was a case of going into every crime movie, every heist, every caper, every TV show, cop show, crime drama, and lifting elements that I hoped would resonate with people."

Brian Baglow is the founder and CEO of Scottish Games Network, and was a writer and publicist for the first Grand Theft Auto game in 1997. (Submitted by Brian Baglow)

The goal, Baglow says, was to have fun and push people's buttons.

"It was just what was funny, what would get a laugh, and what would, in some cases, provoke a reaction," he said.

How it's going

For all that's in the last 29 years, Grand Theft Auto remains a franchise steeped in controversy.

Early versions of the game sparked moral panic about violence and crime, while its latest installment has drawn simultaneous allegations of misogyny and wokeness. 

Baglow says Grand Theft Auto  has outlived the widely debunked idea that playing video games leads to real-world violence. But when it comes to the series's depiction of women, he says he agrees that "it has not been good, to put it mildly."

"I'm delighted that we now have not only a female protagonist, but a Hispanic female protagonist. I think it's been long, long overdue," he said. 

"I think it's showing a level of maturation because from the very earliest days it was a very young group of almost entirely male developers in Scotland."

Lucia Caminos is one of Grand Theft Auto VI's dual protagonists. (Rockstar Games/Take-Two Interactive)

Baglow says it's likely no coincidence that GTA VI's early development coincided with the rise of Gamergate, an online movement connected to the widespread harassment of women in the games industry that has often been framed as a blueprint for the alt-right.

"I don't think that could have passed Rockstar by, so I'm relatively sure Lucia would have been part of their plans from quite early on," he said. 

The Guardian's video games editor, Keza MacDonald, wrote that there's "still a lot of sexism in GTA VI’s world, but now we have Lucia to react to it."

"Women aren’t always the butt of a joke," MacDonald wrote. "This is a game in which women can be as violent, visibly tough and deeply messed up as the men."

Grand Theft Auto VI-mania hits Canada

In fact, Lucia's inclusion has altered Grand Theft Auto's gameplay in some fundamental ways. 

For example, unlike in previous installments, players can no longer hire, kill and rob sex workers. The studio says that's because GTA VI, unlike its predecessors, "is a game about a couple in a bind, lovers on the run."

Changing with the times

Baglow says Grand Theft Auto has had to evolve with the times. 

"The game was never about the controversy. And you can't sustain that because you have to get more and more extreme. You have to sort of go to ever greater lengths to and kind of get that reaction," he said.

"At some point you have to relax into the fact that this is a world, an environment, a game where everybody should be able to come in and play and enjoy it without feeling that they are, as Keza said, the butt of the joke."

Still, he says, like all Grand Theft Auto games, GTA VI continues to empower its players with an incredible amount of freedom to move through the world as they see fit.

"And if you show up as a psychopath, there will be consequences. The police will chase you, arrest you, or more likely run you over," he said. 

"Oh, and you might get eaten by an alligator. It's wonderful."
