By Michael Janda

By Michael Janda

There is no doubt that the minutes from the Reserve Bank's September board meeting reveal a very "hawkish" stance — that is they very much skew to the risk of further rate rises.

"Members agreed that financial conditions were restrictive but that some indicators could imply that the extent of restriction was only modest," the board minutes noted.

"Members agreed that financial conditions were restrictive but that some indicators could imply that the extent of restriction was only modest," the board minutes noted.

And the implication from the minutes was that a "modest" restriction on economic activity might not be enough given the economy was "a little stronger" than the RBA had expected and inflation was also higher than forecast.

"Members acknowledged that waiting for further confirmation of the outlook for inflation could be costly, given that inflation had already been above target for an extended period and there were concerning indications from surveys and liaison of a growing risk that medium-term inflation expectations in the business community may be rising," the board members observed.

"Members acknowledged that waiting for further confirmation of the outlook for inflation could be costly, given that inflation had already been above target for an extended period and there were concerning indications from surveys and liaison of a growing risk that medium-term inflation expectations in the business community may be rising," the board members observed.

The RBA staff also cast doubt on the recent rise in the unemployment rate as being representative of the state of the jobs market due to methodological changes in the survey.

They believe the jobs market is still a bit "tight".

The RBA's economists also downplayed the risks from the housing market slowdown feeding through into the economy.

"Scenario analysis suggested that even relatively large falls in housing prices would have only modest direct effects on activity and spending," the minutes noted.

"Scenario analysis suggested that even relatively large falls in housing prices would have only modest direct effects on activity and spending," the minutes noted.

At best, you could read this as the September rate hike getting in ahead of time, obviating the need for further hikes later.

This appears to be how the market interpreted it, with the chance of a November hike virtually unchanged at 33%, with a 50% chance of a hike before the end of this year.

However, you could also interpret these minutes as reflecting an RBA economics department that is blasé about the risks of a sudden, rate-driven economic downturn and may stumble into causing one with a further hike or hikes later this year and early next.

IMF upgrades global growth forecast

K

By Katie Silver

By Katie Silver

The IMF has upgraded its global growth forecast to 3.1% for 2026. That's up ever so slightly from the 3% it forecast in July. It says strong investment in AI is a major reason for its better outlook, but downside risks are mounting, including rising debt loads and the Middle East conflict.

At its meeting in Bangkok, the IMF noted a growing divergence between countries benefiting from AI and those bypassed by it. Just 32 countries now account for 95% of global growth, compared to 54 countries in April.

Downward growth revisions have become more widespread, with the IMF downgrading 47% of economies since the April forecasts were issued, including several energy importers outside AI value chains.

Hardest hit by downgrades were the Gulf countries directly impacted by the US-Israeli war against Iran, with Saudi Arabia's GDP now forecast to contract 3.3% this year compared with the 1.7% growth predicted in July.

IMF cites AI slowdown, spiking yields as looming global market risks

K

By Katie Silver

By Katie Silver

The International Monetary Fund warned on Tuesday against "complacency" in global financial markets, citing risks from an artificial intelligence-driven selloff, rising government bond yields, or cyberattacks as potential threats facing still- resilient markets.

The body cautioned in its latest Global Financial Stability Report that should AI profits or investments wane, it could trigger a correction in related asset prices that could spill into broader markets. Such a selloff, as well as a spike in sovereign bond yields or a major cyberattack, were among "plausible near-term catalysts" that policymakers should monitor, the group wrote.

"These risks by themselves do not necessarily threaten financial stability but could become systemic...if they areamplified by underlying vulnerabilities," the group said in the semiannual report, adding that financial stability risks remain elevated.

The IMF said AI investments have so far helped offset economic drags from war in the Middle East and overall policy uncertainty. But it noted "growing evidence" that AI capabilities may not require as much investment, or the potential economic gains may not be as large as expected.

Should the momentum slow, the IMF noted growing leverage in the AI sector could exacerbate any shock by triggering forced selling and higher volatility. For example, hyperscalers now represent 10% of global investment-grade bond issuance, up from less than 1% in 2024.

"The consequences of an AI correction could be global," the IMF warned, as it called on policymakers to curb leverage use, including derivatives and margin loans, where it is "excessive."

Firm RBA September rate hike, but what's next?

By David Taylor

There's no question the Reserve Bank was committed to hiking interest rates at its September meeting.

It flagged an increase in the cash rate in the weeks leading up to the meeting.

Governor Michele Bullock, and deputy governor Andrew Hauser, both spoke publicly about the risks of higher inflation.

One week before the interest rate hike Ms Bullock suggested the unemployment rate may need to rise in order to get inflation back down within the target range of between 2 and 3 per cent.

The release of the minutes to that meeting, published today, made reference to that idea saying, "members acknowledged that the trade-off between inflation and full employment had deteriorated because of global cost pressures."

"They confirmed that their strategy was still to return inflation to target in a timely manner, while preserving as many of the gains in employment as possible.

"However, executing this strategy in an environment of more persistent adverse supply shocks would require accepting slower growth in demand and employment than would otherwise be the case," the minutes said.
