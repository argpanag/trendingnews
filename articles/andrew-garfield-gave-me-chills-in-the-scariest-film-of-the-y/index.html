Andrew Garfield gave me chills in the scariest film of the year

TheTools · 12.10.2026 00:18 · source · permalink

A sci-fi horror about the rise of Sam Altman and Open AI, Artificial will make you want to leap into the screen and pull out all the plugs

5/5

Luca Guadagnino’s Artificial – a vigorous thriller about OpenAI, the Californian company whose signature product ChatGPT turbocharged the artificial intelligence boom – is the scariest thing you will see in the cinema this year.

Its plot is pure sci-fi horror. A gaggle of young, naive, headstrong tech bros, led by a boyish yet formidable Andrew Garfield as chief executive Sam Altman, develop and refine a technology that, they all seem to admit to varying extents, may prove cataclysmic for humankind.

Nonetheless, they press on – for influence, or profit, in the utopian belief that things will work out for the best; or perhaps just for the simple buzz of being first to the prize. It is the stuff of a prologue to a Terminator or Matrix film, and makes you want to leap into the screen and pull out every plug you can find.

What compounds the terror is that the places in which this insane experiment is being conducted are utterly familiar, even banal. No gleaming labs here, nor the sleek, low-lit, humming interiors of David Fincher’s The Social Network. (Though in substance, if not style, that landmark 2010 founding-of-Facebook film is an obvious antecedent.)

The action here unfolds instead within drab academic offices, airy hipster cafés, bustling rented apartments and, later, increasingly chic corporate suites. It is unavoidably our world, which means what we are watching is unavoidably our problem.

Simon Rich’s drum-tight screenplay instantly fosters an atmosphere of helpless acceleration. It is 2007, then it is suddenly 2008; we are in a classroom in Toronto, then at a tech conference, then another, then San Francisco.

Meanwhile, Guadagnino’s direction is stylistically measured yet thrillingly psychologically vigilant. During key conversations, characters occasionally gaze directly into the lens, as if sitting one of Blade Runner’s Voight-Kampff tests that distinguish humans from androids.

Garfield’s Altman gets a couple of these that make your spine ice up, though he begins as a relatively minor, scatty and puppyish figure. His stature in the narrative grows like a gathering storm, and his final few scenes are among the best and most chilling the actor has played.

At first, he is eclipsed by Ike Barinholtz’s Elon Musk – clenched and squinting, with a pleasing hint of Derek Zoolander. Part rival, part eccentric patron, he potters around on the sidelines, lobbing money OpenAI’s way while grumbling impatiently about timescales and awkwardly posing for selfies.

The steadiest presence here is Artificial’s moral compass: the Soviet-born scientist Ilya Sutskever, played by Anora’s Yura Borisov with subtlety, control and a heartbreaking gleam of human goodness. Ilya alone seems fully to apprehend the possible dangers ahead – yet it is his computational brainwave, which strikes under the tutelage of Mark Rylance’s Professor Geoff Hinton, that fires the starting pistol on the AI arms race.

In a naturalistic yet unremarked-upon touch, more and more of Ilya’s hair falls out as the film goes on. Frankly, so may yours.

Screening at the London Film Festival. In UK cinemas from Jan 22
