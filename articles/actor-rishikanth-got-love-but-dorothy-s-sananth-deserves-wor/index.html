Actor Rishikanth got love, but Dorothy’s Sananth deserves world for transwoman act

TheTools · 27.10.2026 10:08 · source · permalink

Actor Sananth's performance as Subbu in Dorothy brings a trans woman's concealed identity and tenderness to life. Its precision and restraint place him alongside Rishikanth in the film's awards conversation.

It has been over a month since director Karthik Subbaraj’s Dorothy released in theatres, and the film continues to receive praise for its treatment of queer themes in commercial cinema. Amid all the conversations around the film – good and bad – actor Rishikanth, who plays Karthi, has deservedly received plenty of attention. But Sananth, who plays Subbu, a character who embraces her identity as a transwoman, deserves just as much love, recognition and a place in the awards conversation.

Rishikanth had an edge when Dorothy arrived in theatres. The film released shortly after director Raja Karuppasamy’s Modha Rathri hit theatres, turning Rishikanth into a sensation, particularly among women. Sananth, meanwhile, was already known for several memorable roles in Tamil cinema, including Petta, Mahaan and many more.

Yet it was Rishikanth who dominated the chatter around Dorothy. Now that the film is out on OTT, it is time to look closer at Sananth’s performance - one that deserves to be discussed not just for the character he plays, but for the intricacies of how he brings her to life.

Sananth’s performance is in the details

Dorothy begins with a young Subbu (Sananth), a child with an unabashed attitude who becomes the saviour his school friend Karthi (Rishikanth) needs when senior students bully him. Having grown up in a household full of men, Subbu repeatedly tells his friend that "men do not cry." It is Karthi who is frightened by fights and the sight of dead bodies floating in the village well. Subbu, who has lost his mother and sister in quick succession, has seen the devastating consequences of the men in his own family. He becomes a shield for Karthi, even as he struggles to find a way to protect himself.

As the two grow older, Subbu begins to understand and embrace her femininity, initially only within the privacy of her room. In a household where caste pride, honour and rigid ideas of masculinity take precedence, there is little room for love and acceptance. Subbu cannot freely be herself without risking the consequences of defying the world around her.

Enter Dorothy (Keerthy Suresh), Subbu’s sister-in-law, who gives her the space to be herself. Dorothy sees her for who she truly is, validates her identity and promises to support her through her transition. When Dorothy meets a tragic end, Subbu is left devastated. Her death becomes the catalyst for Subbu to finally confront years of repression. Unable to hold back any longer, she steps out of her room in a saree and a large bindi, coming out to her family for the first time.

This is where Sananth’s performance becomes particularly striking. Playing a transwoman is a delicate balancing act. There is always the risk of a portrayal slipping into caricature, relying on exaggerated gestures or mannerisms rather than building a believable person. Sananth avoids that trap. His performance finds a careful balance between the masculinity Subbu has learnt to perform for survival and the femininity she has long been forced to suppress.

What makes the portrayal remarkable is how little Sananth needs to spell out. From the very first scene, there is a sense of a person who has spent years concealing parts of herself. Then come those sudden, almost instantaneous shifts in body language when Subbu no longer wants to hide. Sananth changes the character’s energy in a fraction of a second. The physicality changes, the gestures soften or sharpen, and the audience sees the difference between the self Subbu presents to the world and the person she knows herself to be.

These transitions are not merely dramatic flourishes. They tell us how instinctively Subbu has learnt to adapt to her surroundings, and how exhausting it must be to keep doing so. The performance is especially effective because the changes never feel like a switch put on for the audience. They feel like glimpses of a person who has finally found brief moments in which she can stop pretending.

Even the smallest gestures carry meaning. The way Sananth locks and twirls his fingers, the shifts in his posture and the ease or restraint in his movements reveal what Subbu cannot always say aloud. These details are easy to miss if one watches only for the big emotional moments. But they are precisely what make the performance worth revisiting. Sananth gives Subbu an inner life that exists beyond the film’s central conflict.

There are moments when the character’s femininity emerges almost instinctively, only for Subbu to pull back into the masculine persona expected of her. Sananth makes those shifts feel lived-in rather than rehearsed. He understands that a person who has spent years suppressing her identity will not necessarily transform into one grand, theatrical moment. Sometimes, the truth escapes through a gesture, a fleeting expression or a change in the way she carries herself.

A woman who gives the love she was denied

Perhaps the most affecting aspect of Subbu’s journey is what she does with the love she has been denied. Growing up in a home that offers little room for vulnerability or acceptance, she knows what it means to have parts of herself rejected. Yet, as she comes closer to embracing who she is, she also becomes a person capable of giving warmth and affection. There is something deeply moving about a character who has been denied the freedom to be herself, becoming a source of love for others.

Sananth brings that tenderness to the performance without turning Subbu into a symbol or reducing her to the suffering she has endured. She is not defined solely by the prejudice she faces. She is also a friend, a person with instincts and desires, and someone who wants the freedom to live and love without having to conceal herself. The affection she offers carries added weight because the film makes us understand how little of it she has received.

That is why Sananth deserves more than a passing mention in the praise surrounding Dorothy. Rishikanth’s performance has rightly struck a chord, but Sananth’s work is equally deserving of attention for its precision, restraint and emotional depth. It is a performance built from details rather than declarations, and one that rewards a closer look.

Awards often celebrate the performances that announce themselves loudly. Sananth’s work deserves to be in that conversation because of the opposite: the control with which he handles the smallest changes in expression and movement, and the way those choices reveal an entire inner world. He gives Subbu (who gets the name Dorothy after the ritual) the complexity of a person, not merely the burden of a story about identity.

More than a month after Dorothy’s theatrical release, the film’s arrival on OTT offers viewers a chance to revisit those moments and notice what they might have missed the first time. Sananth deserves the love, the recognition and the performance-intensive roles that allow him to explore this kind of range again. If Dorothy has given audiences reasons to celebrate Rishikanth, it has also given them a compelling reason to celebrate Sananth. He deserves his flowers, and then some.
