Answering 3 remaining questions about NFL shift to open distribution

TheTools · 12.11.2026 09:34 · source · permalink

Last month, my colleague Ben Fischer offered the first glimpse at the NFL’s ticketing future.

The league reached a new deal with Ticketmaster, keeping the giant in the top slot for the NFL’s secondary ticketing sponsorship starting next year (current deals run out in March 2027, as we’ve previously reported). But that comes with a significant task: Ticketmaster will also be the engine behind a new open-distribution setup for the NFL Ticket HQ. SBJ has since learned that Ticketmaster’s deal with the league is a seven-year agreement, with a fourth-year opt-out based on performance of the new setup as well as the tech.

That licensed ticketing ecosystem will have access to not only club single-game tickets, like before, but also season-ticket resale inventory leaguewide. The NFL’s season-ticket base is consistently massive: Across all 32 franchises, that segment represents nearly 90% of team total inventory on average, according to two league sources.

It’s a big change for the league as it positions itself toward a future that sports has talked about for some time. While that work starts in earnest this offseason, there are many questions right now. So let’s tackle a few, shall we?

What does this setup mean for teams?

Team sources shared that their immediate reaction to this shift was that it’s a win for franchises. For one, an open distribution setup implies an increased reach for teams selling their single-game inventory, or even a fan trying to resell a ticket.

But furthermore, this system will allow for greater data flow, meaning that teams will soon have clearer insights on how many tickets are actually being resold, which fans are reselling and where they’re doing it. Currently, a ticket sold outside of the NFL Ticket HQ, such as on StubHub, appears as a forwarded ticket in a team’s system and not a resale.

“There’s a lot of big ideas and concepts thrown out over the last year and a half about what this might look like,” said one ticketing executive. “And I think most teams are, at least from what I’ve heard, pretty satisfied with where we landed.” At one point, some of those big ideas from the NFL included owning its own ticketing platform, or at least having much more control of the fan data attached to digital ticketing.

The Ticketmaster deal brings to an end a contentious bid process for this top slot between the company and SeatGeek, which is still working out the details of its gold-tier membership. “They did a great job of balancing so many variables,” another team source said of the league.

Which other companies could join the HQ?

An open distribution setup is only as good as its reach. As of right now, Ticketmaster is locked in, and SeatGeek is very much in the fold. The other companies that join that cluster — such as fellow HQ members Sports Illustrated Tickets and Tixr, the latter of which joined the network in May — remain to be seen.

A source indicated to SBJ that the league’s conversations have spanned across “a lot of different groups.” StubHub, for example, with its significant user base and market share, used to be part of the formerly named NFL Ticket Network. It isn’t far-fetched to see a player like that coming into the fold again.

Sports Illustrated Tickets CEO David Lane said that because he hasn’t seen the membership and sponsorship required, the company has yet to decide on whether it will remain part of the HQ. It’s worth noting that Sports Illustrated Tickets just rolled out a new resale platform called SeatHQ, which it has vetted with NFL league and team reps. “This strategy of ours, again, whether we are part of the resale program or not, accomplishes what we need to do as a company,” Lane said.

Tixr maintains its stance that its relationship with the league will remain a longer-term partnership, as CEO Robert Davari told me back in May. Davari added that this new setup will provide a new playing field for fledgling ticketing players. “We’re excited about it because we’re part of crafting it,” Davari said.

Which other team decisions can this affect?

If the reach of HQ is large enough, it may eliminate the need for NFL teams to pursue other ticketing deals. One team operator, for example, mentioned that their franchise has a separate deal with a secondary marketplace that may no longer be necessary. “What other partners are coming in the mix?” a source asked. “And based on that, that might change some business decisions on our side.”

Also, a large network may require more human power to tackle game-day customer service related to ticketing issues, at least at the start. More marketplaces in play means more sites and more resources to troubleshoot some of the initial growing pains.

Lastly, the reselling of season tickets is a tight needle to thread for teams. It’s positioned as a perk for fans when they can’t make a game, but it also opens up opportunities for profit flippers, both of whom should find more sales success with a theoretically larger reach.

Generally, though, as long as the buying experience remains familiar to the fans, so too will the NFL’s stronghold of season-ticket holders who continue showing up. “This organically takes care of itself — it’s the benefits of free markets and information,” said an NFL C-suiter. “So where people see opportunity in markets, people will go.”
