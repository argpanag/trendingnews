Newsroom journalists took Starship case to CCRC – now it’s heading to Court of Appeal

TheTools · 21.10.2026 16:00 · source · permalink

A mother imprisoned after Starship Hospital child protection unit doctors testified that her baby had been deliberately injured will have her conviction reconsidered by the Court of Appeal, following a years-long investigation led by Newsroom’s Melanie Reid and her team.

Te Kāhui, The Criminal Cases Review Commission (CCRC), has referred the conviction and two-and-a-half-year prison sentence of the mother to the country’s second-highest court.

Acting Chief Commissioner Paula Rose says the new evidence raises important questions about medical evidence central to the original case.

“There is now significant expert evidence that was not available to the jury or the Court of Appeal and which challenges the medical evidence relied on at trial,” says Rose.

“Having considered that evidence, Te Kāhui concluded it is in the interests of justice for the convictions to be considered again by the Court of Appeal.”

(The case first went to the Court of Appeal in 2021 and was dismissed.)

Rose says the referral does not determine guilt or innocence.

“A referral does not overturn a conviction, and it does not determine whether a miscarriage of justice occurred. Those matters are now for the Court of Appeal.”

In what is believed to be a first in New Zealand, the submission to the CCRC was made on behalf of the mother by journalists, alongside former race relations and human rights commissioner, Joris de Bres.

“It is not usual for us as journalists to be making submissions to the CCRC, but in this case I felt like we had no choice,” says Reid.

“This mother had her baby uplifted at two months old and her child is now eight. The removal of her child and subsequent conviction and two-and-a-half-year jail term is based on medical evidence that, following extensive international reviews, is seriously questionable.

“There is growing concern internationally among medical and legal experts about the misdiagnosis or the over-diagnosis of non-accidental injury, and New Zealand is no exception.”

The CCRC was established in 2020 to assess potential miscarriages of justice and whether they meet the legal threshold to be referred back to the appeal courts.

Since it was set up, the CCRC has received 706 submissions. Only seven have ever been referred to the appeal court. This case is the eighth.

The mother’s lawyer, Christchurch barrister Kerry Cook, says this is an important decision.

“Prior to the existence of the CCRC, the mother wouldn’t have been able to have her case reopened. The justice system’s moved on from her. So it is a big deal. It’s a very big deal.”

He says the material Melanie Reid and team gathered “undermines the evidence which was used to secure the conviction. So it’s a big deal for society. It’s a big deal for the mother.

“It does make me wonder, as a lawyer involved in appeals, whether or not there have been other people who have suffered a miscarriage of justice because of expert evidence that has led a jury to conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that that person did cause injury to their child on purpose. And it’s well known that incorrect, erroneous expert evidence, well meaning though it may be, is one of the major causes of miscarriages of justice.” 

The referral marks a major turning point in a case Reid and her team have investigated for almost three years, which you can listen to in depth across two seasons of Fractured on Newsroom’s investigative podcast channel, Delve.

(The follow-up podcast, Diagnosis of a Crime, is also available to listen to on Delve. It tells the stories of three more families facing the fallout of strikingly similar diagnoses.)

A three-year investigation

In November 2023, Joris de Bres wrote an email to Reid with the subject line: “Separated by the State”.

De Bres described how he’d been helping an Auckland-based couple who had arrived in New Zealand from India to make a life for themselves in what they called “the dream country”.

What followed could only be described as a nightmare: a mother jailed, their baby put into foster care and both parents facing deportation without their daughter.

“We set out to discover whether the baby at the centre of our Fractured investigation was put into a foster home and her mother sent to prison based on a flawed diagnosis. We ended up ploughing head first into a story of international proportions,” says Reid.

The father at the centre of the case, Ravi (not his real name), told Reid in their first interview in 2023: “I came here for a better future – a better future for my family, to grow myself here, find a better job and make a happy life here. Now we are living as if we are dead.”

Five years ago, the mother at the centre of the case, who we call Zita, was convicted of intentionally hurting her infant daughter. At her sentencing, Judge Ema Aitken described the medical evidence as “overwhelming”.

During Zita’s three-week trial, four doctors from Starship Hospital told the jury the injuries her baby had suffered were non-accidental and ‘abusive head trauma’, a description that in medical circles has replaced ‘shaken baby syndrome’.

No expert medical witnesses appeared for the mother’s defence – the couple were immigrants with limited English, relying on legal aid and facing a well-resourced Crown case supported by medical experts. They say they realise now they “never stood a chance”.

She was found guilty and sent to prison for two years and seven months. Zita has always maintained she did not, and would not ever, hurt her daughter, who we refer to as Baby K.

“She’s the first child in our family – no one had before, so she’s very precious to us in our culture. We believe that she’s the goddess,” Zita told Reid.

The paediatrician from Starship Hospital’s child abuse unit, which diagnosed the then-eight-week-old Baby K’s injuries as non-accidental, did so less than 24 hours after she had been admitted to hospital. 

In the intervening years, the little girl was put into four different foster homes, and Oranga Tamariki would not allow her father – who has no convictions – to regain custody of his child, despite his repeated requests.

“The argument seems to have been that because he didn’t accept that his wife had done it, that he was somehow in collusion with her and therefore he was unfit to see his daughter.

It’s a very twisted sort of reasoning, which then extended to the whole family because there was a whole period when he couldn’t see his daughter at all, but he was told after his wife went to prison by a social worker that he would never get custody of his daughter back unless he separated or divorced from his wife,” de Bres explained.

On the day Zita was released from prison after serving almost her entire sentence, she faced imminent deportation back to India and Oranga Tamariki secured a court order to keep Baby K in New Zealand under the care of the state.

Because Ravi’s visa was tied to his wife’s, he too faced deportation, meaning their daughter would be left in NZ without parents.

Finally in April 2025 after 18 months of investigating, 20 podcast episodes, dozens of interviews, reports from four international medical experts and the submission to the Criminal Cases Review Commission, the orders relating to Baby K were discharged and she is now back with Zita and Ravi.

To date, they have spent more than nine years fighting the authorities to get their daughter back, stay in the country and defend Zita through the legal system.

The international experts

In the course of their extensive investigations, Reid and her team secured the high-resolution 3D CT scans, x-rays, birth records and fetal heart monitoring tracings of the child at the centre of this case.

They then engaged experts in genetics, neurosurgery, obstetrics and neuropathology from Canada, Norway, the Netherlands and the United States to analyse the records.

“We of course did not know when we got the medical files reviewed if they would come back for or against the mother at the centre of this case,” says Reid.

The experts each wrote a formal report and provided evidence that the likely causes of the baby’s injuries were related to her traumatic labour and delivery, and her severely low levels of vitamin D, which is integral to bone health.

That fresh evidence was submitted as part of the application to the CCRC, and helped cast serious doubt over the mother’s conviction.

When told of the news that her case was being referred to the Court of Appeal by the CCRC, Zita told Reid: “I feel so happy. Finally, I’m getting somewhere because it’s been long time and I just don’t believe it’s happening. It’s very great news.”

The process following the referral can now go one of three ways: the Court of Appeal can recognise there’s been a miscarriage of justice and quash Zita’s conviction, giving her a clean record; it can order a retrial, meaning the Crown has to make a determination about whether or not they will retry her and she would have to go through another trial; or it can dismiss the appeal which would uphold her conviction.

Associate Minister of Immigration Cameron Brewer has also made the decision to grant Zita and Ravi work permits for up to 18 months to allow Zita to remain in New Zealand with her family while her CCRC application is investigated and any related Court of Appeal proceedings are undertaken.

Multiple cases

Following the publishing of the Fractured podcast, Reid and her team were approached by more than a dozen other families who have also been affected by a diagnosis of non-accidental injury in their infants, including a farming family from Central Otago, a father serving a six-year prison sentence in Auckland and a professional athlete from the South Island.

There are striking similarities across these cases:

  • They all involve infants (children one year old and younger), most of them very young babies.
  • In almost every case, there is a history of traumatic birth.
  • The parents are all highly engaged and proactive with medical professionals, taking their infants to numerous visits with health professionals before hospital admission.
  • Before taking their infants to hospital, there were no outward signs of anything wrong, such as bruising.
  • In every case, the diagnosis of non-accidental injury came after a paediatric review of x-rays and CT scans.
  • The parents are all confused and bewildered, saying they have no idea what’s wrong with their infants.
  • Severe vitamin D deficiency is a recurring feature in many of the cases.

In each case, Reid and her team again obtained the medical files, scans and x-rays used to accuse and convict parents of causing the injuries, then engaged internationally recognised specialists in radiology, orthopaedics, pathology, genetics, obstetrics from around the world.

These experts were asked a simple question: do the medical findings support the conclusions reached by the New Zealand doctors?

In all of the cases, the answer was no.

(Investigating these cases also raised another issue: even when parents found specialists in New Zealand who supported them and believed there were potential medical causes for the injuries, they weren’t prepared to publicly disagree with Starship doctors.)

The farming couple from Central Otago lost custody of their newborn twins for two years after Starship’s lead paediatrician at the time, Dr Patrick Kelly, told them there were fractures to one of their eight-week-old babies, which became the turning point for the abuse diagnosis. However, overseas experts found no evidence of those fractures. 

In the case of the young father currently serving a six-year prison sentence, this was based on a hip fracture and 19 back rib fractures to his one-year-old son. Three independent international specialists have found the hip fracture is consistent with an accident in the shower the father had described, and that there is no evidence of 19 rib fractures. 

And in the case of the pro athlete, he was charged with causing multiple rib fractures to his four-week-old son, described by Starship as likely from “squeezing”. However the jury in his three-week trial heard from international experts arranged by Reid and team that the baby’s fractures were likely due to his severely low vitamin D levels and precipitous (very rapid) birth. He was found not guilty.

And involved in almost all of these cases is a small group of specialists from Starship’s child protection unit, Te Puaruruhau.

Set up in 2002 to bring together police, Oranga Tamariki and medical staff, it was led by paediatrician Dr Patrick Kelly for 25 years.

Since at least 2010, serious concerns have been raised that Te Puaruruhau had reached conclusions without considering all the evidence, and in many cases families say they’ve been treated like criminals before their cases are heard in court.

The cases investigated by Delve raise the question: If internationally respected medical experts can look at the same scans and medical files and reach fundamentally different conclusions to the New Zealand doctors, what is going on?

How can New Zealand radiologists be diagnosing fractures that overseas radiologists say don’t exist? Why are potential birth injuries and metabolic bone issues being diagnosed as non-accidental injuries within hours of distressed parents turning up to hospital with their infants? And why are independent obstetrical reviews of these cases not being immediately requested by the paediatricians?

According to a growing number of international scientists, lawyers and medical specialists, the answer lies in the theories underpinning these diagnoses.

Australian scientist Professor Chris Brook describes it as, “one of the biggest public health and legal disasters of our era”, and attributes these diagnoses to a belief that certain injuries are ‘highly indicative’ of abuse.

In practice this means when these paediatric doctors see particular injuries, there is little room for alternative explanations.

Reid and her team have repeatedly gone to Health NZ over the past three years to ask them to identify the fractures international experts have said don’t exist.

They consistently provide the same response, that “it would not be appropriate for Health New Zealand to comment on a specific case in which our clinicians have provided evidence.”

And, “We have the utmost confidence in the expertise, impartiality and integrity of our paediatric radiologists and child protection teams who provide expert evidence.”

You can read more about the ongoing attempts to get answers from Health NZ here.

‘Last line of defence’

Reid said the only way to determine whether there was a broader problem was to engage independent international specialists to review the same scans, medical records and evidence being relied upon in court.

“Too often, lawyers don’t understand how critical that independent evidence is. If the medical evidence goes unchallenged, the outcome is largely predetermined. That’s why access to independent experts isn’t a luxury in these cases – it’s fundamental to a fair process.”

Zita’s lawyer, Kerry Cook, credits the investigative journalism behind her case for achieving outcomes such as hers.

“Investigative journalists are often the last line of defence against the justice system that’s already made up its mind and moved on. And so when a case is closed and the appeals are exhausted and the public’s forgotten about it, it’s usually journalists or a family member that keep asking the questions that no one else will. You started involving experts, you spent the time, and it was months and months re-examining evidence, tracking down new evidence, involving experts and exposing the flaws that sent an innocent person to jail.

“In my experience, and I think the experience of all those around the world, time and time again, it has been a dogged reporter, not a lawyer and not a politician, but a dogged reporter who’s provided the crucial material that’s allowed the Criminal Cases Review Commission to reexamine the case like the safety valve it is.”

When asked if anybody could be accountable if Zita’s conviction is quashed and her name cleared, Cook says there is the ability in certain circumstances to apply for compensation for the time that they’ve spent in jail, but it’s difficult to say at the moment how that might look for Zita.

“There are lots of factors that go into it and the Court of Appeal hasn’t determined whether or not they’re going to allow the appeal yet.”
