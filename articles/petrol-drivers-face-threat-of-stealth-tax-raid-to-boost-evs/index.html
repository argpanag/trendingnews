Petrol drivers face threat of stealth tax raid to boost EVs

TheTools · 28.10.2026 23:00 · source · permalink

Petrol and diesel car drivers face being hit with a “hidden levy at the pumps” under proposals to cut charging costs for electric vehicles (EVs).

An official review published alongside the Budget urged ministers to cut costs for EV drivers by introducing new subsidies ultimately paid for by petrol and diesel motorists.

Under the proposals, EV charging companies would be given financial support if they use renewable electricity.

It would be paid for by petrol stations with the cost then passed on to millions of motorists via higher forecourt prices.

The proposal came as John Healey, the Chancellor, announced fuel duty would be frozen at its current rate until July as he sought to ease the impact of the Middle East crisis on households.

But campaigners claimed the new EV charging scheme amounted to a stealth fuel duty rise that would transfer cash from one group of motorists to another.

It would put further upward pressure on petrol and diesel costs at a time when the US-Iran conflict has already driven them to their highest level in four years.

The review of EV charging costs, led by Philip New, a former BP executive, suggested that renewable energy subsidies would be funded through a certificate scheme. Charging companies given a certain number of certificates based on how much green power their sites consumed.

They would then be able to sell these certificates to petrol and diesel suppliers, which would be legally required to buy them.

However, the review also admitted that “obligating fuel suppliers to purchase more certificates would create an additional cost for petrol and diesel vehicle drivers as costs are passed through”.

It estimated that the scheme would add 0.4p per litre to the cost of fuel if implemented next year.

However, the review acknowledged the cost “will grow” in parallel with EV uptake and the rollout of renewable energy and suggested ministers should cap resulting fuel price increases at 1p per litre to limit the impact.

But Howard Cox, of Fair Fuel UK, said: “This is economic and social madness.

“Petrol and diesel drivers should not be forced to subsidise cheaper charging for EV owners through yet another hidden levy at the pumps.

“Motorists already pay a huge tax burden just to get to work, run a business, or take their children to school.

“If the Government wants to make public EV charging cheaper, it should tackle costs within the charging market, not raid the pockets of millions of the Treasury’s cash-cow drivers who still rely on petrol and diesel.

“Today it may be less than 0.4p a litre, tomorrow the review itself envisages costs rising and even discusses a 1p cap. Once this principle is established, where does it end?”

Electric vehicles are a crucial pillar of the Government’s plan to reach net zero carbon emissions by 2050. But in a foreword to his review, Mr New warned it was essential to cut the cost of charging for drivers to improve uptake.

“Unless it is addressed, it will slow the move to EVs and weaken the economic and environmental gains that the transition can deliver,” he said.

Costs are particularly problematic for those who cannot charge at home. One third of drivers lack a driveway, meaning it can be more difficult to install a dedicated charger at home.

Telegraph View

While drivers who charged at home paid 8-12p per mile, the equivalent cost was 12-16p for those who depended on slower public chargers and 24p for users of rapid chargers.

This compared with a typical cost of 22p per mile paid by petrol car drivers, based on September 2026 prices.

Mr New’s proposal has not yet been endorsed by the Government. The Department for Transport was approached for comment.
