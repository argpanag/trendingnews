For the fans, football is worth the wait and expense

TheTools · 14.11.2026 16:29 · source · permalink

"Bangladesh! Bangladesh! Bangladesh!"

The roar began as soon as Hamza Choudhury and his teammates walked into the National Stadium on Saturday evening. Drums beat, flags waved and the upper galleries shook with chants of support. The upper stands were already packed, while the lower tiers still had plenty of empty seats. But as kick-off approached to the semifinal between Bangladesh and Pakistan, more and more supporters found their way in, through both online bookings and sales at the gates.

For many, getting there had been an effort in itself. Tickets were expensive, the wait was long and the facilities left plenty to be desired. Yet, none of that was enough to keep them away. They came after closing their shops, travelling from outside Dhaka or simply making room in their day for a few hours of football.

Ahmed Jamil Rasel, an entrepreneur who shut his shop on a working day to watch Bangladesh, believes the price of tickets is keeping many supporters away from the lower galleries.

"The main reason the lower gallery doesn't fill instantly is the price," he said. "For a student, Tk 1,000 is a week or two of pocket money. Then you come here, and the seats are filthy. Rainwater and garbage are everywhere. They could have easily cleaned these this morning."

His frustration was understandable. But he had still made the trip, as had thousands of others. For supporters, the chance to watch the national team in a major match still carries a pull that ticket prices and uncomfortable surroundings cannot entirely diminish.

Mohammad Roisuddin travelled all the way from Tangail in a rented microbus with 11 friends and his young son, Morsalin Mahi. The journey and the long wait became part of their matchday experience, with stadium khichuri, friends and the anticipation of seeing the men in red and green providing plenty to talk about.

"The ticket price is high, but being here with everyone makes it worth it," Roisuddin said.

Beside him, first-grader Mahi proudly showed off his new football boots. His favourite player is Lionel Messi, he said, but Hamza Choudhury is not far behind. For a young fan, getting to see the national team in person was an occasion in itself.

The excitement around Hamza has also reached supporters who have followed Bangladesh football for decades. Kamruddin, who passionately supported Abahani during the 1980s, believes the current buzz has brought back memories of a different time.

"For 15 years, people bypassed football for cricket," he said. "But Hamza brings a smartness, a craze we haven't seen since Monem Munna. When he plays, people finally believe there will be a goal."

There was a large tifo of Monem Munna proudly visible in the galleries.   

That connection across generations was visible in the stands. Young children along with their families arrived in their first football shirts, older supporters returned with memories of past heroes, and groups of friends made the journey together. They had different reasons for coming, but shared the same desire to see Bangladesh play.

The ticket prices remain steep, the facilities need attention and the long wait between matches tests supporters' patience. Those are concerns the organisers cannot afford to ignore.
