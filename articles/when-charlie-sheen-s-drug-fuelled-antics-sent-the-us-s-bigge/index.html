When Charlie Sheen’s drug-fuelled antics sent the US’s biggest sitcom into meltdown

The Plaza Hotel is a New York landmark and a place unaccustomed to surprise visits from the police. But in October 2010, this gilded institution was dragged into tabloid infamy when NYPD officers responded to a 911 call from porn star Capri Anderson.

She had barricaded herself in a bathroom on the 18th floor, saying she felt threatened by sitcom megastar Charlie Sheen, who was in a state of advanced intoxication in the adjoining suite. The cops offered Sheen a choice: leave New York or face arrest, according to a new account of the incident by Sheen's former mentor, Chuck Lorre. The actor, says Lorre, gathered his stuff and was gone, although Sheen has maintained his departure was merely a routine return home.

Sheen had been out of control for years – it was his curse but also his brand. And yet the Plaza was seen as a low point, even for him. That was certainly the view of Lorre, who had made the actor fabulously wealthy by casting him as the bad-boy lead in the blockbuster US sitcom Two and a Half Men.

Chuck Lorre, Sheen's former mentor, cast him as lead character Charlie Harper in Two and a Half Men - Getty/Michael Tullberg

Working with Sheen was, Lorre would come to conclude, a pact with whatever passed for the devil in Hollywood. Two and a Half Men traded heavily on Sheen's real-life reputation as a hell-raiser supreme – nobody else could have played perpetual bad-boy Charlie Harper. However, the popularity of the show and the gravity-defying wealth it brought Sheen were fuelling his demons, and his behaviour was becoming increasingly unpredictable, according to Lorre in his upcoming autobiography, Sitcom: A Memoir.

Lorre is a funny, self-deprecating writer, but his recollections of Two and a Half Men's unravelling and his public falling out with Sheen make for chilling reading. Over the space of three months at the start of 2011, Sheen would go from one of the world's best-paid and genuinely beloved actors to a pariah, whose ever-more deranged diatribes against Lorre would leave both friends and strangers fearing for Sheen's mental well-being. It's a story far more unhinged than any Hollywood script – encompassing stolen cars, threats from the mob and a stage-struck Hugh Grant.

Sheen was, of course, notorious long before Two and a Half Men, which started when he was in his 40s. He slept with his first prostitute as a teenager, had his inaugural stint in rehab in 1990 and discovered crack cocaine in his late 20s in 1992 – by which time his infamy as a party animal had eclipsed his status as the star of Platoon and Wall Street.

Sheen with co-star Jon Cryer (left, as Alan) and guest star Gail O'Grady - CBS Photo Archive via Getty/Robert Voets

So Lorre knew what he was getting when he cast Sheen, but the actor "more or less behaved himself" until season eight, which began filming in mid-2010 – when things began to change.

Sheen began calling in late or not showing up at all (having negotiated a four-day work week), offering excuses such as repeated bouts of food poisoning and dental appointments. The Plaza incident happened in October, and by February 2011, everyone but Sheen knew the jig was up.

"The last scene we shot was with Charlie and Melanie Lynskey. He couldn't remember any of his lines and had to use the furniture to keep himself upright. Melanie was terrified. It was clear to everyone that this man, whom we'd all grown to love, was in trouble. I had no choice but to shut down the show," Lorre recalls.

He rang Sheen with his offer. They would shoot two more episodes of Two and a Half Men and then take the summer off so the actor could get his head straight. But he claims Sheen pushed back, demanding they film four more episodes. Lorre was puzzled – two, four… what did it matter? All that was important was that Sheen focus on recovery. Why was the actor so desperate to work another fortnight and pocket the additional $1.25m per episode? It was a lot of money – but surely chump change to the best-paid performer on television?

"Years later, I put a few things together. I was sitting next to him before a Monday table read [a run-through of the script], when he told me he'd lost half a million dollars betting on yesterday's football games. Forgive me for this mental image, but my a--hole puckered right up. I was shocked. Amazingly, he wasn't. He was confident he could 'get healthy' on the Monday night game," he says.

Lorre then recollects two separate incidents in which Sheen's luxury cars were stolen from inside the gated community where he lived, and crashed into a ravine. "Two different Maybachs, two hundred grand apiece, inexplicably trashed, no witnesses, no video evidence," he says. "Was it possible that Charlie wanted the two extra episodes in order to settle a debt with… bad people?"

In a panic, Lorre called Sheen's father, fellow actor Martin Sheen, only to be angrily told that Martin had run out of patience trying to put his son on the straight and narrow.

By the mid-2000s, Sheen was one of the highest-paid actors on TV, earning $2m per episode. (Pictured on Fox News in 2025) - Getty/Arturo Holmes

He next turned to CBS TV chief Les Moonves, who offered to fly Sheen into rehab personally. The actor agreed, asking only for a few days to sort his affairs out. But Sheen changed his mind and said he would clean up at home, at what he called "Sober Valley Lodge".

"Complete and utter bulls---. He was getting loaded the entire time," says Lorre – who called the "lodge" to ask for Sheen, only to be told the actor was freebasing (ingesting cocaine in its base form) in a locked room.

Lorre offered to quit. But that decision was taken from him when, on February 24 2011, Sheen went on The Alex Jones Show to attack Lorre – referring to him by his Hebrew name, Chaim Levin – and describe Alcoholics Anonymous as a "bootleg" cult.

CBS and Warner Bros Television had run out of patience and wanted Sheen gone. He felt betrayed and wrongly directed his rage at Lorre – firing off potshots on the talk-show circuit, on his own YouTube channel, and on a hastily arranged live theatre tour where he boasted about "winning" and his indomitable "tiger blood".

"Charlie started to go after me. On TV talk shows, online, everywhere. It was ugly. It was humiliating," Lorre says. "For about two years, I had to eat s---. Not just from Charlie. A lot of people piled on: David Letterman, NBC's Today show, ABC's Good Morning America, Alex Jones, Piers Morgan, TMZ, Twitter."

But if Lorre was done with Two and a Half Men, it was not done with him – as became clear when Les Moonves called and suggested replacing Sheen as the lead. "Somehow Hugh Grant's name came up. We met with him. Lee Aronsohn [the show's co-creator] and I wrote a couple of test scenes so he could see what kind of character he'd play. A character very much like Hugh Grant. He confided that he was terrified of working in front of an audience. No problem. I assured him I could get rid of the audience."

CBS offered $750,000 an episode, but when it came time for Grant to sign a contract, "he abruptly walked out of the room and became 'unreachable'", claims Lorre. Grant has since said he backed out of the project because he was "too scared" of signing up for a role for which there was no script, but it must surely have also been playing on his mind that at that point the show seemed little less than radioactive.

Lorre cast Ashton Kutcher instead, in the end, and Two and a Half Men tottered on for another four seasons.

The writer later reconciled with Sheen and worked with him on the HBO drama Bookie, in which Sheen played a gambler on the run from the mob, in the early 2020s. But the show came and went without a trace. Sheen had sobered up and was no longer making headlines every month. He was no longer a bad boy, and, it turned out, no longer a star either.

Chuck Lorre's Sitcom: A Memoir is out on Oct 13
