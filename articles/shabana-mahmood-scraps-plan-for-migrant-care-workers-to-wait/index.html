  3. How reliant is the care sector on foreign workers?published at 17:34 GMT
    17:34 GMT

    By Nicholas Barrett

    Foreign workers make up a third of staff in the independent care sector in England, according to figures from charity Skills For Care.

    A total of 6% of care workers were from EU countries in 2025/26, while 27% were from non-EU countries, including Nigeria, India and Zimbabwe.

    The King’s Fund, a health think tank, recorded nearly 1.7 million roles in the adult care sector in the same period, around 96,000 of which were vacant.

    This was despite a "substantial increase" in international recruitment in the sector. It said the latest vacancy rate is 6.1%, which is down from a high of 10.2% in 2022.

    Skills for Care also recorded a lower turnover rate among foreign care workers than those recruited in the UK.

    Ben Brindle, from the Oxford Migration Observatory think tank, told BBC Verify: "The care sector has struggled to attract UK workers because of its relatively poor pay and working conditions.”

    But he added that while care wages were relatively low by UK standards, “they can still be attractive to migrants compared with what they could earn in their home countries”.

  4. Retrospective nature of Mahmood's reforms has not changedpublished at 17:15 GMT
    17:15 GMT

    Jack Fenwick
    Political correspondent

    The watering down of these plans comes after months of pressure from some Labour MPs including Angela Rayner.

    There were two aspects of the plans that they were most unhappy with.

    Rayner had said the retrospective nature of the plans was "un-British" and amounted to changing "the goalposts".

    She and others had also criticised the impact on social care workers, who under the old plans would have had to wait 15 years for settlement.

    The most significant change in the updated plans has come on social care workers – those who came to the UK before May 2025 and earn £25,000 a year will now be able to get settlement after five years.

    But the retrospective nature hasn't shifted – many people who came to the UK expecting to qualify for settlement after five years will now have to wait 10 years.

    People inside the Home Office will be keenly looking at how Labour MPs react to that element.

    But the early evidence from inside the House of Commons chamber suggests that levels of anger are not that high.

    Backbenchers including Diane Abbott, John McDonnell and Stella Creasy have all raised potential concerns, but all chose to largely pull their rhetorical punches.

    Rayner was in the House of Commons earlier and didn’t stick around for the home secretary’s statement, but I'm told we shouldn’t read too much into that.

    17:07 GMT

    While Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood was outlining her immigration plans in the Commons, Reform UK leader Nigel Farage held a press conference.

    Farage says his party has consistently said indefinite leave to remain (ILR) after five years in the UK "is not a right" and is being abused by people on benefits.

    He then accuses Mahmood of following the Reform line, and says she "kicked the can down the road" for the next five years instead of removing ILR entirely.

    Farage says Mahmood's initial reform proposals were "tough", but calls today's announcement a "capitulation".

    He describes the announcement as a "very major U-turn" that will cost the country "a vast amount of money".

    The Reform leader says his party would review the status of anybody granted ILR and citizenship under the changes announced today and analyse whether they benefit the British economy.

  7. Mahmood sets out changes to settlement rules - recappublished at 16:50 GMT
    16:50 GMT

    Joe Coughlan
    Live reporter

    Setting out her plans for migration in the Commons, Shabana Mahmood has said migrant care workers who earn at least £25,000 and arrived in the UK before May 2025 will be eligible for indefinite leave to remain (ILR) after five years.

    • The home secretary also said those in the social care sector who have not fulfilled the conditions of their visa will be offered a "second chance" by applying for another five-year visa if they arrived before May 2025
    • A five-year path to settlement will remain open to workers on a salary of more than £50,000 a year and those who have made contributions in healthcare and education, as well as refugees who come to the UK via safe and legal routes
    • She says those making "exceptional" contributions, such as taxpayers in the highest band who earn more than £125,000, can apply for settlement after three years
    • Announcing a policy "guided above all by fairness", Mahmood said those who do not come under the new exceptions will now be on a 10-year path to settlement status, including those earning less than £50,000
    • She also set out the government’s disqualifying criteria for settlement, including having a criminal record and failing to speak English to a high standard – with those committing an immigration breach having their wait pushed back to 20 years
    • Those who arrived in the country by small boat and are in work or study will have to wait 20 years for settlement, with a further 10 years for those who have not entered work or study

    MPs are continuing to ask the home secretary questions about her planned reforms - stick with us as we bring you more updates from the Commons.

  13. Migrant settlement timelines - in a nutshellpublished at 16:28 GMT
    16:28 GMT

    We've just heard Mahmood's announcement that government is scrapping plans for migrant care workers already in the UK to wait 15 years for settlement.

    Under her proposals, here's a closer look at the timeline for legal migrants to permanently settle in the UK:

    • Three years: those earning over £125,141 and highly skilled migrants who are on routes such as the Global Talent and Innovator Founder
    • Five years: those paying higher rates of tax with a salary of over £50,271 and skilled public service workers such as doctors, nurses and teachers, refugees arriving on safe and legal routes
    • Ten years: this is now the new standard route for all other migrants, as well as refugees who move into work and study
    • Twenty years: those who claim certain benefits while in the UK or commit a visa offence, as well as refugees who arrive on a small boat and enter work or study
    • Thirty years: refugees who arrive on a small boat and do not enter work or study

  16. Documents show staggering response to Mahmood's initial proposalspublished at 16:21 GMT
    16:21 GMT

    Chris Mason
    Political editor

    The reaction to the government’s initial proposals was huge.

    In the documents published alongside the Home Secretary’s statement, we are told that the "consultation received 204,667 responses online and 904 responses over email".

    "Of these, 202,241 responses were submitted by individuals, of which nearly three quarters (73%) were current visa holders and a further 18% were British Citizens."

    Given the scale of the response to the initial plans, what did those who responded to the consultation say?

    The government says:

    "Most respondents disagreed with the proposed changes to the settlement framework (84% of individuals and 79% of organisations). Disagreement was higher among individuals with refugee status or humanitarian protection (89%) and current visa holders (86%) than among British Citizens (77%)."

    Some of the most common themes "were the perceived unfairness of the proposed changes, particularly for those already on a path to settlement," it adds.

  18. Small boat migrants who do not work or study to wait 30 years for settlementpublished at 16:18 GMT
    16:18 GMT

    Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood moves on to address settlement rights for refugees.

    "I know the people of this country wish to continue welcoming genuine refugees," she says, but adds that "there is nothing fair" about permitting unsafe and illegal entry into the UK.

    As a result, Mahmood says refugees who come to the UK via safe and legal routes will be placed on a five-year path to settlement - as fast a path as higher earners entering the UK today.

    At the same time, the government hopes to discourage illegal crossings by making the disqualifying period for settlement 20 years, including for those who are in work or study.

    A further 10 years will be added for people who enter the country by small boat and do not enter work or study.

    "These reforms are the right thing to do both morally and financially," Mahmood tells the Commons, and says a full impact assessment will be completed as the reforms are implemented.

    "But we will preserve the critical element of this reform that settlement must be linked to contribution."

    The same rules will apply to public sector workers in roles such as education and healthcare, the home secretary adds.

    This arrangement applies only to those who came to the UK before May 2025.

  20. Mahmood says some social care workers to be exempt from new 10-year settlement waitpublished at 16:13 GMT
    16:13 GMT

    Mahmood says that during the government's consultations on proposed immigration changes, many people brought up social care workers.

    She says 250,000 people and their dependants arrived or switched into the social care route between 2022 and 2025.

    The home secretary adds that this came at a time when the sector was under considerable pressure following the Covid-19 pandemic, but says the route was "abused" and that the last Conservative Government "lost control" of the country's borders.

    She says the government's solution works in two ways, first by providing a five-year path to settlement for care workers who lived up to the promise they made to the country, joined the social care route before May 2025 and earn £25,000 a year working in social care.

    She says the second group of people, who have not fulfilled the conditions of their visa, will be offered a "second chance" by applying for another five-year visa.

    "Everyone will have a fair shot to make a contribution and no one will be punished for the sins of their employer," she says.

The reaction to the government’s initial proposals was huge.

In the documents published alongside the Home Secretary’s statement, we are told that the "consultation received 204,667 responses online and 904 responses over email".

"Of these, 202,241 responses were submitted by individuals, of which nearly three quarters (73%) were current visa holders and a further 18% were British Citizens."

Given the scale of the response to the initial plans, what did those who responded to the consultation say?

The government says:

"Most respondents disagreed with the proposed changes to the settlement framework (84% of individuals and 79% of organisations). Disagreement was higher among individuals with refugee status or humanitarian protection (89%) and current visa holders (86%) than among British Citizens (77%)."

Some of the most common themes "were the perceived unfairness of the proposed changes, particularly for those already on a path to settlement," it adds.

Mahmood wraps up by saying earned settlement represents a "new paradigm" in the country's migration system which is "long overdue".

"The right to live in this country must now be earned, it will no longer be automatic," she says.

She calls it a "fair settlement for all" and ends her speech.

Small boat migrants who do not work or study to wait 30 years for settlementpublished at 16:18 GMT
16:18 GMT

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood moves on to address settlement rights for refugees.

"I know the people of this country wish to continue welcoming genuine refugees," she says, but adds that "there is nothing fair" about permitting unsafe and illegal entry into the UK.

As a result, Mahmood says refugees who come to the UK via safe and legal routes will be placed on a five-year path to settlement - as fast a path as higher earners entering the UK today.

At the same time, the government hopes to discourage illegal crossings by making the disqualifying period for settlement 20 years, including for those who are in work or study.

A further 10 years will be added for people who enter the country by small boat and do not enter work or study.

"These reforms are the right thing to do both morally and financially," Mahmood tells the Commons, and says a full impact assessment will be completed as the reforms are implemented.

Skilled healthcare and education workers will also be exemptpublished at 16:15 GMT
16:15 GMT

Mahmood says there are "tens of thousands" of legitimate vacancies in the care sector, so those who seek a new job in the industry and have the relevant skills "should be able to find one".

"Everyone will have a fair shot to make their contribution," she says.

"But we will preserve the critical element of this reform that settlement must be linked to contribution."

The same rules will apply to public sector workers in roles such as education and healthcare, the home secretary adds.

This arrangement applies only to those who came to the UK before May 2025.

Mahmood says some social care workers to be exempt from new 10-year settlement waitpublished at 16:13 GMT
16:13 GMT

Mahmood says that during the government's consultations on proposed immigration changes, many people brought up social care workers.

She says 250,000 people and their dependants arrived or switched into the social care route between 2022 and 2025.

The home secretary adds that this came at a time when the sector was under considerable pressure following the Covid-19 pandemic, but says the route was "abused" and that the last Conservative Government "lost control" of the country's borders.

She says the government's solution works in two ways, first by providing a five-year path to settlement for care workers who lived up to the promise they made to the country, joined the social care route before May 2025 and earn £25,000 a year working in social care.

She says the second group of people, who have not fulfilled the conditions of their visa, will be offered a "second chance" by applying for another five-year visa.

"Everyone will have a fair shot to make a contribution and no one will be punished for the sins of their employer," she says.
