Will a strong El Niño give us more snowfall this year? What does historical data say

TheTools · 17.09.2026 02:01 · source · permalink

COLORADO SPRINGS, Colo. (KKTV) - Climate models currently show a 75% chance of seeing the strongest El Niño on record develop this winter. The 11 Alert Weather Team is closely tracking Pacific Ocean temperatures.

When an El Niño reaches this strength, it reshapes weather patterns across North America by energizing the subtropical jet stream. That shift pulls moisture into Colorado, while usually keeping the Midwest drier.

Last winter, a weak La Niña pattern was in place, which tends to favor the Pacific Northwest with precipitation. This left Southern Colorado with significantly less snowfall.

Why past El Niños don’t tell what exactly will happen:

To see how past events stacked up, meteorologists track the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), where positive numbers indicate El Niño and negative values signal La Niña:

During the major El Niño winters of 1982 and 2015, Colorado Springs recorded modest snowfall totals between 28″ and 37″. That sits near our 30-year average of 33.6″ and just below the city’s all-time average of 39.0″. While 1997-1998 finished above average, nearly 20″ came from a single storm in late October. Without that storm, the rest of the winter was near average.

Why didn’t those massive tropical warm-ups translate into huge local snow totals? The answer lies in a body of water much farther north.

The Pacific Decadal Oscillation:

The main reason past El Niño winters varied comes down to a secondary pattern in the North Pacific: the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, or PDO.

During 1982, 1997, and 2015, the PDO was in a warm, positive phase. This ocean background altered storm tracks, keeping moisture away from Southern Colorado. Conversely, negative PDO phases during El Niño years correlate with above-average snow. This was a trend clearly seen in 1956, 1972, and 2023.

The 1956 Connection with the drought:

Outside of 2023, you have to go back to 1956 to find a strong El Niño paired with a sustained negative PDO. That season started neutral before a late-winter surge dumped 90″ of snow on Colorado Springs.

Before that 1956 winter, Southern Colorado was locked in a severe, multi-year drought. The intense late-season storm track completely erased those conditions heading into summer.

Uncharted Territory

While historical records contain various ENSO and PDO combinations, none match what models project for this upcoming winter. This winter is looking to have a negative PDO with ENSO potentially above +2.7°C. For meteorologists, that means there is no existing analog for comparison. We are stepping into brand-new territory.

Forecast models show an active southern jet favoring heavy upslope snow along the I-25 corridor and Southern Mountains. Still, late-season storm tracks will ultimately make or break our final totals.

Even if snow in Colorado Springs ends up spotty, building a strong mountain snowpack would be a huge win for local reservoirs, rivers, lakes, and drought recovery.
