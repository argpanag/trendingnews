A-Leagues look past Manchester City distraction to ‘showpiece’ Melbourne derby

TheTools · 23.10.2026 14:00 · source · permalink

The Premier League scandal may have gripped English football but the A-League club is ‘critical’ to football’s presence and new strategy in Australia

In Australia’s most populous city, the A-Leagues derby between Melbourne Victory and Melbourne City will have more than a tinge of Manchester on Saturday night.

Former United player Juan Mata, now a part-owner at Victory, will be farewelled after he chose not to play on with the A-League foundation club this season. The match is also City’s first at home since their parent club was found to have breached the Premier League’s financial rules.

These storylines are both peripheral to the main event, however. The marquee fixture is both a “shop window” for the A-League Men and the competition’s footballing “showpiece”, according to Melbourne Victory chief executive Caroline Carnegie, who is not shy about her ambitions to sell out the derby. “Anything less than full will be less than what we would like to see,” she says.

Melbourne derby attendances peaked at more than 50,000 for Victory’s 2015 semi-final win, in the first year their opponents swapped the red of Melbourne Heart for the sky blue of City. Since Covid, crowds have been strong but not as they once were, with only one sellout at the 2025 grand final. “This is a really good opportunity for us to get people in to see what we’re all about, and then hopefully, we can convert them into people who are coming to other games,” Carnegie says.

Under new coach Giovanni Savarese, Victory are reeling after a shock loss to semi-professional South Melbourne in the sold-out Australia Cup final, and a first round league defeat in Wellington. A-League officials have not been generous to the Venezuelan, handing Victory a schedule with their earliest derby since 2022.

Veteran City attacker Mat Leckie has recovered from a hamstring injury suffered at the World Cup in time to play a part, and he will be joined by Socceroos teammates Aziz Behich and Awer Mabil. The experienced trio will complement emerging talents such as Medin Memeti, Quinn MacNicol and Kavian Rahmani, emphasising the club’s status of one of Australia’s most important talent factories. “The young boys that we have are some of the best in the country,” Leckie says. “So the expectation on them to go on and do good things is there.”

The City model mixes proven internationals with opportunity for youth. Three of the A-League Men’s four most valuable players according to Transfer Room are at the club, with Memeti, Rahmani and forward Max Caputo all said to be roughly worth $1.5m. “You look at our team and everyone’s age, I think when the club’s looking for talent and new players to bring in, they’re obviously looking for the young talents of Australia,” Leckie says.

City’s approach reflects a broader shift of the A-Leagues towards development, from its initial promise as a mass entertainment product competing alongside major players like the AFL and NRL. Annual transfer revenue for Australian clubs more than trebled in 2023-24, and has stabilised at between $10m and $20m per year, softening the economic strain for owners faced with declining broadcast revenue.

That dynamic happens to be aligned with the motivations of City’s network – City Football Group. Socceroos goalkeeper Patrick Beach moved to another CFG club, Troyes, in the off-season, and his World Cup teammate Kai Trewin joined New York City FC at the start of the year. Melbourne City has set multiple outgoing A-League Women’s transfer records amid the global women’s football boom, most recently Leticia McKenna to Nottingham Forest for close to $200,000.

Melbourne City’s investment is focused on development. The owners splashed out $2m for a training pitch when they first bought into Australia, and a new academy worth close to $20m was unveiled in 2024 (with roughly half local government funding).

Steve Rosich, chief executive of A-Leagues operator APL, said City were a “significant” club in the competition and “critical” to football’s presence in Melbourne. “City continue to produce a number of Socceroos and Matildas, and we commend them in particular on their investment in the women’s program,” he says. “They have state of the art infrastructure, they’ve invested in the region. They’ve got an academy that’s produced well over 50 A-League players, which is a market leader.”

Melbourne and New York were the first stops in City’s multi-club ownership rollout and CFG connections ripple throughout Australian football. One of Rosich’s bosses, APL board member Simon Pearce, was an architect of the separation of the A-Leagues from governing body Football Australia in 2021 and is also vice-chair of Melbourne City. The PR executive was born in England but lived in Australia prior to moving to Abu Dhabi. There he found work with Khaldoon Al Mubarak before the well-connected Emirati became Manchester City chair. Pearce has since returned to live in Sydney.

FA chief executive at the time of the A-Leagues’ “unbundling”, James Johnson, spent time within the City family, as did several current and former executives. CFG minority owner Silver Lake also invested $140m in the A-Leagues in 2021.

While the Manchester City scandal may have gripped English football, across the other side of the world there are more pressing concerns. Leckie says the matter has been raised but not discussed at length in the Melbourne City dressing room. “Whatever happens, there’s nothing we can do,” he says. “It’s just play on.”

The outlook is similar for Rosich, who deflected questions about City at the season launch and already has his hands full. An arrangement with FA headed off a dispute with referees earlier this month, and a new player pay deal is close. Clubs have begun transferring increases agreed during negotiations, even before the final deal has been signed.

The work in the off-season has cleared a backlog – largely outstanding items dating back to the APL’s separation with FA – that has afforded oxygen to the start of the season, including this week’s clash in Melbourne. The “reach” for the round one broadcast on Network Ten, featuring the Sydney derby, was the highest since 2021 according to Rosich. The five matches recorded the highest average attendance for an opening round since 2019, even with Macarthur’s crowd of 3,441.

But there is clear appetite to do more to grow the league, and urgently. City coach Aurelio Vidmar called for an expansion of the competition on Friday by adding four clubs as soon as possible to lengthen the season. Rosich has had a “near term” strategy signed off by Pearce and his board colleagues, that will now be taken to clubs. The document will home in on the A-Leagues’ popularity among fans under 35, the potential of the A-League Women, football’s large participation base, and how these components can deliver immediate gains, especially around fan growth.

In that context, the Melbourne derby will be instructive. Carnegie says investors like Victory’s minority owners Mata and Tony Bloom from Brighton, QPR – whose owners Total Soccer Holdings hold the Central Coast Mariners licence – and Auckland FC’s American billionaire Bill Foley, show the “underlying foundations” of the A-Leagues already exist. “What we need is for people to come along and support, and then it will snowball from there.”
