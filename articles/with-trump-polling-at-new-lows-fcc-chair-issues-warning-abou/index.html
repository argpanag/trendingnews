With Trump polling at new lows, FCC chair issues warning about ‘fake polls’ | CNN Business

TheTools · 22.10.2026 00:08 · source · permalink

With numerous reputable polls showing Democrats poised to make big gains in the midterm elections, the Republican chair of the FCC is advising local broadcasters to beware of “fake polls,” without specifying which ones he thinks are bogus.

Brendan Carr, a close ally of President Donald Trump, warned FCC-licensed stations on Wednesday, the same day two new, highly respected national polls showed Trump registering another record-low approval rating.

His warning also came one day after a self-professed polling firm admitted to being a “fictional entity” that fabricated data and fooled news outlets.

That’s a real — and infuriating — problem, but it shouldn’t besmirch the high-quality polls that are helping inform the electorate.

So which polls was Carr warning about? His X post didn’t say, and he didn’t respond to CNN’s request for comment on Wednesday afternoon, leaving the matter nebulous. And perhaps that was partly the point.

A group of former FCC officials called Carr’s message “part of an accelerating White House strategy to tilt the electoral playing field in the President’s favor and deny election results that don’t go his way.”

The former officials also noted that “the FCC has zero authority to dictate editorial decisions.”

Indeed, Congress has prohibited the FCC from trying to prevent the broadcast of any point of view. But the agency is responsible for licensing local radio and TV stations across the US and upholding basic standards, and Carr has repeatedly used that limited power to pressure broadcasters.

Press advocacy groups argued that Carr’s latest post flew in the face of the First Amendment.

“Literally every time he attacks the press, it’s because of reporting that was unfavorable to his boss, Donald Trump, whose gilded face he’s been known to wear as a lapel pin,” said Seth Stern, the chief of advocacy at the Freedom of the Press Foundation.

Right now there’s a lot of unfavorable reporting about the Republican Party’s electoral prospects, given widespread voter dissatisfaction with the economy and the state of the country.

Trump has defaulted to calling the polls “fake” and has occasionally threatened to match his words with actions. “I’ve been fighting pollsters,” Trump said at a rally on Tuesday.

But if Trump has intended to chill reporting on public opinion, he hasn’t succeeded.

Last month, an Iowa judge dismissed the president’s lawsuit against pollster J. Ann Selzer, her polling company, the Des Moines Register and Gannett. A spokesperson for Trump’s legal team vowed to appeal.

On Wednesday, Carr claimed that this election season has brought “a significant surge in entirely Fake Polls and Suppression Polls designed to do nothing more than interfere with the regular functioning of the electoral process and alter the normal patterns of voter turnout and enthusiasm.”

He said “many journalists and broadcasters have been amplifying or airing” the polls, though he didn’t name any polls or names.

Carr then invoked the FCC’s power to penalize stations that violate the agency’s policies against “news distortion” and “hoaxes,” though those policies are narrowly tailored and rarely enforced.

“Broadcasters are advised to avoid engaging in News Distortions or running Broadcast Hoaxes by airing Polls that would violate their regulatory obligations,” he wrote.

Anna Gomez, the FCC’s lone Democratic commissioner, immediately spoke out against Carr’s message.

“Pollsters who intentionally mislead the public should answer for it, but broadcasters who report publicly available polling data should never be punished when a poll is wrong,” Gomez wrote on X. “FCC rules on news distortion or hoaxes do not apply to a station that reports this data in good faith.”

Stern made a similar point in a statement on Wednesday. “News broadcasters have a First Amendment right to report polling data, even if it proves to be inaccurate, just like they have a First Amendment right to report on Carr and Trump’s own countless lies and distortions, even when they turn out to be inaccurate,” he said.

By inveighing against “fake polls,” Carr is once again embracing the rhetoric of the president who appointed him in 2017 and promoted him to chairman last year.

The timing is striking, since the midterm elections are less than two weeks away and polls show Democrats with an edge in many key races.

Many viewers and readers wonder about the reliability of polls, and the reality is that some are more trustworthy than others.

Earlier this week, the poll-tracking site FiftyPlusOne prepared an investigation into a group called “Wedgewood Polls,” which had released more than a dozen election-season polls.

FiftyPlusOne included the group’s data in its polling averages, but found evidence of fabrications. After the poll tracker asked for comment, Wedgewood issued a statement claiming it was an experiment “to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.”

FiftyPlusOne co-founder G. Elliott Morris charged that “they are trying to cover up their fraud with the ‘we were doing a social experiment’ excuse.”

The American Association for Public Opinion Research condemned the fabrication on Wednesday.

“Professional polling organizations are transparent, ethical, and work hard to provide critical information to the public and other stakeholders. It is important that we don’t conflate fraudulent activity like this with legitimate election polling,” the association’s president Mary Losch said.

Even if it was just an experiment, it did real damage, playing right into the hands of public figures who want to sow distrust in even the most authoritative polling sources.

“Fake news, fake polls, they’re all fake,” Trump said Tuesday. He then added in the next breath, “I have some great polls.”
