‘The greatest game ever made’: readers and Guardian staff on Grand Theft Auto VI

TheTools · 20.11.2026 18:04 · source · permalink

Rockstar’s newest installment of its hit franchise still has plenty of action, along with a dose of ‘healthy masculinity’

Described as the biggest entertainment launch in history, the new Grand Theft Auto game has prompted avid fans around the world to take the day off work, stock up on snacks and celebrate its arrival after 13 years of waiting.

While some have already spent hours immersed in the game’s criminal escapades, others have found themselves doing household chores, building houses or simply taking in the scenery – which includes more than 170 flora and fauna species native to Florida. GTA VI has generated extraordinary global anticipation for its action-packed gameplay, but players are now finding new delight in the game’s more intimate moments.

From the relationship between protagonists Jason and Lucia to the painstaking world-building of Leonida, Rockstar’s fictional take on Florida, players have shared their wide-ranging experiences with the Guardian, revealing what has surprised and challenged them about this long-awaited release – and what this all means for the series’ evolution.

Pete Langhelt, 39, from Colchester, pre-ordered GTA VI “as soon as I could”, and has been looking forward to its release for a long time. “Out of my group of friends I’m probably the only one really nerding out about it,” he says.

While Pete hasn’t been able to take time from work in the civil service, he’s still clocked “about 10 hours” of gameplay already. “The core of the missions themselves are pretty similar,” he says. “You turn up somewhere, shoot a load of people and escape. But the development of the characters has changed an awful lot. I have enjoyed the dynamic between Jason and Lucia. I never thought you could make comfortable domesticity into an enjoyable side activity in a video game.”

Pete has played all the installments of GTA franchise from the first, when he was “probably too young to be playing”. “I think Red Dead Redemption 2 was my favourite Rockstar game going into this, and it seems to have developed the things that I really liked about Red Dead.”

The game encourages exploration, he says. “The day and night cycle seems a lot less intense than GTA V, meaning you don’t feel rushed to get things done. I can let Jason take a slow start to the day before taking a slow drive or boat ride to whatever criminal activity needs completing.”

His partner is less keen. “She’ll have to put up with me being on the Xbox for the next few weeks,” he says.

A 59-year-old MBA retiree from Venice, Florida, was also struck by just how much of the experience revolves around everyday life. He described it as the “most beautiful, real, open-world game that’s ever existed”.

“I was surprised that, with about 10-plus hours of play, most of my time has been spent performing mundane tasks like home repair, grocery shopping, personal grooming and trying to open a bank account. The realism is incredible, but perhaps too real,” he said.

“It’s as fully realized as a game world could be. I never expected to see anything more fully developed,” he added.

Rob Smeraldo, 28, from Dundee, took the day off work yesterday to play the game on his PlayStation 5. “I made sure I had loads of snacks and Domino’s,” he says. And already, Rob believes that “GTA VI must be the greatest game ever made”.

“I think the realism and just the detail put into it is absolutely astounding. The shadows, the way the hair moves when people move around … They took their time, but it’s definitely been worth it,” he says.

Rob is a longtime GTA fan and wore a GTA VI T-shirt and drank from a GTA mug to mark the occasion. “I purchased the physical copy [of the game] as soon as it became purchasable – I just wanted the memorabilia, you know?”

Ella Delancey, 35, of Berkshire has been playing GTA since she was a child. “GTA III is a huge core memory for me. My brother and I were much too young to be playing it and I definitely wouldn’t let my daughter play it now, but at the time we were obsessed.”

So far, the social and brand director has been surprised by how much there is to do aside from pushing the story forward. “I’m not very far into it yet, but I’m already struck by the graphics and the sheer depth of what you can do. It’s very violent, obviously, but I play by the rules, which probably makes me a bit boring. I’ve been birdwatching and exploring, and there’s so much to do outside the main story.”

There is one thing she is struggling with, however, and that’s Lucia, one of the main protagonists, leaving her child in a cafe and going to prison, only to come out and start committing more crimes. “As a mother, that doesn’t sit comfortably with me. I know it’s a game and you have to suspend your morals to a degree, but that part has given me pause,” she says.

“I do think it’s a step forward that you can no longer hire sex workers as in previous games. GTA still has a complicated relationship with misogyny – and it’s far from perfect, but that change feels significant.”

While Ella hasn’t taken any time off work to play, she has set aside time on Friday to play or watch her partner play. “It’s so immersive that it doesn’t feel like a chore, and I can see it replacing our usual after-work TV downtime for a little while,” she says.

Clint, 36, who works in the music industry in Chicago, describes the game as “truly one of one”, pointing to its unexpectedly domestic moments and the relationship between Jason and Lucia as some of its biggest draws.

“The story is just downright cute. I just had my character build an Ikea table with his girlfriend, and loved it. I typically skip some cutscenes, but now I’m rewatching them when I miss something,” he says.

Clint says Jason and Lucia’s relationship is particularly significant, describing it as an example of healthy masculinity that could resonate with younger players.

“The main character’s relationship is healthy (at least so far, at six hours in – and ‘healthy’ for a GTA game), which I see as such a positive. Positive both because I can relate to caring for one’s partner and because I love that real, healthy masculinity is being portrayed. I love that you are incentivized to complete menial tasks to make Lucia happy in between the wild and chaotic portions of the storyline. I’d like to hope that this might show young and/or disaffected men that misogyny is super lame and unmanly, at least in some small way,” he says.

Beyond the relationship dynamics, Clint praises the game’s approach to world building, which he says “feels so deep and textured”.

“Also refreshing are the built-in guardrails that make the game feel so lifelike … The player cannot just go steal a car or engage in indiscriminate violence without consequence. Every fight comes with police scrutiny, even speeding got me chased until I slowed down. It moderates what was a free-for-all melee atmosphere in the previous titles. This both enhances a sense of realism and immersion and makes me wonder if that doesn’t have some, albeit minor, benefit towards the ‘video games breed violence’ crowd,” he says.

The Guardian’s own team is also getting in on the action. The US’s deputy sport editor, Bryan Graham, says “it’s been a strange 24 hours” since the game dropped.

“Maybe we’ve been starved of genuine monocultural events for so long that we’ve forgotten how to handle them. The first sign the discourse might be getting a little carried away came when the New York Times invoked Jean Baudrillard, Jorge Luis Borges and bell hooks in their suite of GTA 6 think pieces (a dream blunt rotation I’d be sure to remember later),” he said. “Meanwhile, the day’s sanest take came from Glenn Beck, of all people, who went viral for pushing back on the strange spectacle of gen Xers scolding younger generations for getting excited about a video game.”

Graham continued: “Then came midnight. Really, it should be called Florida: The Game. With apologies to Sean Baker, I’m not sure there’s ever been a piece of media that is more of a love letter to America’s weirdest state, or more affectionate in its ridicule.

“Having played only a couple of hours before passing out, I’ve mostly been poking its version of the Florida Keys, where shimmering waterways give way to sun-bleached houses, shabby strip malls and alligators in the reeds. But that small sampling has been enough to see that Rockstar has created something both exquisitely observed and gloriously unhinged, a funhouse mirror held up to an America that increasingly resembles its own parody. Make no mistake: this is art!”

Tom Johnson, the Guardian’s vice-president of audience and strategy, says he was “probably lying to myself when I fired up my PS5 at six thirty this morning to ‘make sure the game installed properly’.

“I blinked and nearly an hour had passed and I had to remind myself I did not in fact have the GTA flu and needed to start getting ready for work,” he quips.

Of the game, Johnson said “the stunning graphics and well-paced storyline sucks you in immediately by allowing you to play as both characters, drive a car, captain a boat and even fly a small plane.

“Even the more mundane side quests are enthralling – I spent a few minutes doing a kettlebell workout and painting a room before wondering if I was just playing a sexier version of The Sims.

“I grew up in Miami, far away from the more glamorous parts of the beach, and I can’t wait for this workday to end so I can head west towards Kendall in search of my childhood home. At this point, given the game’s commitment to realism, it wouldn’t surprise me if an accurate replica of my parents’ car will be there waiting for me, parked in the driveway.”
