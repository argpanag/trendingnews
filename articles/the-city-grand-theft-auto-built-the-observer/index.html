The River Cafe’s starriest seat

When Grand Theft Auto VI is released on 19 November, it will confirm the video game’s status as the world’s largest entertainment franchise. Financial filings suggest that the cumulative development costs of game-maker Rockstar’s global studios have reached almost $2bn including marketing. But GTA V alone pocketed a cool $6bn. Not bad for a game first dreamed up above a disused chip shop in Dundee.

At the outset, “only two people on the team had ever made a game before,” says Brian Baglow, now founder and CEO of the Scottish Games Network but then “head of propaganda and indoctrination” at the game’s inventor DMA Design. “Everybody was making it up as they went along. No deadlines, no precedent, all we had was – is it working and is it fun?”

It was the fun, argues Prof Gregor White at Dundee’s Abertay University, that all but saved the city as deindustrialisation tore out factories and shipbuilding, leaving high levels of poverty and unemployment. Today, Dundee has the highest share of gaming companies per working-age person of any British city. A research and development hub in AI, cybersecurity, fintech, electric vehicles, medicine and life sciences, the city is also home to comics Beano and the Dandy. There are more statues of characters from video games and comics in Dundee than of empire builders or slavers.

“There’s a particular subversive streak in Dundee, we won’t be told what to do,” says Baglow. “We know what we’re all about and, quite frankly, fuck you. That’s why GTA never takes itself seriously. It’s satirical and absurd with the business names, the talk [radio] DJs, the music, some of the asides that you hear other characters saying to each other.”

This absurdity came at exactly the right time for Dundee, he says. 

“You can’t underplay the effect the success of Grand Theft Auto had on the city,” White says. “Things were spiralling, then suddenly there was an industry and a career and parents would allow their children to study video games development at university. 

“Sony came, and Microsoft came, and Nvidia came to speak to DMA. They’d get involved in the city and that broader ecosystem spawned the rest of these successes.”

However, the real hero of this story is the Timex factory and its irreverent workforce. Home of the US watchmaker since 1945, the factory became the assembly line for Clive Sinclair’s ZX Spectrum. At the time of its release, it was one of the cheapest all-colour home computers on the market, but it still sold for between £150 and £200 – “unless you lived in Dundee where, for the princely sum of a fiver and a well-positioned pack of Embassy Regal cigarettes, you could acquire a Sinclair Spectrum for home use”, according to Chris van der Kuyl, founder of Minecraft developer 4J Studios. “I had six at that point. Every kid in Dundee had one.”

Van der Kuyl graduated with a computer science degree, wanted to start a business and spent about a year and a half thinking it wasn’t going to be in games. “Then after Ferrari after Ferrari and Porsche after Porsche passed me coming out the DMA car park, we decided to follow those guys.”

DMA itself was a product of the illicit trade in ZX Spectrums. It was founded by David Jones, Mike Dailly, Steve Hammond and Russell Kay, who met at the Kingsway Amateur Computer Club in the local tech college. Jones ended up working at the Timex factory but was made redundant in 1988 after Clive Sinclair threw his well-earned Spectrum cash into a ridiculous EV called the Sinclair C5 which drove the company to bankruptcy.

Backed by Jones’s £3,000 severance pay, DMA opened its first offices above a former fish and chip shop in Nethergate in August 1989. On 14 February 1991, DMA released Lemmings, a game inspired by an animated character demo Dailly had created during a lunch break. It sold 55,000 copies on its first day and achieved lifetime sales of 20 million copies across 21 platforms. The co-founders were all suddenly millionaires.

GTA followed. Originally a cops-chasing-robbers concept, the Beano spirit flipped roles so the players were the crooks in a game that grown-up corporations would never make. The company was bought by the Edinburgh-based Rockstar Games. Perhaps in search of that founding spirit, however, it returned to Dundee in 2020 when Rockstar’s owner Take-Two Interactive acquired local developer Ruffian Games.

Which is why it’s so important to learn the Dundee lesson, Baglow argues. “If you get a technology into the hands of hundreds of bored teenagers in council estates, they’ll push it to breaking point to find out what its real use case is,” he says. “Just like kids in the Bronx took the failed TR-808 drum machine and made it the cornerstone of hip-hop. Taking the lessons of Dundee and GTA into AI, for instance, it would not be as psychotic as what we’re currently seeing with the tech bros. It would be anti-authoritarian and a lot more fun.”
