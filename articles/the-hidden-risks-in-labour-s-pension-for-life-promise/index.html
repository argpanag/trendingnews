The hidden risks in Labour’s ‘pension for life’ promise

TheTools · 14.11.2026 09:56 · source · permalink

Government plans could railroad retirees into collective schemes that leave them worse off

Labour has promised to “resurrect proper pensions” and offer millions of savers an income for life by overhauling Britain’s retirement system.

Plans to introduce “retirement collective defined contribution” (RCDC) schemes will push pensioners towards receiving regular payouts, which ministers said would bridge the gaping chasm between private and public sector retirees.

The approach sounds sensible at first glance, given that 15 million people are currently undersaving for retirement and on course to be poorer than today’s pensioners.

But former pensions ministers and industry professionals warned it wasn’t the right solution for everyone, with one expert saying retirees could be railroaded into complex, volatile schemes instead of receiving the “much better deal” they were promised.

Under RCDC schemes, the level of payouts is not guaranteed and if investments underperform, pensioners could see their monthly income fall.

Telegraph Money examines the risks behind the Government’s latest grand vision for pensions.

‘A much better deal’

As part of Labour’s flagship pension reforms, which became law earlier this year, providers will have to offer customers a default retirement solution. Known as “guided retirement”, it means that when a saver wants to access their pension, they’ll be offered a default option alongside their usual choices.

The Government is proposing that one such option that firms should provide is an RCDC scheme, which pools members’ resources and pays retirees a lifelong target income.

The amount they receive depends on the size of their pot and investment returns. Although it’s not guaranteed, payouts could also rise annually with inflation.

In announcing the plans, Torsten Bell, the pensions minister, said too many workers were left without a genuine pension and deserved a “much better deal”.

However, experts have cautioned that an RCDC scheme might not be smooth sailing for everyone.

‘Locked into the wrong option’

RCDC schemes would provide a target annual income, initially based on the size of someone’s pot and expected investment returns. By pooling the pension funds of every member, the scheme can invest at scale, spread risk and potentially boost returns.

However, incomes are not guaranteed and can fall in any given year, depending on investment performance and the number of people in the scheme. This could affect the quality of someone’s retirement, particularly if they had counted on receiving more.

Income decisions are also based on detailed calculations made by experienced actuaries, which may be complex for retirees to understand.

Rachel Vahey, of investment platform AJ Bell, said: “The Government rightly wants to help more people make better decisions regarding their pension savings. But we could end up with people railroaded into a solution that doesn’t suit their needs or wants in retirement.

“Savers need straight talking, not spin. They must get clear, balanced information on the risk of every option, including whether they can transfer out.

“Otherwise, pension savers could find themselves in arrangements they don’t fully understand, with less retirement income than they were expecting – and possibly no way of changing their minds later.”

Rob Morgan, of Charles Stanley, added that such schemes were “not a free lunch”.

He said: “Income levels are not guaranteed, inflation protection may fall short, and payments could be reduced if investment returns disappoint. Risk does not disappear in an RCDC; it has simply moved behind the scenes into a collective structure.”

Lack of flexibility

When pension freedoms were introduced in 2015, savers were handed much more control over how they spent their pots. Rather than being pushed into an annuity, they could enter drawdown or even take their entire pot in one go.

Under Labour’s plans, however, savers in RCDC schemes could be given a deadline of just 12 months to change their minds. Once this period ends, they would be stuck with a regular income – hitting their financial flexibility.

Sir Steve Webb, of consultants LCP and a former pensions minister, said: “Once you are in such a scheme, you are expected to stay in it and cannot generally change your mind beyond the first year.

“The risk is that people have all of their pension wealth put into such a scheme ‘by default’ and only later realise that they would value the freedom to take some of their pot as a lump sum, perhaps to help children with a wedding or a house deposit, or to pay for the trip of a lifetime.

“It’s vital that people understand this before the decision becomes irreversible.”

Lost investment growth and smaller inheritances

Another key issue is the potential for lost investment returns. Pension pots continue to grow tax-free until they’re withdrawn, boosting someone’s funds even after they’ve retired.

In an RCDC scheme, payouts can increase if investments perform, but it’s unlikely to be at the same rate. The holder also doesn’t have the same level of control over when and how their funds are invested.

Chris Rudden, of digital wealth manager Moneyfarm, said people might miss out on investment growth that could “radically improve their retirement” and they could face leaving less behind for their loved ones.

He said: “In 11 years since pension freedoms, a fairly simple investment strategy could have easily doubled someone’s savings, and retirements can be three times that long.

“People also lose control of how and when they can access their own money and, crucially, there’s nothing left to leave to loved ones because it stops when someone dies.

“We can see the Government’s intention, but this approach is a bit heavy-handed. People can spend decades in retirement, so there’s plenty of time to let that money grow, rather than encouraging everyone to cash in their chips immediately.”

The Government is running a consultation on its proposals, with experts across the industry expected to contribute. The final regulations are then expected to be enforced from summer 2029 for most providers.

A government spokesman said: “Millions of private sector workers reach retirement with a pot of money rather than a pension they can rely on. Guided retirement will give them the option of a secure income for life.

“No one will be put into a default pension without engaging with their scheme, seeing the options and giving their explicit consent. Anyone who wants something different will always be free to choose it.

“Collective pensions share risks such as market shocks across members rather than leaving savers to face it alone.”
