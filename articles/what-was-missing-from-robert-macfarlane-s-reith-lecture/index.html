What was missing from Robert Macfarlane’s Reith Lecture

I listened to the 2026 Reith Lecture while walking round my local park, watching my dog snuffle and romp through fallen leaves. It was appropriate. This year’s lecturer, Robert Macfarlane, professor of Literature and the Environmental Humanities at Cambridge University, is known for his nature writing, and his first lecture was about earth (his other three will take on the other elements: air, water, fire).

Macfarlane’s voice is young and light. He’s 50, but sounds as if he’s in his 20s. He talked to me, as I stomped along sodden paths, and the audience in front of him at the British Museum, about what we humans think of ourselves. We have this idea that we’re at the top of a ladder when it comes to living creatures, said Macfarlane; but it’s just an idea, rooted in Aristotle’s De Anima.

And our arrogance can be traced back even further, to a fragment of clay on display in the British Museum, the remains of a text from ancient Mesopotamian times. This was the Epic of Gilgamesh, in which Gilgamesh and Enkidu lay waste to a thriving forest: “the original ecocide”, as Macfarlane had it. He is big on stories, he said, because they are how humans understand the world, and if we want the world to change, we need to change our story. He argued that we should replace our self-aggrandising tale of ladder-topping with a story of us as part of a “mesh” of nature. In this, humans are not superior to other animals, or even plants, but intimately involved with them; we co-exist, intertwined, on a planet with finite resources.

I think many people would agree, and Macfarlane can certainly write. I was moved by a section where he described a dying man who marvelled at the beauty of silver birch trees and the joy of a blackbird’s song. But there were a couple of problems with his lecture. First: his delivery. He gave his well-crafted ideas an amateur thesp’s delivery, emphasising the wrong words, running the ends of sentences into the next. It killed his few jokes (“Go Dolly!” he said, about Dolly Parton, to stony silence), and made his modern language seem odd (“main-character-energy-cubed” landed as though spoken by an alien).

Second – and this difficulty was identified by host Anita Anand – is that when we’re asked to save the planet, it can feel overwhelming. How can we save anything, when impenetrable corporations are pillaging nature and nation-leading lunatics are waging wars? (At one point, Macfarlane said that, globally, three-quarters of back-boned wild animals have disappeared in the last 50 years: a statistic so bleak it made me breathless.) During the audience questions, he addressed this, recommending that we start small: clearing our local river, joining community groups. It felt almost tragically minimal.

But it was during the questions that things perked up. It was fun hearing Macfarlane trying to be polite to a Telegraph writer who shoots deer, or primly admitting to an eco-activist that he might, under certain circumstances, join in with direct action. “If I came to a point where I thought that me undertaking peaceful protest as a form of civil disobedience would be more effective than the work I can do from outwith prison, I would do that.” I enjoyed his lecture, but the atmosphere lifted with the spikiness of the Q&A.

Here’s a spiky man: Ross Kemp, rope-veined luvvie, a man always on the lookout for someone who might be a Traitor. He once had a podcast called the Kempcast; now he’s the host of Heist Society, a four-part series about the life of Benedict Marsh. Marsh is a well-born international fraudster who, with friend-of-the-royals Darius Guppy, staged a fake armed robbery in 1990 to bag an insurance payout of £1.84m. (Spoiler: they were convicted of conspiracy to fraud and went to prison for five years.) “How does privilege become a passport to criminality?” wonders Kemp in his intro, and, through his chat with Marsh, we proceed to find out. This is not my sort of podcast: long anecdotes, dramatic music, sealed-off, bumptious ego. Kemp does his best, but Marsh is impenetrable: he’s decided he knows who he is and gets busy telling us. The result is not as much fun as he thinks it is.
