Key highlights from Trump's Asia visit

• Historic talks: President Donald Trump is back at the White House after the first ever US-China-Russia leaders’ meeting in Shenzhen. As allies of Ukraine gathered in Berlin, he posted on social media, “God, protect us from such friends.” Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner and envoy Steve Witkoff also attended the talks.

Trump teases Friday press conference after returning to the White House from China

President Donald Trump returned to the White House Thursday evening after a whirlwind — and historic — trip to the Philippines and China earlier this week.

Trump, fresh off of 15 hours of travel from Shenzhen, China, declined to answer questions about whether he’d meet with Ukrainian President Vlodymyr Zelensky, after meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping earlier Thursday.

White House national cyber director being replaced as nation reels from tech outages

Sean Cairncross is being replaced as the Trump administration’s national cyber director, a source close to the White House said Thursday — a move which comes as the nation is still reeling from a string of cyber outages.

He is the latest top cyber official to leave the administration.

Trump says he's "not rushing" any Iran decision, eyes the new year

Historic summit leaves looming questions about what truly was discussed regarding Ukraine

The peace process around Ukraine carried the burden of expectation in the historic summit between US President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping in Shenzhen. It was discussed, a readout said, but no public declarations of actual progress followed.

Instead, Trump called into the meeting of European leaders in Berlin to say … well, we don’t really know what. UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham suggested Trump was not “rosy” about Russia and upset about the loss of (often Russian) life in the war. He said progress had been made and that no one would talk about Ukraine without Ukraine.

A senior European official aware of the call’s contents described it as “a good call.” Trump’s main message was that “Putin is tired, wants to end this, ready for a deal.” The official said Trump’s suggestion was “to reach out and grasp at the opportunity.” He said the Europeans were “constructive in response but want proof.”

He said Trump also said at the end of the call that “the war needs to end because so many people are dying and it’s costing money (the European states) would want to spend on other things in their own countries.”

Trump’s envoy and son-in-law, Jared Kushner, maintained Europe would be part of the peace process going forward, in multilateral talks with Ukraine, Russia, and the US. And that was even as Putin had, hours earlier, said European officials must come visit him in Moscow to convince him if they could join.

Europe had staged the Berlin meeting to present a united, enduring front against whatever emerged from Shenzhen. And then nothing really did. Yet, Trump will present his vision of the summit on Friday in a noon ET news conference when he is back on his home turf, and Beijing and Moscow are deep into their dusks.

There is as yet little reason for Europe and Ukraine to stop feeling anxious about what may have been agreed on behind closed doors. Putin sounded defiant, almost belligerent, even as he endured a gentle but palpable snub from his hosts, in photo ops and dinner arrangements. He still opted, at his Shenzhen news conference, to contradict Trump’s envoys about Europe’s role in the talks.

In short, the summit saw three strongmen meet, who are either killing Ukrainians, paying for that, or often more worried about Russian deaths than those they seek to occupy. In keeping with their habitual lack of transparency, we don’t know what they said. And that should be no cause for comfort, if not pause for alarm.

Four-way talks on Ukraine could happen "very soon," says German chancellor

Speaking in Berlin, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz hinted that four-way talks between Ukraine, Russia, the United States and Europe could “take place very soon.”

At a six-way press conference, after the largest gathering of the Coalition of the Willing, Merz said they are looking for “neutral” venues, like the United Arab Emirates, but he didn’t expect it to be difficult to work out.

The chancellor added: “we hope that Russia too is willing to engage in these talks, as agreed with the United States.”

If all those conditions were met “we are willing to engage President [Vladimir] Putin on all of these topics,” he added.

The view from Moscow, however, appears less clear.

Before his departure back to Russia, Putin suggested he would like European leaders to visit him in Moscow so that “their position can be clarified.”

“We have proposed, through diplomatic channels, that the Europeans come to Moscow so that their position can be clarified. Once that is done, we will decide on the format for continuing the dialogue with Ukraine,” he said.

Trump sent "very strong message" to Putin and Xi, says Kushner in Berlin

Jared Kushner said that US President Donald Trump dialed into the meeting of the Coalition of the Willing in Berlin and gave his assessment of his talks with the leaders of Russia and China in Shenzhen.

The president’s son-in-law — speaking alongside the leaders of Germany, France, Ukraine and the United Kingdom at the Berlin summit — said Trump had conveyed “a very strong message to both of them [Xi and Putin] on the desire for peace and would like to engage both of them very aggressively to get there.”

He didn’t elaborate on the details of that message but said Trump felt “he also came away believing that peace is achievable.”

Steve Witkoff, the president’s special envoy, noted that Trump made “Ukraine and a peaceful end to this” a central part of his message at the trilateral talks.

British Prime Minister Andy Burnham said that on his call to Berlin, Trump had spoken “passionately” about the loss of life on both sides.

The Europeans reaffirmed their position that Europe requires a seat at the negotiating table.

“Negotiations can only succeed together with Ukraine and Europe. A Yalta-style format will not bring peace,” said German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, referring to the 1945 Yalta Conference at which Allied powers carved up the postwar world into spheres of influence.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky later told reporters that he is especially concerned with multilateral negotiations and having an agenda to understand what will be negotiated with Russia.

The Ukrainian leader also said how long the war will drag on depends much on “the readiness of the US to mediate and pressure (Russia).”

Referring to the trilateral meeting in Shenzhen, Zelensky said both the US and China have the ability to help stop the war.

Trump made 700-plus stock trades in September, including in AI companies

Why the trilateral summit was such a gift for Putin

Russia’s President Vladimir Putin desperately wanted to be seen as the leader of a world power that, far from isolated and shunned over his brutal war in Ukraine, is back on the international stage.

The brief summit with his American and Chinese counterparts in Shenzhen handed the Kremlin strongman exactly the kind of public relations coup even he had barely dared to hope for.

“No-one expected everything to go so well,” Putin gushed to his Chinese host after the 90-minute meeting closed. Russian state media, always loyal to the Kremlin, soon chimed in, cheering it as a “historic” summit.

And for Putin, it wasn’t just a flattering photo-op with US President Donald Trump and Chinese leader Xi Jinping at his side.

The substance of the trilateral talks also seems to have been somewhat favorable to the Russian leader, whom Trump had warmly greeted as “my friend” earlier in the day.

A Chinese readout of what was discussed behind closed doors said the three leaders agreed, among other things, to “increase production and supply of refined petroleum products.”

Such an export boost, if it materializes would be a massive economic boon for Russia, where its vast oil and gas industry is under crippling international sanctions, badly denting the Kremlin’s war economy.

Later, taking questions in Shenzhen, Putin used the stage to once again blame Kyiv and its European backers for the ongoing war in Ukraine, now in its fifth year. They are “not yet agreeing to compromises regarding a settlement in Ukraine,” Putin told reporters.

“But they might eventually come around to it,” he added.

US readout of trilateral talks echoes those of China and Russia

The US readout on the trilateral meeting between US President Donald Trump, Russian leader Vladimir Putin and Chinese leader Xi Jinping largely echoed those from Chinese state media and the Kremlin — saying the three talked about global energy flows, changes to the United Nations, the International Criminal Court and reducing nuclear risks.

In a release from the White House, officials reiterated that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought” and promised to “conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing the proliferation of nuclear weapons.”

The US readout also mentioned the leaders’ talks about protecting the immunity of state officials from the ICC, adding that court officials must not “abuse their jurisdiction.” Potential changes to the UN were discussed so the group “can respond more effectively to global challenges and conform to its fundamental mission of international peace and security.”

Catch up on what happened in China and reaction to the historic summit

An unprecedented three-way meeting between US President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin and China’s Xi Jinping unfolded Thursday – marking a historic shift in US foreign policy under the Trump administration.

The three men sat across from each other on a long table in Shenzhen, China’s preeminent tech metropolis, where Trump heralded a “special responsibility to maintain open, respectful and productive dialogue.”

The 81-minute meeting didn’t yield any diplomatic breakthroughs. The talks themselves were the deliverable.

Meanwhile, in Berlin, dozens of European leaders comprising the Coalition of the Willing – a tranche of nations coordinating support for Ukraine – are meeting to react to the trilateral discussion. They are hoping to amplify their collective voice in ongoing talks to end Russia’s war in Ukraine. Trump’s son-in-law, Jared Kushner, and his special envoy, Steve Witkoff, will also be present.

After another day of high-stakes diplomacy, here’s what you need to know:

What did Trump say?

  • ‘We’re really making history’: Trump called both men friends in individual meetings beforehand, telling his Chinese counterpart, “I think as long as you and I are in charge of things, we’ll never have a problem.” He has used flattery to massage relations with both men over his tenure – and extended that display today.

What did Putin say?

  • War in Ukraine: The Kremlin’s invasion in Ukraine was one of the most salient items on the agenda. After the meeting, Putin described the “complex” state of play – insisting Trump was “genuinely committed to improving bilateral relations and bringing an end to the conflict.”
  • Falsehoods: The Russian president doubled down on lies about the war. Putin further claimed Kyiv and its European allies were “not yet agreeing to compromises regarding a settlement,” re-stating demands that Ukraine cede territory.

What did Xi say?

  • A trifecta of “great powers”: The Chinese president was able to present himself as a diplomatic heavyweight – even as economic problems rumble at home. As the talks got underway, Xi said, “History tells us that when great powers are at peace, the world is secure; when great powers clash, the world is in chaos.”

How have other world leaders reacted?

  • “Protect us”: Global reaction to the trilateral discussions is starting to emerge. In a stark statement, Poland’s Prime Minister Donald Tusk seemed to condemn Trump’s reference to his Chinese and Russian counterparts as “friends.” Tusk posted on X, “God, protect us from such friends.”

Why was Canada's PM in China instead of at today's Ukraine talks?

Canada is typically a prominent member of the Coalition of the Willing in support of Ukraine, which is holding meetings in Berlin today.

But while roughly 40 of Ukraine’s allies gather in Germany, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney is halfway across the world in China. That’s because Canada is also a member of APEC, or the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation bloc, which met in Shenzhen this week.

Carney also held a bilateral meeting with US President Donald Trump on the sidelines of APEC on Wednesday.

That means Carney, along with Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum, was one of the last world leaders to speak to Trump ahead of his unprecedented trilateral talks with strongmen Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin.

In the past, Western leaders – like Finland’s President Alexander Stubb – have made an effort to meet with Trump before big foreign policy moments. Notably, before last year’s US-Russia summit in Alaska, European leaders held a virtual summit with Trump to discuss the negotiations. But the Carney-Trump meeting on Wednesday might have had a different tone, given the recent tensions and trade war between their administrations.

On Thursday in Shenzhen, Carney also held meetings with the leaders of Australia, New Zealand and Singapore. He then departed for the Canadian capital, Ottawa.

US envoys Witkoff and Kushner arrive at Berlin's Chancellery ahead of talks

US special envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff have arrived at the Chancellery in Berlin for talks with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz ahead of a key “Coalition of the Willing” meeting in the German capital.

The “Coalition of the Willing” talks – co-hosted by Merz, French President Emmanuel Macron and UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham – are being attended by multiple European heads of state as well as NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte.

Witkoff and Kushner exited a US government SUV in the pouring rain and were ushered inside away from media representatives awaiting around 40 delegations from Ukraine, European and NATO countries, the EU and the NATO alliance.

Shortly after, Macron and Burnham arrived at the Chancellery.

They were followed by Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelensky and foreign intelligence chief and top negotiator Rustem Umerov.

This meeting was timed to coincide with the three-way summit of the Chinese, US and Russian presidents in Shenzhen, China, to be able to react to the events there.

It comes as European nations are looking to gain a seat at the table of US-Russian-Ukrainian diplomatic efforts to end the war in Ukraine.

Putin repeats false narratives about Russia's war on Ukraine

Knowing the world would be watching closely, Russia’s President Vladimir Putin used his news conference in Shenzen to repeat lies about the war in Ukraine.

The Russian leader falsely claimed it was not Moscow who started the conflict, which first began in 2014, when Russia illegally annexed Ukraine’s Crimean peninsula and provide backing and weapons to Pro-Russian separatists in eastern Ukraine.

Putin, who could end the war immediately by ordering the Russian military to stop attacking Ukraine, also said Kyiv and its ​European allies were “not yet agreeing to compromises regarding a settlement in Ukraine, but they might eventually come around to it.”

Russia’s long-standing demands include Ukraine giving up control over territories, reducing the size of its military and agreeing not to seek NATO membership in the future.

Asked about the issue on Thursday, Putin questioned whether there was “point in holding on those 10% of the territory,” alluding to the Donbas region in eastern Ukraine.

Putin added that Russia agreed to what he called “compromises” proposed by the US during his meeting with US President Donald Trump in Anchorage in Alaska. No agreement from that meeting was ever made public.

Asked about the comparison between the meeting on Thursday and the 1945 Yalta conference at which Soviet leader Joseph Stalin effectively redrew the global map of spheres of influence with the Allied powers, Putin said he didn’t think there was anything wrong with the agreements reached 80 years ago.

“Yalta solidified the victory over Nazism. It would be both appropriate and right to solidify a victory over neo-Nazism — over xenophobia, Russophobia, and the like,” Putin said, a nod to another of his false accusations that Ukraine’s President Volodymyr Zelensky and his government are neo-Nazis.

The Russian leader has also addressed the latest Ukrainian strikes agains the Kerch Bridge which connects Russia with Crimea, claiming the link served “purely peaceful purposes,” despite ample evidence the the Russian military uses the bridge as a key supply route.

He said Russia would retaliate for the strike.
