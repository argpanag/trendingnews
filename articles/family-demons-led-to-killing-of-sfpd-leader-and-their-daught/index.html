‘Family demons’ led to killing of SFPD leader and their daughter, says victim’s brother

TheTools · 16.10.2026 23:02 · source · permalink

Kevin Mannix’s troubled childhood — marked by orphanages, suicide, and violence — may hold clues to the tragedy.

The killing of retired San Francisco Police Deputy Chief Ann Mannix and her daughter, allegedly by her husband Kevin Mannix, who then turned the gun on himself, was the result of years of struggles with inner demons, according to Ann Mannix’s brother, retired San Francisco police Cmdr. Rich Corriea. 

“This behavior, while growing in intensity … was around for a long time,” Corriea told The Standard. “The demons are his family demons. The violence in his past. Kevin’s youth was devastating and damning to a whole generation of his siblings and mother.”

Corriea believes Kevin Mannix’s decades-long career with the San Francisco Police Department helped tamp down the impact that history had on his mental health.

Corriea described Kevin Mannix’s troubled youth. His father died when he was young, and his mother suffered from severe mental health issues. Mannix and his siblings were split up and sent to orphanages, Corriea said. 

Corriea said the family experienced further tragedy and violence. One of Mannix’s brothers died by suicide, and another was killed in a robbery. Another family member killed his wife, Corriea said.

Corriea, who knew Kevin Mannix for decades, said he struggled with that family legacy. 

“What he did was evil and the work of a monster, but I don’t think anybody can look over Kevin’s life and say he was a monster,” Corriea said. “What he did was monstrous. It was almost ordained that we would end up here.”

Sources say Mannix, 68, appears to have killed himself Sunday in the family home near Mill Valley after allegedly killing Ann, 65, and their 24-year-old daughter, Katherine. The couple had been experiencing marital problems.

Chief Derrick Lew sent a message to rank-and-file officers notifying them of the deaths before the Marin County Sheriff publicly identified the victims. No possible motive has been revealed.

The murder-suicide has rocked the department, and many officers have showered Ann Mannix with praise. Meanwhile, little light has been shed on Kevin Mannix or the divergent career the pair had. 

“Nobody would have seen this coming,” said retired Deputy Chief Michael Connolly. “It doesn’t make sense. Everybody is rocked.”

As tributes to Ann Mannix have been shared across social media and news articles, far less has been said about her husband’s 27 years on the force — or the starkly different paths the two took through SFPD.

Interviews with more than a dozen current and former officers who worked with both Mannixes paint a picture of a celebrated department leader who forged a path for women at the SFPD, while her reserved, good-natured husband spent much of his career outside the spotlight.

“He was always in the shadows of Ann. He was DC Mannix’s husband,” said a former SFPD officer.

Contrasting careers 

Ann Mannix was a sixth-generation San Franciscan raised in a large family with a long connection to police work.

Corriea said the day his parents brought his newborn sister home from the hospital in a red Buick runs like a movie reel in his head. The family watched as they cut off her name-tag bracelet. It’s his first memory.

On one of the many skiing trips the siblings went on, he accidentally passed over her skis and crashed. He recalled that as she stood over him, she said, “‘We’re done for the day. You don’t know how to ski,’” then slapped him with her pole and skied off.

The family was tested by the premature deaths of other relatives. In the late 1980s, one of Ann’s three brothers was murdered in Oklahoma. Another brother died of liver disease in 1992.

Ann Mannix joined the SFPD in 1986 and quickly moved up the ranks. Corriea said she was always more into police culture than he was. For instance, when he got a promotion, it was his sister who was enthusiastic about seeing him participate in the formal ceremony. She said ‘What, you’re not going to your own primary ceremony? Oh no, you’re going, you’re absolutely going. I’m gonna come pick you up.’ And we went.”

She was promoted to sergeant in 1996 and lieutenant in 2003. She became a captain in 2009, commanding Northern Station, and was the first woman to lead the SFPD’s SWAT team. 

In 2015, she was promoted to commander. In 2018, Chief Bill Scott tapped her as deputy chief of the Field Operations Bureau, a position she held until her retirement in 2021, after 35 years with the department. 

“She always struck me as sort of formidable, but in a positive way,” said Catherine McGuire, who until recently headed the Strategic Management Bureau. “She was just sort of quietly strong.”

When John Alden, a former prosecutor, first saw Ann Mannix in action, he was impressed as she went head-to-head with the mostly male officers at a department meeting. 

“Man, she’s standing up to all these dudes in the room. That’s pretty cool,” recalled Alden, who stayed after the meeting and saw Mannix turn to an officer with whom she had been arguing vigorously. It turned out to be her brother Corriea.

“Am I gonna see you at mom’s for dinner, Rich?” Alden recalled her saying.

‘Background Mannix’

Kevin Mannix grew up in Chicago and attended Loyola Marymount University, where he met Ann Mannix. 

After a stint as a sheriff’s deputy, he joined the SFPD in 1991. 

Retired Commander Steve Ford, who worked with him in background investigations of new hires in the late 1990s, said Mannix was smart and likable.

Another officer said Mannix had been tasked with conducting background investigations for so long that his nickname was “Background Mannix.” 

But he did get into some tough spots while on patrol. At Northern Station in the early 2000s, he was seriously injured while on duty, according to several officers who knew him.

In 2009, he was promoted to sergeant at Southern Station. The next year, he was transferred to Taraval Station, where he worked until his 2018 retirement.

One officer remembered him as “super nice, a funny guy. I don’t think I ever saw him in a bad mood.”

Before his retirement, he was evaluated by the department for mental fitness for duty because of memory issues, according to a former officer.

During his last posting as a sergeant at Taraval Station, colleagues and subordinates complained that Mannix would periodically forget what he had said or contradict previous orders, which led to the review of his mental fitness, according to one former officer.

The review found that Mannix was fit for duty. After his retirement, he worked part time for the SFPD conducting background checks until September, when he was transferred to the property division, according to the department.

Ford, who considered Ann Mannix a mentor, said he spoke to Kevin Mannix about six months ago. “He seemed fine,” Ford said. “We had a fun-loving relationship.”

‘Quirky’ and ‘insecure’

Not all the officers who worked with Kevin Mannix held him in high regard, especially in comparison with his high-flying wife.

Former officer Harry Soulette, who played intramural basketball with Ann Mannix, described the pair as an “odd couple.” 

He said Ann Mannix was an approachable leader whose door was always open. Her husband, he said, seemed insecure and possessive.

“I got me an amazing lady. Nobody’s taking her from me,” Soulette recalled Kevin Mannix saying. At the time, Soulette thought the remark reflected how fortunate Mannix felt to be married to her. In retrospect, he said, it struck him as possessive.

Corriea, however, said he did not recall his brother-in-law as possessive. 

Retired acting Capt. Yulanda Williams, who was promoted by Ann Mannix, praised her former boss for championing women in the department. 

Williams also worked with Kevin Mannix for a time. “He was different; he was very unique. He was a workaholic, and he was obsessed with talking about money,” she said. “He was not a leader. He was a follower.”

For Williams, the lasting memory was the example Ann Mannix set during 35 years in the department.

“Ann was a trailblazer, and it’s just unfortunate that her life had to end this way, because she inspired so many of us to believe that we could climb up in the ranks,” she said.

Corriea said he will always remember her accomplishments, not how her life ended. 

“In a funny way I don’t like using the word ‘proud,’ but I was delighted to be her brother,” he said. “She had a swagger. She had a finesse-filled swagger.”

A memorial is planned for Oct. 23 at St. Ignatius Church.
