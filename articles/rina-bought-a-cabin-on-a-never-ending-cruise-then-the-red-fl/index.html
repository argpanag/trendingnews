Rina bought a cabin on a never-ending cruise. Then the red flags began.

TheTools · 09.11.2026 05:24 · source · permalink

Rina Cavazza stepped off her floating home in October with just a backpack.

The Villa Vie Odyssey, a residential cruise that offered long-term stays up to 15 years, had a long run of sea days ahead and ports she'd already explored.

So the Brisbane mum opted to spend three weeks in Chiang Mai, Thailand, learning massage.

The plan was simple: take a course and rejoin the ship in Bangkok on November 2. October 27 was her last day of learning.

That day, the ship's operator announced the Odyssey was suspending operations due to "extraordinary fuel costs".

The "never-ending" cruise had come to a grinding halt.

Those on board heard the announcement: the ship was stopping in Cambodia and they had just seven hours to get off.

Cavazza had to get herself to the port of Sihanoukville to pack up her cabin. When she arrived, the place she'd called home for two years was unrecognisable.

"You normally would walk through and you find people in the business centre, or sitting in the hallway reading a book, or having a drink in the bar area. It's just dead. No one, nowhere," she told Mamamia.

"It was Halloween and it was a ghost ship."

Cavazza had been burned before. In 2023, she signed up for Life at Sea, another residential cruise, which collapsed while she was already overseas waiting to board.

But when Villa Vie Residences began advertising the Odyssey, she signed on anyway.

For her, the maths made sense.

"It works out cheaper than living at home, where you have to still pay all your outgoings: your phone, your electricity, your insurances, your fuel, your groceries. And this covered everything," she said.

Wi-Fi, meals, laundry, housekeeping and travel from port to port were all included.

"Think of it like a holiday home. You just come and go as you please… it's just a moving holiday," Cavazza said.

She visited Machu Picchu, peeled off for a two-week Antarctic expedition before rejoining the ship in Rio, and squeezed in side trips to Kuala Lumpur.

But it was the people who made it home.

When she rejoined the ship in Sydney last year after four-and-half months away, even the security guard opened his arms to greet her at the top of the gangway.

"My plan was to at least do the first circumnavigation around the world and then look at selling my cabin. So I had another at least 18 months to go," she said.

Instead, she's back home in Australia. And the cabin she paid $170,000 for is on a ship that's stopped sailing.

A dream escape.

It was meant to be a holiday that never ended, a continuous voyage around the world.

A Cambodian official put the numbers at 203 passengers and 156 crew, NBC News reported.

Billed as "an intimate residential cruise ship offering a new way to live", Villa Vie's Odyssey's first circumnavigation was expected to span 3.5 years and cover nearly 150 countries, according to its website.

While by no means cheap, the cruise was sold as an affordable way to experience residential cruising.

Permanent cabins cost US$120,000–$440,000 USD, or rentals from US$3,500 a month, with monthly fees on top.

Residents had access to a pool, pickleball court, theatre, bars and a gym.

It had a rocky start.

The departure was delayed as the ship underwent repairs, leaving passengers stuck in Belfast for months and costing the company millions.

In late September 2024, four months behind schedule, the Odyssey finally left Belfast for its first scheduled stop in northern France.

Between late 2024 and early 2025, it toured Western Europe, Northwest Africa and the Caribbean, before passing through the Panama Canal to South America.

It was more than 750 days into its circumnavigation when it stopped.

How it fell apart.

Internal communications, seen by CBS News, showed the ship was in financial distress for at least three months prior to Cambodia.

Cavazza said the cracks were showing long before that.

"We used to have a piano player, a band, entertainment managers, destination managers. And then slowly but surely, one by one, they started disappearing," she said.

"Costs started being cut down, but no one was fazed by it. We were still living the dream."

Then fuel prices soared.

From April, Cavazza said residents were repeatedly asked to chip in for fuel. By Rina's count, it happened three times.

Melbourne retiree Frances Paroissien told 7NEWS residents were told in August the company owed millions to a bank and other creditors.

She said passengers contributed more than US$2 million to help the ship leave Singapore, but later refused another request for money because they didn't know where the money would go.

"We were worried that the money that we had contributed to fuel and to stabilise the business was going in this fairyland of becoming multiple ship owners," she said.

The final ask came days before the shutdown, after a deal with a potential investor reportedly fell through.

Residents were asked for US$1,800 per occupied cabin to keep the ship moving, Rina said.

"Some people paid, but not everyone," Rina said. "They were like, 'No, we're done. We're not paying another $1,800 each just to get us to the next port.' And then what?"

On the Monday, residents were warned that if the money didn't come through, everyone would have to get off. Few took it seriously.

"We all know the worst-case scenario… we've all heard it before, but we always get through," Cavazza said.

The final day.

Early on Tuesday, the announcement came.

Resident Randy Cassingham, who runs ResidentialCruising.com, wrote in a blog post that a note on the ship's message board said The Odyssey was entering a "temporary lay up" period and everyone had to disembark by 2pm.

The company organised shuttles to take residents into town.

Residents say that's where the help ended.

"We were given seven hours notice to disembark, and the ONLY help we have received was from our loving crew and fellow residents in getting our luggage down the gangway and onto a shuttle," resident Sherrie Cornnett, from Arizona, wrote on Facebook.

"We were dropped unceremoniously in the middle of town, left to our own devices."

Cassingham shared a similar story.

"They didn't even give us a list of hotels, let alone help us get there or pay for them," he wrote on his blog that day.

"They do have to provide shuttles between the ship and town as we are not allowed to walk around inside the port area (it's an active cargo port), but that is all… they did not even give us a list of hotels to choose from. They have absolutely not offered to pay for that lodging, full stop."

Cassingham and Cornett said it took 36 hours before the company posted hotel suggestions, a vague offer of financial assistance and a possible charter flight to the US at US$1,100 a head.

"By which time we had all already found hotels," Cornett wrote. "Way to go, VVR! Right on top of things!!"

As for what they've heard from Villa Vie since?

"Crickets," Cavazza said.

The human cost.

Melbourne retiree Frances Paroissien had already lost $85,000 on the cancelled Life at Sea venture before buying a cabin for over $200,000 on the Odyssey under a 15-year lease. She spent just 25 months aboard.

"I am angry," she told 7NEWS. "I am cheesed off that action didn't happen earlier so that this could be avoided."

She said she sold her house and her business to buy her place on the ship. With nowhere to return to in Melbourne, she's leased an apartment in Thailand for six months.

"I'm not going home because I don't have a home to go to," she said.

Cassingham and his wife are in the same position.

"The ship was our home," he wrote on his blog.

"We sold our house, our cars, and got rid of virtually all of our belongings to live there.

"We are effectively homeless."

Cavazza said she feels the most for the older residents who bet everything on the promise of 15 years at sea.

"A lot of people have sold their houses and all possessions, because this was to run for at least 15 years guaranteed, not two," she said.

"A lot of the elderly were like, 'Well, I'm 78,' or 'I'm 82, I'm not going to last another 15 years, so this is where I'm going to finish off.' And so they put everything into it. Everything."

Cavazza recalled one resident who sold everything and paid US$300,000 to join in August, just two months before the collapse.

"She's only been on board for not even three months… That's like US$100,000 a month."

What they've lost isn't just money.

"The community is what sold the ship," Cavazza said. "It wasn't a luxury ship… it's the people that really made it, and the crew."

She considers herself one of the lucky ones. She didn't sink her life savings or her home into the Odyssey, but her plan to sell her cabin has gone with it.

"For what I've paid and what I've travelled, [my daily cost] has pretty much doubled," she said.

The company line.

In its October 27 statement announcing it was suspending operations, Villa Vie Residences blamed rising fuel costs.

Global fuel prices have skyrocketed since the Iran war began earlier this year.

The company said monthly fuel expenses per cabin had risen from US$1,800 to as much as US$8,000 in parts of Asia.

"Since the conflict began in late February, we have taken significant steps to reduce fuel consumption, including slowing the itinerary and spending more time in port," Villa Vie Residences said.

"Those measures have not been enough to offset the extraordinary increase in fuel costs, and we have been unable to continue carrying the resulting operating losses. Passing the full increase on to residents is not a realistic solution."

It said its focus was on supporting residents, crew and employees while it spends the next 60 days in "restructuring and recapitalisation", though "no outcome is assured".

Villa Vie's founder and executive chairman Mikael Petterson told Business Insider: "I feel awful."

"I'm the villain in the story, but I'm doing everything I can to support the residents on a solution," he said.

Petterson said Villa Vie didn't have the scale to absorb higher fuel prices like larger cruise lines can.

"They're able to hedge and take away some of that risk, but we're too small of a company to do that," he said.

Until last week, he'd been in talks with a potential investor for a much-needed cash injection, he claimed.

When that deal fell apart, the company was out of options, leaving him considering bankruptcy, he told Business Insider.

Petterson told CBS News the company was owned by employees and residents, "so, when there is a financial need, residents are by definition the shareholders of the company and are naturally asked as shareholders to contribute."

He told Bloomberg the company was covering lodging and local transportation, but hadn't funded passengers to leave Cambodia.

Residents tell a different story.

"They are providing ZERO support to residents despite claiming they're helping us with temporary living arrangements. That is total BS," Sherri said.

Many don't accept fuel as the full explanation.

"The owners of Villa Vie have been giving reports about the fact that it was the fuel that put us in this precarious position. I don't believe that," Paroissien told Khaosod English. "They're very nice people; they've worked in the cruise industry before, but they're not business people."

Cavazza also pointed to the business model.

Residents' monthly fees were fixed for the life of their contracts, so the company had no way to raise them as costs climbed.

"They had a great concept, great idea, but their finance management, I don't think was the best," she claimed.

One option, Endless Horizons, let residents pay a one-off US$300,000 to live aboard for the rest of their lives, with no fees ever again.

"They did undercut themselves a lot," Cavazza claimed. "I guess they were just looking at getting people through the door, but for longevity, it wasn't the right thing."

What's next?

The ship is now under the control of its creditor, according to a message sent to residents and obtained by CBS News.

"The lender has taken possession of the ship, but that does not mean the Odyssey's future has been decided. No sale has been scheduled. We believe there is time to bring forward a viable plan and are working to do that," it reportedly said.

CBS reports the ship could eventually be auctioned off, according to multiple sources familiar with the matter.

Petterson told CBS he's optimistic they can get the ship back if they find an operational partner.

"They have now the responsibility to pay the crew, fuel and the operations of the vessel and safekeeping of the vessel," he told the outlet, stressing that they're exploring options to keep the ship going.

"They would be willing to work with a funded plan — they're not out of negotiations — it's not like you get your car repossessed and you can't get it back."

The ship left Cambodia on Wednesday with no passengers and about 50 crew members, Tang Sochetkresna, head of the Sihanoukville tourism department, told NBC.

As of Thursday, Villa Vie Residences has yet to confirm where the ship is heading. However, some passengers and maritime tracking websites indicated the ship was bound for Singapore.

Some residents are waiting it out in Cambodia or Thailand in case they can return. Others are planning trips together, and some have no plans to go home at all.

The Cambodian government has stepped up, with the tourism minister helping with transport, luggage storage and shipping.

"Cambodia has just welcomed us. They've given us free tours, free dinners… They've just gone above and beyond," Cavazza said.

She's going to try her travel insurance, and is hoping a buyer for the ship might still mean someone buys her cabin, "even if it's for pennies on the dollar".

If the Odyssey ever sails again, she'd go back as a renter, not an owner. But for now, she said, "I'm out."

First, she's home for her granddaughter's christening. After that, the reformer instructor plans to keep travelling and working. Former shipmates have already offered her rooms in Miami, Orlando, Mexico and Hawaii.

"I'm definitely not going to just come home and lie in a corner," she said.

"Like a car wreck, the car's replaceable, so money's replaceable, but people aren't. So I'm going to keep going and see where life takes me."
