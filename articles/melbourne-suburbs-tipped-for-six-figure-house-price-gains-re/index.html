Melbourne suburbs tipped for six-figure house price gains - realestate.com.au

TheTools · 17.10.2026 22:03 · source · permalink

Melbourne could have 372 suburbs with typical house values above $1m by 2036, almost double today’s 188, if historical growth patterns repeat, according to realestate.com.au

Homeowners in 69 Melbourne suburbs could see six-figure house-value gains by 2031, with cheaper neighbourhoods leading the charge if history repeats.

New realestate.com.au modelling puts five-year growth in those suburbs at 25 per cent or more.

Langwarrin tops the rankings, with its typical $875,000 house potentially reaching $1.14m, a gain of almost $269,000.

But million-dollar house suburbs could almost double, from 188 in 2026 to 372 by 2036.

Only 23 of Melbourne’s 395 analysed house markets would remain below $1m under that scenario.

Eight of the top 10 have typical values below $1m, with Frankston North and Mickleham following Langwarrin.

Under the separate 10-year scenario, 285 suburbs would gain at least 50 per cent.

Langwarrin leads Melbourne’s modelled five-year house-price growth rankings, with its typical $875,000 house potentially reaching $1.14m by 2031, a gain of almost $269,000.

Mickleham’s typical $683,000 house could reach $884,000, while Rockbank’s $616,000 benchmark could climb to $793,000.

Balnarring, Coburg North, Baxter, Doveton, Narre Warren North and Cannons Creek complete the top 10, ranked by annual percentage growth.

Unlike houses, none of Melbourne’s 271 analysed unit markets would grow 25 per cent over five years under the modelling.

Realestate.com.au senior economist Angus Moore said Melbourne’s relative affordability could attract buyers, but cautioned the historical-growth modelling was not a house-price forecast.

Realestate.com.au senior economist Angus Moore cautioned the figures illustrated historical trends, rather than predicting where prices would actually land.

He said Melbourne’s prolonged slump had left home values near mid-2021 levels, making the city comparatively affordable against other capitals.

“Over the next sort of five or 10 years, that is going to increasingly look attractive for buyers,” Mr Moore said.

This Frankston North house sold for $611,000, with the suburb ranked among Melbourne’s 10 strongest potential growth markets under REA Group’s five-year modelling.

Interstate migration was recovering after years of weakness, with affordability an important attraction.

Mr Moore expected at least one further interest rate rise, with a more sustained recovery dependent on inflation and borrowing costs stabilising.

“Once rates start to stabilise … that will start to get buyers a bit more confidence,” he said.

Over a decade, household incomes, population growth and housing construction would matter more than tax settings, particularly as rising costs threatened new developments.

M R Advocacy founder Madeleine Roberts said she had secured stand-alone houses in Langwarrin for about $800,000, below the suburb’s typical $875,000 value.

M R Advocacy founder and lead buyer’s agent Madeleine Roberts said buyers were finding value amid Melbourne’s market weakness.

She had seen $300,000 to $400,000 discounts on some homes valued around $2m compared with last year, while buyer inquiries were increasing.

In Langwarrin, Ms Roberts had secured stand-alone houses for about $800,000 over the past six months, despite higher typical values.

“People will have regret in 12 months’ time when they look back and see this opportunity that was in front of them,” she said.

This Mickleham home sold for $730,000, while REA Group modelling suggests the suburb’s typical $683,000 house could reach $884,000 over five years.

Interstate investors were also weighing purchases before the November election.

“I’ve had people wanting to buy before the state election,” Ms Roberts said.

OBrien Real Estate Frankston’s Mark Burke said doctors, nurses, tradies and young families were increasingly buying in Frankston North.

The 40-year veteran accused Labor of discouraging investment through property taxes and rental regulations.

“They are strangulating the industry, absolutely strangulating,” Mr Burke said.

Alba founder Tom Mifsud blamed government decisions for weakening Melbourne property investor confidence and questioned how political parties would fund their housing promises.

Alba founder Tom Mifsud also blamed government decisions for much of Melbourne’s prolonged underperformance, arguing the political environment had damaged investor confidence.

“We’ve always copped our shit from Labor,” Mr Mifsud said.

High land, construction and finance costs were making once-attractive developments financially unviable, Mr Mifsud argued.

“If the numbers don’t work, the numbers don’t work,” he said.

Eight of Melbourne’s 10 strongest modelled house-price growth suburbs have typical values below $1m, highlighting the potential of more affordable markets.

He also condemned Premier Ben Carroll’s pledge to reverse land tax changes introduced under former premier Daniel Andrews.

“It’s reactionary. It’s desperation. It’s not thoughtful, it’s not authentic, and frankly, it’s disingenuous,” he said.

Victoria lowered its general land tax-free threshold from $300,000 to $50,000 in 2024.

Labor has promised to restore the threshold over six years if re-elected, while Jess Wilson’s Coalition proposes doing so over five.

Premier Ben Carroll’s Labor government has pledged to restore Victoria’s land tax-free threshold from $50,000 to $300,000 over six years if re-elected. Picture: NewsWire/Ian Currie

The Opposition Leader also wants to abolish stamp duty for eligible first-home buyers purchasing properties valued up to $1m.

Victoria currently exempts eligible purchases up to $600,000, with partial concessions available up to $750,000.

Mr Mifsud backed the proposed relief but questioned how lost revenue would be replaced, and doubted the land tax timetable difference would transform demand.

“For the typical investor? Very little,” he said.

Opposition Leader Jess Wilson wants Victoria’s $300,000 land tax-free threshold restored over five years, a year faster than Labor’s proposal. Picture: Facebook

Mr Mifsud challenged all parties to explain how they would fund housing promises without increasing Victoria’s financial burden.

“Show us the numbers,” he said.

Labor says reversing its threshold changes would eventually remove more than 250,000 people from the land tax system.

Labor also announced developer concessions to encourage construction.

The Victorian government was contacted for comment.
