But before that begins in earnest, one of the lineups that resonates most with Canadian hockey fans is becoming one of the most discussed in the sport.

The buzz around Canadian NHL circles these days is what will happen with one of the most prominent hockey insiders, Elliotte Friedman, and his Rogers hockey studio analyst sidekick, Kevin Bieksa?

Zooming out, how different will Canadian hockey viewing be as a landmark, 12-year, $11 billion rights deal for the telecommunications behemoth kicks in this season?

Zooming in, both Friedman and Bieksa are reported to be free agents, with contracts that apparently expired on Aug. 31. While it could simply be part of the negotiating process, such uncertainty this close to the season is at the least intriguing.

Spokespersons for both Rogers and Sportsnet did not immediately return messages asking about the status of the two most important voices on the network’s hockey panels, a development that is creating all manner of chatter throughout the hockey world.

Both would also appear critical to Rogers and Sportsnet moving forward in a rapidly changing broadcast universe, not to mention the dominance the company that employs them has on the Canadian sports landscape.

The uncertainty may ultimately turn out to be nothing, part of the regular negotiating that goes on in the industry all the time. In that regard, the status of one or both could all change in an instant.

Friedman, we’re told, is still negotiating the specifics of his future while taking some welcome downtime before  the season.

On the other hand, losing one or both of those important voices could result in a dramatic implosion of the way Canadians watch Saturday night hockey, as revered a broadcast institution as there is in this country.

What is of broader and considerable interest to industry observers is how Rogers will reshape its coverage as it launches the most expensive sports rights deal in Canadian broadcast history.

With its controlling ownership interest in MLSE, a record payroll with the Blue Jays and this massive rights deal with the NHL expenses have never been higher. Already this summer, we’ve seen Rogers shutter radio stations in markets across the country.

As for broadcast plans, will Rogers skimp on hockey or will it go all out like it does on Blue Jays coverage where for home games there are often a dozen or more content gatherers for the communication giant’s many platforms.

If cutting edge insider information remains important to Rogers, it can’t afford to let Friedman go.

If refreshing, incisive commentary is important, the same can be said about Bieksa, unless Rogers can find a replacement with similar talent.

However it shakes down, the closer we get to NHL training camps opening, the more intriguing the for now blurry broadcast picture becomes.

What’s changing with Rogers hockey?

Likely more than we already know. The company officially begins its 12-year, $11 billion deal with the 2026-27 season and does so in a rapidly changing sports broadcast universe.

Personnel wise, the first mic to drop was that employed by former NHL goalie Kelly Hrudey, who revealed earlier in the summer that he was being let go by Rogers.

Of course, that move triggered speculation as to who else may be gone – some of it far-fetched, much of it pause for thought.

There have been rumours this week that recent Hockey Hall of Fame inductee Jennifer Botterill, will no longer be on the Saturday panel. That has yet to be confirmed and worth nothing that Botterill has worked for TNT as part of that U.S. network’s dynamic approach to hockey coverage.

As always, there has been a loud constituency bellowing for the removal of Ron MacLean as Sportsnet’s hockey host, more an emotional cry than a practical one. And for now, anyway, it doesn’t appear as though MacLean is going anywhere.

Also, now that CBC has severed its association with Sportsnet in conjunction with the new Rogers deal, there is no longer any Hockey Night In Canada branding associated with live NHL coverage.

With regards to streaming, Rogers announced in July that it has a sub-licensing agreement with Prime Video for exclusive coverage of Wednesday night games, a deal that extends through all 12 years of Rogers NHL contract.

That means as many as nine Leafs games will be Prime only.

Why is Elliotte Friedman so important?

In hockey especially, among the currency most highly valued by viewers and fans is that of the insider and Friedman is one of the best in the business with breaking news.

As connected as any of his contemporaries, Friedman has arguably become the most important personality on Rogers hockey coverage, a status that naturally gives him some negotiating power.

Like most broadcasters, Friedman has an agent and negotiating is part of the process. Deadlines pass and talks heat up and yes, there’s still plenty of time to get a deal done.

Would Friedman land at TSN if Rogers let’s him go? While nothing is impossible, it seems like a stretch given that the network is already loaded with superb insider talent in Pierre Lebrun, Chris Johnston and Darren Dreger. More likely, something gets done to keep one of the best in the business at the Canadian rights holder.

Need for a change?

At the Milan Olympics, Canadian hockey fans were treated to what hockey broadcasts can be. The hybrid of TSN and Sportsnet personalities showed the value of chemistry as a virtual all-star team of talking heads informed and entertained throughout the NHL’s long-awaited return to the Olympics.

Likewise, U.S. audiences have been treated to some fresh work from panels on TNT, most noteworthy social media star Paul Bisonnette, who has had some entertaining cameos on Sportsnet.

Bieksa has the ability to hold similar star power with the right colleagues and exposure. At the least, the Rogers approach to hockey coverage could use a freshening, but are far bigger changes on the horizon?
