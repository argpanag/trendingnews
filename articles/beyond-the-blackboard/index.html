Restoring the role and dignity of teachers in Kashmir

As Teachers’ Day arrives, we often remind ourselves of the age-old saying that a teacher is the spiritual second guide of a community. This maxim signifies the supreme importance of the educator. But a sobering question must be asked: Do we truly have those teachers available in our classrooms today?

If we look closely at regions like Kashmir, we must confront whether our educators genuinely possess that historic, venerated status. This is not an indictment of teachers, but an honest look at a reality that has drastically shifted. We are witnessing a gradual erosion of respect for teachers due to various systemic, cultural, and behavioral reasons. This loss of societal reverence is a matter of grave concern because every other profession, doctors, engineers, scientists, and leaders, is born from the hands of a teacher. To build a great society, we must first build great teachers. And great teachers are not merely products of academia with high degrees; they are individuals of high moral and spiritual standards. 

In contemporary times, the teaching profession faces a multifaceted crisis of dignity. Several factors contribute to this decline, particularly in societies transitioning through rapid social changes. Chief among them is the commercialization of education, which has increasingly transformed schools into corporate-style businesses rather than sacred missions. When institutions operate like factories and coaching centers prioritize profit over mentorship, the emotional bond between teacher and student is broken. 

Furthermore, we see extremes of discipline and freedom, where respect has been damaged on both ends. In some spaces, excessive academic freedoms and a lack of institutional boundaries give license to student indiscipline, while in others, rigid authoritarian styles devoid of empathy alienate young minds. Finally, the erosion of moral anchors plays a heavy role. True teaching is about shaping human emotions, behaviours, and souls, but when the selection of educators relies solely on paper qualifications rather than innate vocation, passion, and moral character, the classroom loses its spiritual guide.

Redefining the teacher means recognising that they do not run human machinery, nor do they manage factories or program robotics. They deal with human beings—living souls filled with complex emotions, behaviours, and vulnerabilities. 

These minds need someone who can teach them humanity, morality, and behaviorism far beyond textbooks and machines. True educators act as surrogate fathers and mothers; they feel the human pulse in a child, see them from top to bottom, and recognize their individual struggles, functioning as modern-day saints who nourish young minds with ethics. As students, when we look back at our true mentors, we realize the profound truth of the metaphor: “Oh teachers, you are role models everywhere, in every place, and at all times. Your talk is our advice, your dress is our costume, your face is our mirror, and your character is our faith.” When that mirror cracks or that character loses its moral compass, the entire society falters.

To revive the glory of the teaching profession, especially in culturally rich yet regions like Kashmir, specific steps must be taken immediately. First, we must re-establish accountability and merit so that schools and educators are held responsible to their sacred trust, ensuring that employment filters for candidates with psychological aptitude and empathy rather than treating teaching as a mere fallback career option. 

Second, we must boost morale and human knowledge by investing in continuous development programs, equipping educators with emotional intelligence to understand the changing pressures faced by modern youth. Third, society must actively work to restore student reverence by partnering with schools, upholding teacher authority, and ensuring that educators maintain transparency, fairness, and unconditional care.

Ultimately, celebrating Teachers’ Day should no longer be confined to token ceremonies, flower bouquets, or social media statuses. It must serve as a collective awakening. If we want a prosperous, peaceful, and morally grounded society in Kashmir and beyond, we must return the teacher to their rightful pedestal, not as a mechanical distributor of notes, but as the true moral anchor of human civilisation.
