DGRO's Dividend Only Gets the Lower Tax Rate If You Hold It 61 Days Inside a 121-Day Window

TheTools · 16.10.2026 23:03 · source · permalink

One IRS sentence buried in Publication 550 decides whether your DGRO dividends get taxed at the lower rate or treated as ordinary income, and most traders never see the trap until they get their tax form.

The ETF Examiner desk. Editor: Ryne Mauck.

Every quarter, iShares Core Dividend Growth ETF (NYSEARCA:DGRO) pays its holders a dividend. Whether that money gets the lower rate schedule for qualified dividends or is taxed at ordinary income rates depends on one sentence in IRS Publication 550: “You must have held the stock for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date.” In practice, you need at least 61 days inside that window. The publication’s own worked example ends with this line: “You have no qualified dividends from XYZ Corp. for 2025 because you held the XYZ stock for less than 61 days.”

What You’re Actually Paying

The fund’s fee is small. DGRO’s net and gross expense ratios are both 0.08%, according to the prospectus dated August 31, 2026. That works out to $8 a year for every $10,000 invested. The bigger leak is the tax label.

If you fail the holding test, the same dividend moves from the qualified schedule to ordinary income rates. Compare your own two rates. The difference between them, applied to each payment, is the cost the factsheet never shows you.

How the 121-Day Window Decides Your Rate

Think of the window as a fixed frame. It opens 60 days before the ex-dividend date and runs for a 121-day stretch. The frame stays put no matter when you bought. Only the days you owned shares inside it count. Publication 550 sets the counting rule: “When counting the number of days you held the stock, include the day you disposed of the stock, but not the day you acquired it.”

Your buy date sets where your count starts, and your sell date sets where it ends. Here is how that plays out:

  • Long-term holder: you owned shares well before the frame opened and kept them well past its close. You clear the test easily.
  • Late buyer: you bought inside the frame just before the ex-dividend date. Your count started late, so most of your required days have to come after the ex-date, and your sale has to come late enough to supply them.
  • Round-tripper: you bought and sold around the ex-dividend date within a short period. The two dates together leave you short of the threshold.

In each case, the combination of both dates determines the outcome. Hedges can also shrink your count. Publication 550 says “you cannot count any day” on which you held an option to sell or had otherwise reduced your risk of loss on substantially identical shares.

DGRO Opens Four Windows a Year

DGRO pays quarterly, so every payment has its own window. The latest dividend was $0.384996 per share, with an ex-dividend date of September 15, 2026. Trailing twelve-month distributions total $1.493698 per share, and the annualized forward rate is $1.539984. Rotating in and out around each ex-dividend date can cause a trader to fail the test on every payment. That would put a full year of distributions at ordinary income rates.

SCHD and VIG Run on the Same Clock

The Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF (NYSEARCA:SCHD) and Vanguard Dividend Appreciation ETF (NYSEARCA:VIG) offer similar dividend-growth exposure. Their price returns vary. Over the year through October 15, 2026, SCHD returned 31.78%, DGRO 17.68%, and VIG 13.33%. Over ten years, DGRO leads at 265.94%, ahead of VIG at 255.92% and SCHD at 245.75%. The same holding test applies to dividends from all three funds. Switching tickers leaves the tax problem in place. Your holding period is what closes the gap.

Who Needs to Track This

Buy-and-hold investors can clear this test without trying. Dividend-capture traders, swing traders, anyone hedging with options, and anyone who sells within weeks of buying need to track it on every payment. If you fit that second group, check both your purchase date and your sale date against each ex-dividend date before you trust the qualified label on your tax form.
