George Fenton, composer of the Newsnight and Bergerac theme tunes and five-time Oscar nominee

TheTools · 10.10.2026 06:53 · source · permalink

He wrote the music for films ranging from Gandhi to Groundhog Day, and for the television series The Blue Planet and The Jewel in the Crown

George Fenton, the composer, who has died aged 76, made his name writing television news jingles before earning Oscar nominations for the scores to feature films such as Gandhi (1981), Cry Freedom (1987) and Dangerous Liaisons (1988).

Although Fenton wrote the music for more than 100 films, he accepted with good grace that his most enduring composition was the resonant theme for BBC Two’s nightly current affairs round-up Newsnight. It was a commission from Fenton’s brother-in-law, George Carey, who was Newsnight’s founding editor; launching the first edition in 1980, the distinctive result, with its rock-inflected guitar riffs, is still in use today.

Having produced theatre and television scores throughout the 1970s, Fenton became a go-to composer for feature films following the success of his score for Richard Attenborough’s Gandhi in 1981. Attenborough’s son Michael, then joint artistic director at the Royal Shakespeare Company, had been impressed by Fenton’s work for the stage and suggested he send his father a sample tape. Three weeks later Fenton was on a plane to Bombay.

Unusually versatile, he drew on a wide variety of musical themes and traditions. He moved easily between the evocative electronic soundscapes of The Company of Wolves (1984) and full orchestral scores, sometimes coloured by unorthodox instrumentation, an example being his original use of viols and sackbuts for The Crucible (1996).

He successfully merged Western techniques with Indian textures in both Gandhi (scored in collaboration with Ravi Shankar) and his subtle, haunting score for ITV’s The Jewel in the Crown (1984), as well as celebrating African music in Cry Freedom, in a score co-written with Jonas Gwangwa. When the occasion demanded, Fenton made effective use of folksong, jazz, rock, classical and Baroque elements, while in his television themes he proved a talented exponent of minimalist techniques.

The title scores for two television detective series proved particularly memorable: the harmonica-led theme for Shoestring (1979-80) and the instantly hummable Bergerac (1981-91) tune. More portentous was his 1988 opening music for the BBC’s Nine O’Clock News, a 12-second, 10-beat curtain-raiser which replaced one that he had composed for the programme’s relaunch only three years earlier.

Fenton’s one venture into radio was his opening jingle for Radio 4’s drivetime news magazine PM. Some listeners found it incongruously jaunty and it was eventually dropped in 1997 following the death of Diana, Princess of Wales.

In 2001 Fenton’s incidental music accompanying the BBC’s natural history television epic The Blue Planet won him further accolades, among them a Bafta for best original television music. Later he wrote the majestic score for Planet Earth (2006). Both series were narrated by Sir David Attenborough, and in scoring them Fenton would treat Attenborough’s narration like the main character in a drama, working the music around the inflections of his voice.

“His inflection is so precise, so deft, that it’s like having a singer, because it tells me exactly what the juxtaposition of his line reading and the image is, so I can inflect in a more subtle way,” he explained. “The only dramatic arc in the story comes from his voice and the music.”

Fenton was also, for half a century, the favoured composer of Alan Bennett, starting with the music for a series of television plays by Bennett for LWT (1978-79), and another cycle of plays, Objects of Affection, in 1982. He went on to write the score for Bennett’s television film An Englishman Abroad (1983), as well as for the two highly acclaimed Talking Heads series in 1988 and 1998.

He scored one of Bennett’s short memoirs for string quartet under the title Hymn (in 2001), and the film The Lady In The Van (in 2015), starring Dame Maggie Smith as the eccentric Mary Shepherd, who had lived in a van on Bennett’s drive for 15 years.

Fenton was the only person on the production, apart from Bennett himself, to have known Miss Shepherd. He remembered her from his teenage years when he had redecorated Bennett’s house in north London while working as a young actor, recalling her as strange, sad and rather fierce.

Bennett regarded Fenton as central to the success of The Lady of the Van, noting in his recent diary collection Enough Said: “His music, and the placing of the music, makes the film.”

He was born George Richard Ian Howe on October 19 1949 in Bromley, south London, the son of Captain Dick Howe MC, an engineer and former PoW who had organised several successful escapes from Colditz, and his wife Gwenda. Fenton was his mother’s maiden name.

George was educated at St Edward’s School in Oxford, where he received his only formal musical training, learning the guitar and church organ. As a boy he had pondered careers in acting and professional football, but on leaving school he worked in a London off-licence as a cellarman while daydreaming of a career in rock ‘n’ roll and never planning to settle in a regular job.

In 1968, after his mother pointed out an advertisement in The Daily Telegraph headed “Boys Wanted”, he attended an audition at Her Majesty’s Theatre. Somewhat to his surprise, he was cast as head boy of a prep school in Alan Bennett’s first stage play Forty Years On (Apollo, 1968), an engagement that lasted for 14 months and launched a lifelong collaboration. The music for the play was provided by Carl Davis, who encouraged Fenton’s burgeoning interest in composing for the theatre.

Fenton took further acting roles in Bennett’s first television play, A Day Out (1972), directed by Stephen Frears, and in the mid-1970s played Martin Gimbel for a year in the soap opera Emmerdale Farm. But writing music became his focus, and he landed his first major commission in 1974 as composer and musical director for Peter Gill’s production of Twelfth Night at Stratford-upon-Avon, the first of several collaborations with Gill during the 1970s and early 1980s.

Fenton also worked regularly at the Belgrade Theatre, Coventry, and collaborated with Adrian Noble on three productions at the Royal Exchange Theatre, Manchester. In the meantime he had diversified into composing for television, initially for Peter Gill’s production of Stephen Poliakoff’s Hitting Town (1976).

Although Fenton produced more music for the stage, including The Judas Kiss (1998) and The Last Cigarette (2009) for Richard Eyre, he turned more of his attention to films and television, scoring Hollywood hits such as Groundhog Day (1993) and You’ve Got Mail (1998). Another of his frequent collaborators was the filmmaker Ken Loach, with whom Fenton worked on My Name is Joe (1998), Carla’s Song (1996), and Land and Freedom (1995). In 2001 he scored Navigators, Loach’s film about railway privatisation.

In 1994, after nine months of work, Fenton was told, with no explanation, that his film score for Interview with the Vampire had been scrapped. “It destroyed me. Your score is like one of your babies,” he lamented. “ Writing 90 minutes of music for a full orchestra, recording it, putting it on the film, seeing the film play well with it and then having it junked is hard.”

He continued to work with Alan Bennett, scoring his stage musical Allelujah!, set on a geriatric ward, and the 2022 film version. They crowned their decades of collaboration with the film The Choral (2025), with Fenton not only arranging the music but appearing in a cameo role as the chauffeur of Sir Edward Elgar (Simon Russell Beale).

Fenton won four Baftas, two Emmys and five Ivor Novello awards, and received five Academy Award nominations. He taught at the National Film School, London, and at the Royal College of Music, where he was both a Fellow and a visiting professor.

In 2007 he was awarded a fellowship of the British Academy of Composers and Songwriters. He was appointed CBE in 2023.

George Fenton is survived by his second wife, Amanda, as well as his former wife Jane and three daughters.
