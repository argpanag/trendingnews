Central Park's horse-drawn carriage drivers sue NYC over ban starting in June 2028

TheTools · 20.11.2026 02:08 · source · permalink

Meanwhile, Mamdani says he's commissioning a feasibility study examining electric carriages and electric pedal-assist bicycles as potential alternatives.

The union is asking a judge to issue a temporary restraining order. NBC 4’s Erica Byfield has the latest. 

Hold your horses: Minutes after Mayor Zohran Mamdani signed a bill into law Thursday that would end New York City's 150-year-old horse-drawn carriage industry, the carriage drivers filed a lawsuit to halt the ban from taking effect.

Under the new law, horse carriages will not be able to operate on city streets as of June 1, 2028. However, the union representing carriage drivers filed a federal lawsuit in Manhattan attempting to have the ban struck down, arguing the city is taking away their livelihoods.

“The City’s approach can be summarized in twelve words: immediately cripple the owners, ignore property interests and contracts, and maybe compensate,” the lawsuit reads.

The lawsuit is the latest turn in a long fight over the carriage rides, as animal welfare activists and industry supporters have tussled over whether the carriage rides amount to animal cruelty or are simply a romantic throwback to the city's past.

The Mamdani administration said it will support about 200 affected workers through medallion buybacks, career services, and workforce development. It also says it intends to incorporate their feedback into a more comprehensive plan slated for release next April.

"For 150 years, horse-drawn carriages have been a fixture of Central Park. Today, we are bringing that chapter to a close,” Mamdani said in a statement. “This industry will come to an end, and we have a responsibility to the workers whose livelihoods depend on it. We will work alongside them to ensure they have the financial support and career opportunities they need to build their next chapter.”   

At a bill signing ceremony at City Hall, Mamdani lauded the role of horses in the city’s development but said the animals should no longer be hauling tourists around a perpetually busy Central Park among joggers, cyclists and other passersby.

“Our city has changed and now it is time for our laws to change, too,” the mayor said.

According to Mamdani, City Hall will establish a new Horse Carriage Worker Industry Program to provide career advisement, job training, entrepreneurship support, connections to employers, educational opportunities, and tuition assistance for affected carriage drivers and stable hands. 

That'll be in partnership with NYC Talent and the NYC Department of Small Business Services.

As for Central Park, Mamdani says he's commissioning a feasibility study examining electric carriages and electric pedal-assist bicycles as potential alternatives, as well as electric carriages along the park’s perimeter.

Drivers argue their future remains uncertain.

"Despite the good intentions of the mayor, the protections he outlined recently should have been written into the legislation," said John Samuelsen, head of the Transport Workers Union, which represents carriage drivers. 

The union's lawsuit argues the law is unconstitutional and that the city violated local legal procedures when it approved the law. It names the mayor and various other city officials as defendants.

The New York City Council approved the bill in October after city lawmakers reignited their push for a ban earlier in the year in response to the death of 18-year-old Indian tourist Romanch Mahajan. The teen's father previously said the vote would be part of his son's legacy.

"It was very heartbreaking," Deepak said on a video call from India. "We weren't able to save our child."

Back in June, 18-year-old Romanch's deadly fall came after the carriage horse driver stepped away from the animal to take a family photo. Something spooked the horse, causing it to speed off and tip the carriage over, causing the accident that took Romanch's life.

Three consecutive mayors have called for an end to carriage horses in New York City. After the death of Romanch, the movement gained new energy.

Over the years, horses have collapsed or gotten startled and run off into cars or other objects. In response, the city has put various safety regulations in place for the industry, including veterinary checkups, vacation time for horses and a retirement age for the animals.

A spokesperson for the city's law department said it is reviewing the case.
