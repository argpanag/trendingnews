Bank of AMD

TheTools · 19.11.2026 20:15 · source · permalink

Wall Street has picked its alternative to Nvidia. It is run by the legendary Lisa Su, who also happens to be Jensen Huang’s first cousin once removed. 

Advanced Micro Devices (Nasdaq: AMD), founded in Sunnyvale, California, in 1969, designs chips for computers, gaming consoles, and data centers.

As the scandal at Nvidia unfolded, some investors have treated Su’s company as a less risky way to own the AI buildout. AMD shares rose almost 45% in the five sessions through November 12, the day Nvidia announced Huang’s resignation in what CNBC reported was a board decision to oust him. William Blair and Oppenheimer upgraded the stock. 

As of the November 18 close, AMD traded at about 30 times its expected earnings for its next fiscal year, compared with about 9 times for Nvidia, based on analyst estimates published by Yahoo Finance. 

The Bear Cave believes investors are trading one cousin’s problems for the other’s. 

Today, our affiliated publication Hunterbrook Media exposed that IEIT Systems, a Chinese server maker, incorporated at least six newer versions of AMD’s firmware into its products after it was put on a U.S. export blacklist. The investigation also revealed that three server companies with ties to IEIT offer closely matching firmware. You can read their full investigation here.

The Bear Cave is more focused on AMD’s financials, particularly the ways in which its business model looks much like some of the most problematic aspects of Nvidia’s. 

Think: All of the circular financing of Nvidia, but without the margins or pricing power. 

AMD finances its own customers much the way Nvidia does. It guarantees leases, backstops unsold cloud capacity, signs data center leases on behalf of customers, and buys stock in customers. 

AMD has also done something that appears nowhere in Nvidia’s latest quarterly report. It gave its two largest AI customers the right to buy up to 16% of the company for a penny a share.

If Nvidia is the Central Bank of AI, then AMD is more like the Silicon Valley Bank of AI. On the surface, its financials look solid. But a proverbial AI capex bank run could test just how solid they are. 

That’s because, if the very customers whose growth AMD is helping finance pull back on their spending, even briefly, AMD could face weaker chip sales at the same time it remains on the hook for tens of billions worth of commitments.

On the eve of Jensen Huang’s downfall earlier this month, Hunterbrook Media graphed all of Nvidia’s commitments. The Bear Cave has now done the same for AMD.

The high-level numbers are striking. AMD has promised customers up to 320 million shares, worth $161.1 billion as of the market close on November 17. It has also committed about $28 billion through guarantees, partner leases and cloud-capacity agreements, plus $5.1 billion in equity investments. All while AMD itself has just $15.8 billion of cash and short-term investments on hand. The commitments span multiple years and, in some cases, depend on certain conditions, such as a partner defaulting or their computing capacity going unsold, before AMD is required to kick in any money.

But there is a bigger problem: AMD is scaling up production at an extraordinarily fast pace — its purchase commitments have quadrupled in nine months, from $12.2 billion at the end of 2025 to $48.8 billion in September — right as competitors are expanding supply. 

Shipments of Google’s TPUs doubled in a single quarter, measured by computing power, according to Epoch AI. On November 9, Google added two more chips to the lineup and said it has nearly 100 TPU customers. Amazon says its chip business is growing at triple-digit rates, and it plans to start selling Trainium outside its own cloud late next year. 

And, perhaps most interestingly, the very scrutiny on Nvidia that has led AMD’s share price to soar may itself create big problems for AMD.

If activist short-seller Culper Research was right — and more than 20% of Nvidia’s AI chip sales are driven by China — then Washington blocking Nvidia’s sales to China via intermediaries will lead to a surge in advanced chip supply available for the rest of the world. That supply will compete directly against AMD, right as Lisa Su’s empire has taken on more commitments than ever before.

Below the paywall, The Bear Cave takes a forensic dive into AMD’s financials. If you want a peek, you can explore our interactive graphic of The Bank of AMD. 

The key takeaway: Proportional to its revenue, AMD has made way more promises to its customers and partners than Nvidia has. It has less cash, too. Which means, if the smoke surrounding Nvidia becomes an inferno for the AI build-out, AMD will be engulfed by the flames.

And don't take our word for it. Lisa Su has sold 640,000 shares for about $248 million this year, including $35.5 million worth on Monday. 

As Vampire Weekend once sang: “Me and my cousins and you and your cousins. It’s a line that’s always running.”
