The 'quiet assassin': Pope reveals his early inspiration as he collects another major award

TheTools · 26.11.2026 18:30 · source · permalink

When he was in primary school in Canberra, David Pope would cut out the Peanuts comic strip from the newspaper and paste it into a scrapbook or head to his local library to borrow comic books like Asterix and Tintin, unknowingly laying the foundation for a career as one of Australia's most celebrated cartoonists.

"It was pre-internet, so you were limited to what your libraries might provide and what your newspaper might show you or what certain magazines would show you," he said.

"I'd try drawing that sort of stuff [like Peanuts] and I did a lot of other drawing, pictures of birds and things like that.

"Then I got interested in politics at the end of school and started drawing cartoons for some little social movements I was involved in, like the peace movement at ANU. They were pretty crude drawings."

Much later, Pope would be inspired by the cartoon work of Bruce Petty which, amid their often feverish pen-and-pencil markings, would lie a powerful message, political or otherwise.

"I hold onto Bruce Petty who was the great cartoonist who just kept an open, inquisitive, humanist eye on everything right up until he stopped drawing in his quite elderly age," he said.

Now the long-time cartoonist for The Canberra Times, Pope's work is revered in the industry, showing that same humanist quality as Petty's, his cartoons often depicting the little man or woman looking on in almost stunned disbelief at the carry-on of their political masters.

On Friday, November 27, Pope was announced as the 2026 Political Cartoonist of the Year, as part of the popular Behind the Lines exhibition at the Museum of Australian Democracy, a snapshot of the year in politics as seen by 42 of the nation's leading cartoonists.

It's the third time Pope has won the award, also taking home the honour in 2012 and 2022.

A judging panel including satirist Mark Humphries, the 2025 Political Cartoonist of the Year Matt Golding and curators Anne-Marie Conde and Matthew Jones singled out Pope's works for their "excellent draughtsmanship, astute political commentary and clever use of references", capturing many of the year's defining debates from data centres to monoculturalism.

MoAD chair Barrie Cassidy, a veteran political journalist, said it was "quite remarkable" to consider that Pope had been named political cartoonist of the year three times across a 14-year span as part of the Behind the Lines exhibition.

"Other cartoonists have said to me he has this consistency in his work but also this ability to stay with the changing demands of the industry," Mr Cassidy said.

Although a long-time casual illustrator and cartoonist beforehand, Pope started full-time with The Canberra Times in 2008, taking over from Geoff Pryor who had been the editorial cartoonist for 30 years before his retirement.

Mr Cassidy, for one, appreciated the paper's cartooning pedigree.

"I first arrived in Canberra in 1980 and what strikes me is that lineage from Geoff Pryor, who was one of our outstanding cartoonists when I arrived in Canberra, to David Pope, you know, 45 years," he said.

"So at all times, The Canberra Times has had one of the country's best cartoonists on staff. The two have just dominated that period."

Pope may be a genial giant in person, but he knew how to hit the mark, Mr Cassidy said.

"On the face of it, he appears to take a very earnest and considered view to his work, but don't be fooled by that. When he's inspired, he's the quiet assassin, packing a real punch," he said.

Pope was honoured to be named again political cartoonist of the year in the place where national politics all began, Old Parliament House, the building now housing the Museum of Australian Democracy.

"There's very few places where our craft is celebrated in such a strong way by such a big institution so I'm really appreciative of that. It's a good thing," he said.

The Behind the Lines exhibition this year draws connections between high school and parliament, the exhibition nicknamed BTL High, still a snapshot of the political year as seen through the eyes of 42 of the country's best cartoonists.

For Pope, school was in Canberra, in the still-developing neighbourhoods of Belconnen.

Born in Wodonga, Pope was seven when his family moved from Melbourne to Canberra, his father a former teacher who started work with IBM in the national capital.

The family - Pope has a younger brother and sister - settled in Macgregor on the north-west tip of the ACT in what then was the last house in the suburb.

"We had neighbours on one side and on the other side we had paddocks and the giant electrical tower and it was near the creek, so we spent a lot of time playing down there," he said.

"But, as a teenager, I didn't really like living out there. It was a long way to get anywhere on public transport."

Pope attended Ginninderra High and Copland College as well as the Australian National University, always drawing, always illustrating, sometimes submitting cartoons under the punk pen name of Heinrich Hinz.

After working casually for The Canberra Times, drawing illustrations in ink and watercolour and being paid per illustration, he assumed the mantle of editorial cartoonist in 2008, nearing two decades ago. Pope stopped drawing on paper for The Canberra Times in 2011, instead using a pen digitiser to draw his work onto a computer screen.

"I really like the freedom of shifting to a digital drawing because it freed me up with composition because so often you'd pencil out something on a piece of paper and if you wanted to change it, you had to rub it all out again. I loved the freedom of just doing a sketch of something and being able to move it if I changed my mind. So it's really changed the way I draw," he said.

Artificial intelligence is the latest challenge bearing down. Pope recognises that AI can be used as a force for good but believes, more than anything, it's "a lot of hype" that will never replace the human hand, heart or mind.

"It's not going to last," he said. "People can see it. People are aware of it. It's going to make people appreciate human, creative stuff more."

Now with three adult children and one granddaughter, Pope, 61, continues to produce award-winning cartoons under the pressure of a daily deadline.

"There's a great quote from the American architects Charles and Ray Eames about deadlines - 'The best you can do between now and next Tuesday is a kind of the best you can do' - so if you had more time you wouldn't necessarily produce something better," he said.

"The deadlines, I think at one level, are a great disciple and a tool for getting the best out of you but at other times they are just hell."

While his work extends far beyond the politics of his day - his stunning prints and posters of the South Coast and Canberra are collector's items - Pope does not call himself an artist.

"I see myself as a cartoonist," he said.
