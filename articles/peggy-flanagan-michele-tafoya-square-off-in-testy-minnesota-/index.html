Peggy Flanagan, Michele Tafoya square off in testy Minnesota Senate debate

The major party candidates for Minnesota’s U.S. Senate seat faced off in an hourlong debate Friday on MPR News with fraud, affordability and fallout from the Trump administration’s policies as key points of friction.

It’s likely the only time Democratic nominee Peggy Flanagan and Republican nominee Michele Tafoya will appear on the same stage ahead of the election. Their interactions often pivoted to two other officials not slated to be on the ballot — President Donald Trump and Gov. Tim Walz.

The debate comes days after Trump endorsed Tafoya in the contest. Meanwhile, in campaign ads and news events, Tafoya has sought to distance herself from Trump and brand herself as an “independent thinker’ prepared to break with her party. Flanagan frequently highlighted that endorsement and argued her opponent would be a “rubber stamp” for the president.

Tafoya, meanwhile, repeatedly called out the fact that Flanagan was second in command in state government as reports of hundreds of millions of dollars in fraud in state programs came to light.

Flanagan said Tafoya was trying to reinvent herself in the closing weeks of the campaign.

“Let's be clear: Donald Trump doesn't just endorse anybody,” Flanagan said. “He endorses against people in his own party if they dare to take him on, Michele Tafoya passed his litmus test with flying colors. She supports the tariffs, the trade wars, the war in Iran.”

Tafoya said that Flanagan was the one running from her record on fraud.

“Talk about changing tacks when it becomes politically expedient,” Tafoya said. “My goodness, she was second in charge in this state, and she's just going to point her finger at Gov. Tim Walz when she demanded it be the Walz-Flanagan administration, when she demanded to be in the room where everything happened, but suddenly she has nothing to do with this.”

Flanagan said Walz said he would take the buck for addressing fraud and that she was more involved in moving other DFL priorities like paid family and medical leave and free school lunches.

The candidates also spelled out policy platforms when it came to their views on foreign policy, Social Security, taxes and immigration policy. The two, along with Libertarian candidate Rebecca Whiting, independent candidate Marisa Simonetti and write-in candidate Alycia Gruenhagen, are vying to fill the seat set to come open when U.S. Sen. Tina Smith retires.

Some of the key exchanges are distilled below.

Tariffs

“The tariffs that we see happening right now are killing our industries. Soybeans, corn growers are feeling the squeeze. We have lost entire markets potentially for generations,” Flanagan said. “I think Congress has to do their job, and we need to send people who are going to stand up to this president and realize that you know tariffs are under the control of Congress.”

Tafoya said the tariffs are a tool to bring other countries to the table to reset trade agreements.

“The playing field has not been even, and it's been both sides of the (political) aisle, quite frankly, that have led to this,” Tafoya said. “What we're asking for is to level the playing field, to bring partners to the table to say, let's renegotiate this.”

Immigration enforcement

Both were asked about their views on immigration policy and the immigration enforcement surge last winter in Minnesota.

Flanagan said agents responded with violence and chaos. She said ICE needs to be completely overhauled.

"ICE should absolutely be ripped apart, and it should be replaced by a system that absolutely has secure borders, that is grounded in public safety for everyone, dignity, due process, humanity, and a pathway to citizenship for folks who have been here who are law-abiding,” Flanagan said.

Tafoya called the shooting deaths of Renee Good and Alex Pretti at the hands of ICE officers devastating, and said the crackdown was handled poorly by former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem. But she placed much of the blame for unrest on state leaders, including Flanagan.

"This was not about peaceful protesting. This was about violence and criminal conduct. And she should know because she has participated in illegal protests in the past as well,” Tafoya said.

Flanagan then pointed to Tafoya’s shifting views on the federal surge, initially campaigning with footage of federal agents in Minneapolis to later calling out the operation as poorly executed.

"Two Minnesotans are dead due to Donald Trump's ICE. Michele Tafoya was A-OK with all of it. Used it in her campaign rollout video. Said that Renee Good was responsible for her own death," Flanagan countered.

Tafoya interjected, "That is a hideous statement."

Foreign policy

Asked about whether she would vote to end the war in Iran, Tafoya said it’s gone on too long. She said earlier in this year she supported U.S. intervention there.

"The prices are too high. This war does need to end, and it needs to end quickly,” Tafoya said. “But it also needs to end strategically."

Flanagan pointed to Tafoya’s shift on the issue. She steadfastly opposed the war from the start. She committed to voting to end it.

“This war is costing us financially, but also in lives. Twenty-one service men and women have lost their life, including a Minnesotan, a mother. That's unacceptable,” Flanagan said.

The candidates also fielded questions about other conflicts in the Middle East.

Flanagan called the attack on Israelis by Hamas on Oct. 7, 2023, horrific, but said the response from Benjamin Netanyahu's government has been deeply destructive. She’s called it a genocide in the past. She said she would oppose additional U.S. military aid to Israel under Netanyahu.

“The terrorist attack by Hamas was horrific, and I know the incredible trauma, and harm and violence that people experienced,” Flanagan said. “I also know that the response from Benjamin Netanyahu's government since that time has been deeply destructive. Children who have lost limbs, who have lost their lives, entire neighborhoods destroyed in Gaza.”

Tafoya said she completely stands with Israel and raised concerns about branding what’s happening in Gaza a genocide.

"It is not a genocide when you offer medicines to those people and send in life-saving water and food and nutrition to those people, when you drop leaflets to let them know what is coming, that is not a genocide,” Tafoya said.

Social Security

On the future of Social Security, Flanagan said she would avoid benefit cuts by applying the payroll tax to people with higher earnings.

“We have to remove the cap for $250,000 or higher,” Flanagan said. “I support the Social Security Expansion Act, and that will ensure solvency for at least the next seven decades."

Tafoya called Flanagan's answer "pat," but wouldn't say whether she would support higher taxes or cuts in benefits. She declined to lay out a proposed solution.

“I think Minnesotans need to think about who they are sending to the Senate to solve this issue. We need independent thinkers. We need people willing to reach across the aisle to make sure that we find solutions that are second level, third level thinking,” Tafoya said.

Abortion

Flanagan pointed to Tafoya’s past positions on abortion, highlighting a letter she wrote to Republican delegates this spring saying she aligns with Trump on this issue. Tafoya said she would vote to confirm “conservative, strict-constructionist judges” and oppose taxpayer funds to be used for abortion. In the letter, she says there should be “reasonable access to abortion in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy” and opposed Minnesota’s current law protecting access to abortion.

Tafoya said she was a “pro-choice Republican” and reiterated Friday that she would support “reasonable access” to abortion within the first three months of pregnancy. When pressed, she didn’t spell out what that meant.

“I have no desire from the United States Senate to change that law. I have taken a lot of heat from my party for being a pro-choice Republican,” she said.

Flanagan supported Minnesota’s 2023 abortion law change to guaranteed the right to many forms of reproductive health access. She would vet Supreme Court justices after speaking with them. But she said she didn’t anticipate supporting Trump appointees.

Term limits

Asked about term limits for U.S. senators, Tafoya said she favored restricting service to two or three terms. Meanwhile, Flanagan said she opposed term limits and felt voters could gauge when a senator was past their limit.
