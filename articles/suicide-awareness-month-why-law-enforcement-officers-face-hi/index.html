Suicide Awareness Month: Why law enforcement officers face higher risk, and why so many stay silent

TheTools · 26.09.2026 04:10 · source · permalink

BIRMINGHAM, Ala. (WBRC) - Law enforcement leaders in Shelby County are encouraging officers to speak up and ask for help as part of Suicide Awareness Month, observed every September to raise awareness about suicide prevention and reduce the stigma around seeking mental health support.

The CDC says law enforcement officers are more likely to die by suicide than in the line of duty. Public safety wellness researchers estimate more than 150 officer suicides are reported nationwide each year, though the true number may be higher due to underreporting. Officers are among the most at-risk for suicide but are among the least likely to ask for help, according to the CDC.

‘54% more likely’: The numbers behind the risk

Calera Police Chief David Hyche said the crisis is playing out in law enforcement agencies across the country.

“In law enforcement, you’re 54% more likely to have suicide than the general population,” Hyche said.

The CDC says officers face that higher risk because of what they see on the job. Hyche said trauma builds year after year, call after call.

“We’re putting these kids out on the street and they’re seeing young people dying in accidents. They’re having to speak to their parents. They’re seeing teen suicide. We’re having to deal with just horrendous crime scenes,” Hyche said.

Why some officers don’t ask for help

Shelby County Drug Enforcement Task Force Commander Lt. Clayton Smith said the nature of the job can create a barrier to seeking help.

“In our line of work, it’s hard for us to ask for help because we’re the people that are supposed to help,” Smith said. “You’re not weak for asking for help. And you’re only bettering yourself, your family, and your community when you do get that help.”

Hyche said the message has to start at the top.

“If we don’t set an example for the younger generation and show them that it’s okay to ask for help, it’s okay to get help, the suicide rate is going to continue,” Hyche said.

What departments are doing to help

Hyche said Calera police officers have access to peer support teams made up of officers trained to talk with colleagues who may be struggling. The department also works with local clergy members and a police foundation that provides support to staff facing personal or family hardships.

Advocates say those kinds of resources — along with leaders willing to be open about their own struggles — are key to closing the gap between officers who need help and those who actually ask for it.

If you or anyone you know is struggling, help is available 24/7 by calling or texting 988 to reach the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.
