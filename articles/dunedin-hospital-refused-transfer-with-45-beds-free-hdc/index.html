Dunedin Hospital refused transfer with 45 beds free: HDC

TheTools · 25.10.2026 07:29 · source · permalink

A rural doctor did everything right. On 4 August 2023, a 67-year-old woman arrived at a rural emergency department vomiting, confused, with a racing heart and rapid breathing from an infected hand burn. The sole doctor on shift recognised likely sepsis and tried urgently to get her to Dunedin Hospital. A General Medicine registrar declined, citing no inpatient beds and a 16-hour ED wait. According to the Health and Disability Commissioner’s decision, Health NZ’s own records showed 45 beds were available at the time.

She waited 13 hours before being flown by helicopter to Dunedin, where she was diagnosed with necrotising fasciitis. She died eight months later, in April 2024, after prolonged hospitalisation and gruelling rehabilitation.

That detail changes the story. This was not a hospital that ran out of room. It was a hospital that did not know what room it had.

The information never reached the decision

The registrar had not been told of the patient’s elevated white blood cell count. The senior medical officer for Southern General Medicine told the HDC that result would have prompted immediate acceptance, and that she had never refused a patient on bed-capacity grounds. Commissioner Morag McDowell found a breach of Right 4(4) of the Code of Rights, concluding the rural hospital had promptly recognised the severity of the case but was met with “systemic roadblocks”.

Strip away the clinical language and the failure is familiar to anyone who has run a business. A critical data point did not travel with the request. A junior person made a high-stakes call on incomplete information. The capacity figure they relied on was wrong. And no escalation step caught any of it.

A second case, the same fault line

The same week, the HDC released an unrelated finding. A man who injured his elbow in a fall at home in March 2021 had his lower leg amputated above the knee five days later and died hours after surgery. Deputy Commissioner Vanessa Caldwell called it “unacceptable” that vascular specialists did not review him until four days after a senior doctor identified his leg appeared acutely ischaemic. She blamed a series of failings across multiple staff, including poor documentation and communication of senior concerns.

Two cases, two years apart, one common thread. Senior judgement existed. It just did not get where it needed to go.

Dunedin has heard this before

In 2021, an internal review of a Code Black overcrowding event found the crisis could have been avoided had warning signs been acted on sooner, with senior staff taking a further 19 hours to formally cancel elective surgery after the need was recognised. In 2022, the then-Deputy Commissioner found the then-Southern DHB had let more than 10 hours pass between abnormal blood results and sepsis treatment for another patient.

Capacity pressure is real, and it is national. In 2025, RNZ reported Dunedin’s ED hit 187% capacity on one August day, while Wellington ran close to 200% on several days. Back in 2024, Otago cardiologist Gerard Wilkins warned that Dunedin’s single cardiac catheterisation lab left the lower South Island without the redundancy other regions have. Those are legitimate resourcing arguments. They are not what the HDC found this week.

The politics is reaching for the wrong lever

Predictably, the findings became a fight over staffing, with the government blaming Labour and Labour promising to end Health NZ’s hiring freeze. Both positions miss the point. More staff working inside a broken escalation pathway still produce the same outcome. Nobody needed a new hire to send a white cell count down the phone line or to check a live bed board.

The new hospital will not fix it either. In 2025, Health NZ confirmed the rebuild would deliver 424 overnight beds after yet another round of remodelling. Bricks are being argued over endlessly. The software of the system, who knows what, and who can say no, gets far less attention.

Accountability is part of that. A recent analysis in The Conversation criticised Health NZ’s adverse event reviews for defaulting to vague “system improvements” rather than genuine accountability. “Systemic roadblocks” is an accurate diagnosis, but it can also become a place where responsibility disappears.

Why business in the south should care

Otago and Southland employers recruit on lifestyle, and reliable acute care is part of that pitch. When a public institution with a multibillion-dollar rebuild underway cannot match patients to empty beds, it erodes confidence among the doctors, engineers and managers firms are trying to attract. It also nudges more households towards private insurance, a cost that increasingly lands on employer benefits budgets.

Health NZ Southern chief medical officer Dr Rebecca Brushwood says the organisation accepts the findings, has apologised, and has changed how clinicians communicate care plans and escalation pathways. That is the right response. The test is whether it can be measured: real-time bed visibility for anyone taking a transfer call, mandatory sharing of key results, and automatic senior sign-off before a sick patient is turned away. Those fixes are cheap compared with a new building. If Health NZ cannot deliver them, no amount of funding or hiring will close the gap between beds that exist and patients who need them.
