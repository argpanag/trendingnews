52% of Ferrari's fans want stricter rules or team orders

TheTools · 26.11.2026 09:33 · source · permalink

News

Our Ferrari survey finds 52% favour stricter racing rules or team orders after the Leclerc-Hamilton collision, while 15% want the pair to keep racing freely.

52% of respondents want Ferrari to introduce stricter racing rules or use team orders after the latest collision between Charles Leclerc and Lewis Hamilton, according to Formula Live Pulse's latest user survey.

Stricter rules for racing each other attracted the largest individual share at 30%, followed by team orders whenever the drivers fight for position at 22%. Only 15% chose to let them keep racing freely.

The results put Ferrari's management of its drivers at the centre of the debate after their contact at the 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix. There is support for intervention, but respondents are divided over how far Fred Vasseur should go.

Ferrari survey results: what should happen after the Leclerc-Hamilton collision?

We asked Formula Live Pulse users: “What should Ferrari do after yet another collision between Leclerc and Hamilton?”

The results were:

  • Let them keep racing freely: 15%
  • Set stricter rules for racing each other: 30%
  • Use team orders whenever they’re fighting for position: 22%
  • Pick a lead driver and give them priority: 18%
  • Change the driver line-up ASAP: 15%

The leading option received twice the share of either unrestricted racing or an immediate driver change. However, no individual answer secured a majority.

Combining stricter rules with team orders produces the 52% headline figure. Those are distinct approaches, and the result should not be read as a majority specifically demanding that Ferrari ban its drivers from racing.

Why the Las Vegas clash renewed Ferrari's team orders debate

Leclerc attempted to pass Hamilton on the inside at Turn 7 in Las Vegas, and the contact sent Hamilton into a spin. The stewards held Leclerc wholly responsible and imposed a 10-second penalty.

Hamilton finished seventh, while Leclerc dropped from sixth on the road to tenth once his penalty was applied. Ferrari collected seven points between its two cars.

The incident followed the pair's earlier clash in Mexico and talks before São Paulo. As we reported in our coverage of Vasseur's reaction to the latest Leclerc-Hamilton collision, the team principal considered the repeat unacceptable.

That sequence gives the survey its context. Ferrari had already addressed the issue internally, yet another fight between its drivers ended in contact.

Stricter racing rules lead the vote with 30%

The most popular answer would preserve competition between Leclerc and Hamilton while giving Ferrari more control over how they race.

That could mean clearer instructions about defensive moves, when a driver should abandon an attack, and when the pit wall should interrupt a battle. These are possible ways to apply stricter rules, rather than policies Ferrari has announced.

The distinction matters. Drivers can remain free to fight for position within limits designed to protect both cars. An instruction to stop attacking would go further.

Our analysis of what Ferrari must change after the Hamilton-Leclerc clash examines the practical decisions behind that balance. Any intervention needs to be clear enough for both drivers to understand before they reach the next braking zone.

Team orders attract 22%, but create a different challenge

The second most popular answer calls for Ferrari to use team orders whenever Leclerc and Hamilton are fighting for position.

That approach would move more responsibility to the pit wall. Ferrari would have to decide whether to hold positions or arrange a swap, taking account of each driver's pace and the threat from rival cars.

Vasseur identified a complication after Las Vegas: asking a driver to slow down in a slipstream battle can leave that car vulnerable to an opponent. Protecting the two Ferraris from each other can therefore introduce a different risk.

The 22% share shows support for direct intervention. It does not establish which driver respondents would want ahead, or how Ferrari should resolve each situation.

A lead driver gets 18%, while 15% want a line-up change

Giving one driver priority attracted 18% of the vote. A permanent hierarchy would go beyond managing an individual battle, potentially shaping strategy and position decisions across races.

The survey did not ask respondents to choose between Leclerc and Hamilton. It therefore provides no basis for claiming that users want either driver installed as Ferrari's number one.

An immediate line-up change received 15%, matching the share who want unrestricted racing. The question also did not identify which driver should leave or who should replace them.

Across the three options involving stricter rules, team orders or a lead driver, 70% selected a way to manage the existing pairing. For Ferrari, that leaves a concrete question: how much authority should the pit wall exercise before another wheel-to-wheel fight puts both cars at risk?

These results reflect participating Formula Live Pulse users and should not be treated as a representative poll of all Formula 1 fans.
