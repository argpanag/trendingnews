What role could Arab parties play after the election?

Arab parties Joint List and Raam are projected to win a record 18 seats – the highest Arab representation ever.

That voter surge was driven by an urgent desire to dismantle Netanyahu’s right-wing coalition, which includes hardline ministers like Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich.

The Arab parties could play a key role in building a coalition to form a new government. But what that could look like remains unclear.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc is projected to hold 50 seats. But he has explicitly ruled out forming a government with the Arab parties. To form a majority in the Knesset, he needs 61 seats.

Meanwhile, Gadi Eisenkot — whose opposition bloc is projected to hold 52 seats — has said these parties do not meet his requirements for political partnership, including recognizing Israel as a Jewish state and recognizing Hamas as a terrorist organization that must be dismantled.

Still, a survey from the Hebrew University’s aChord Center shows a majority of opposition bloc voters keen to see that partnership come together.

More than 61% of opposition bloc voters want Eisenkot to form a government with Raam, the survey said. Only 20% of those voters said they prefer new elections, while 12% said they favor a rotation government with Netanyahu and 7% prefer a government with ultra-Orthodox parties.

We have the exit polls. What happens next?

While the exit polls give an early indication of what may happen next, the real contest begins once the votes are counted and the coalition building process begins. It can take weeks, or even months, for a winner to emerge.

Once the vote count is final, usually within a few days, the president holds a round of consultations in which each elected party recommends their choice for prime minister.

Based on these talks, the president then tasks one candidate with forming the government. This is not necessarily the leader of the largest party, but rather the one who has the best chance at building a viable coalition.

The way things look right now, the incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is unlikely to be selected because neither the opposition bloc nor the Arab parties will recommend him.

If that is the case, President Isaac Herzog is likely to give the first opportunity to form a government to Gadi Eisenkot.

He would have four to six weeks to negotiate with other parties over cabinet posts, budget priorities and policy, trying to cobble together a majority in the Knesset. If the exit polls turn out to be correct, the two Arab parties will play a key role in this process.

A survey conducted this evening by the Hebrew University’s aChord Center found that a majority of those who voted for the opposition bloc want Eisenkot to form a government with Raam, one of the two Arab parties.

If Eisenkot was to fail – a real possibility given his relative inexperience – the another candidate, most likely Netanyahu, would be given a chance by the president to form a government and the whole bargaining process starts again.

If he was to fail too, the Knesset itself gets a short window to put forward another candidate, and if it cannot - the country goes to new elections.

So winning is not only about votes and seats, it’s about building - and maintaining - a viable coalition.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has refused to appoint such a commission, which is Israel’s highest investigative body, and has not accepted responsibility for the deadliest day in Israel’s history, in which over 1,200 Israelis were killed and 251 were kidnapped to Gaza. In sharp contrast, all the parties in the opposition bloc have expressed support for establishing such a commission. “These elections were about truth, about responsibility, and about establishing a state commission of inquiry into the October 7th massacre,” the group said in a statement. “The citizens of Israel have spoken, and it is time for elected officials to do what is incumbent upon them.”

The council urged all party leaders and candidates who made a commitment to act to establish a commission on the first day of the new Knesset “without delays and without condition,” even before a government is formed. “There is no reason to wait for coalition agreements, the distribution of portfolios, or political negotiations. Establishing a state commission of inquiry is not a prize for the winners of the elections and is not a bargaining chip between parties. Voter turnout on Election Day surged to 77.7%, elections committee says

Voter turnout on Israel’s Election Day surged to 77.7%, the Central Elections Committee said on Tuesday evening, the highest percentage since 1999.

Nearly 5.7 million voters cast their ballot out of approximately 7.3 million eligible voters. Polling places closed at 10 p.m. local time, but the committee must still count double envelope ballots, which could be hundreds of thousands of additional votes.

These double envelope ballots are used by soldiers, reservists, diplomats, and others. They will push the voter turnout even higher, but the Central Elections Committee (CEC) doesn’t begin counting those ballots immediately.

Israel’s highest-ever voter turnout was 79.7% in 1988, according to the CEC.

Netanyahu has lost his majority, but Eisenkot's path to victory is complicated

With exit polls showing that neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu nor his main challenger Gadi Eisenkot have a clear path to a governing majority, post-election coalition arithmetic is now wide open. Deadlock, a minority government, a unity government – or even another election – are now all plausible outcomes.

The news is bad for Netanyahu. His bloc of ultra-Orthodox and far-right allies lost its majority from 68 seats to just 50. Exit polls still project that his Likud party will hold a sizeable 24 seats – and will remain the largest party. But it is still well short of the 61-seat majority needed in the 120-seat parliament. And Netanyahu crucially needs a coalition to stay in power.

The opposition bloc holds a weighty 70 seats on paper, but the reality is that it is fractured and divided, making it difficult to find a way forward. Jewish Zionist parties account for 52 seats, while the Arab parties are projected to win a record 18 seats – the highest Arab representation ever.

Eisenkot’s Yashar party is projected to be the largest party in the opposition bloc with 22 seats, but other party leaders in his bloc have ruled out a government that relies on Arab support – significantly complicating any bid to unseat Netanyahu.

The record Arab turnout appears to be one of the defining stories of the elections. Palestinian citizens of Israel – roughly 20% of Israel’s population – have traditionally voted at lower rates than the national average. In the 2022 election, Arab turnout was 53%. This time, it appears to have surged to over 78%. The move is seen as a protest vote against Netanyahu’s government and his far-right allies, National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich.

Meanwhile, a high voter turnout also raises the bar for the small parties, because they will need more votes to pass the 3.25% electoral threshold.

Exit polls are projections, and the picture could shift as real votes are counted overnight.

Why do many Israelis relate to Gadi Eisenkot? A political analyst explains

What makes a former military chief relatable to many Israelis? For Gadi Eisenkot’s supporters, it’s partly a sense that he shares their grief.

That’s according to Dahlia Scheindlin, a political analyst and senior columnist at Haaretz, considered a liberal publication, who told CNN that Eisenkot’s personal losses often come up in conversations with voters.

“He himself is a bereaved father,” she said.

Eisenkot’s son was killed fighting in Gaza two months into the war. The next day, he lost a nephew. And a year later, he lost another.

“Nobody says this is the reason to vote for him, but it often comes up when you ask why people feel like he’s so relatable,” Scheindlin said. “Many Israelis are living with a sense of loss.”

His military career is another part of his appeal. In Israeli politics, a storied military career is seen as a major asset and a gateway to success. Several former generals became prime ministers, including Yitzhak Rabin and Ehud Barak.

Eisenkot is attempting to play on the same sentiment. Supporters see him as “having devoted his life as a career army person to the good of the state,” Scheindlin said, adding that in Israel, like in many countries, the army is seen as above politics.

“In many ways, he represents everything that Netanyahu does not in their eyes,” said Scheindlin. His recent arrival in politics also leaves him less associated with its “general dirty business,” she said.

Many voters, she observed, feel the current government “is working only for Netanyahu’s closed circles.” In contrast, Eisenkot “is on their side.”

Israelis get a day off and free public transport to vote

Unlike many countries, Israel only allows in-person voting on the day of the election. There are no absentee ballots and no voting by proxy or by mail.

On top of that, each voter can only vote at the polling station that’s been assigned to them in their place of residence – although there are some exceptions to this rule, including for diplomats, soldiers at military bases, hospital patients, prisoners and people who have been evacuated from their homes.

To make it easier for people to be able to turn up, election days are traditionally public holidays in Israel. Schools, which are often used as polling stations, are shut, as are most workplaces.

To make any journey home to vote easier, many public transportation services including intercity buses and trains have been running for free since yesterday evening.

Israel voter turnout remains at record levels, surpassing previous 10 elections

Voter turnout in Israel’s election remains at record levels, according to the latest update from the Central Elections Committee, and has already surpassed the total voter turnout for the previous 10 elections.

As of 8:00 p.m. local time, 74.3% had voted, the committee said, with two hours of voting left until polling places close. A total of 5,450,000 had already cast their ballot out of about 7.3 million eligible voters.

Such a high turnout already surpasses the total turnout in all elections going back to 1999, when voter turnout reached 77.2%. In the 2022 election that put Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu back in power, turnout reached 70.6%.

The final voter turnout figure will be published one hour after polls close, the committee said.

What is the electoral threshold, and why is it so critical?

One of the most important numbers in Israeli politics is 3.25.

If a political party running in the election wants to qualify for the next Knesset, it needs to garner at least 3.25% of the vote. That’s the electoral threshold for making it into Israel’s parliament. Depending on voter turnout, that works out to about 160,000 votes or so.

Of the 37 parties currently running, most will fall far short of the electoral threshold, gaining only a handful of votes. This happens in every Israeli election. At most, 14 parties have a real chance of qualifying for the 120-seat Knesset.

What happens if a party doesn’t make it past the threshold? Its votes are thrown out. And this can have a dramatic effect on the outcome of the entire election.

In 2022, the left-wing Meretz party fell just short of the electoral threshold, bringing in 3.16% of the vote. It would have been enough for about four seats in the Knesset. But without those votes, the balance of power shifted to the right wing and to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who was able to put together a government that lasted its entire four-year term, the first time that’s happened in Israel since 1988.

This time, a number of smaller right-wing parties have been polling ahead of the vote right around the electoral threshold, including Ofer Winter’s Amcha Yisrael, Yoaz Hendel’s Reservists, and even Religious Zionism, the party of Israel’s far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich. If one or more of these parties fall short of the threshold, it could have a dramatic impact on whether Netanyahu has a likely path to remain in power.
