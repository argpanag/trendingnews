'Dug a hole for themselves': Black Caps greats question Australia's sandpaper controversy

TheTools · 19.11.2026 03:04 · source · permalink

Subscribe to listen

Former Black Caps internationals Mark Richardson and Simon Doull have questioned how much Australia’s senior players knew about the infamous sandpaper scandal, with both struggling to believe the ball-tampering operation went unnoticed by the team’s leading bowlers.

Details of one of cricket’s biggest controversies have resurfaced in recent weeks following fresh allegations from former Australian opener Usman Khawaja in his book, reigniting questions over the events of the 2018 test against South Africa in Cape Town, when Cameron Bancroft was caught on television attempting to alter the condition of the ball with sandpaper.

Then-captain Steve Smith and vice-captain David Warner were punished by Cricket Australia, while Bancroft was handed a nine-month ban and largely kept quiet about his version of events.

That changed yesterday, when Bancroft published an explosive 4000-word column in the West Australian, claiming the entire team knew about the plan to use sandpaper.

Bancroft said he felt “angry, betrayed and alone” after watching current Australian captain and fellow member of the 2018 team Pat Cummins deny any knowledge of the use of sandpaper during a 68-minute press conference last week.

Cummins’ camp has strongly rejected Bancroft’s latest claims.

Speaking on Ryan Bridge TODAY, Richardson, who played 38 tests for New Zealand, said his experience playing made it difficult to believe the practice could have been confined to a handful of individuals, particularly the bowling quartet of Cummins, Nathan Lyon, Mitchell Starc and Josh Hazlewood.

“I struggle to believe that everyone didn’t know,” Richardson said.

“I just know the times when I was playing where we were trying to get the ball to reverse swing. We weren’t doing it with sandpaper, but we were trying all sorts of stuff to try to get one side of the ball in a condition where it would start to reverse swing.

“It is a team effort because you want to limit the amount of hands that are holding the ball, you want to be holding on the seams, you don’t want to get sweat on the side that you’re trying to rough up because you’re trying to keep it as dry as you possibly can.

“Bowlers need to know how to hold the ball before they run in and bowl it and they’re the ones actually seeing it after each delivery. They see what’s going on.”

Doull, who played 32 tests for New Zealand, shared Richardson’s doubts, arguing that Australia’s leading bowlers would have been aware something was happening with the ball, even if they did not know sandpaper had been taken on to the field during the Cape Town test.

“I think going back eight years, we all knew that they must have known,” Doull said. “As a bowler myself in my former years, you know when something’s going on with the ball.

“Their whole defence, and I mean them as in the big four, Lyon, Cummins, Starc and Hazlewood, their whole defence was we didn’t know that sandpaper was taken onto the field that day.

“And that’s all the question has ever been around. They knew it was happening, they didn’t, maybe didn’t know that sandpaper was on the field that day, but they knew it had been going on in the previous test matches.”

Doull believes the renewed scrutiny could leave Australia’s senior players in an increasingly uncomfortable position, particularly Cummins, who has since established himself as one of the most prominent figures in world cricket.

He suggested that the Australian captain’s reputation as a fresh, clean-cut face of the national team had been damaged by the latest developments, warning that further revelations could make matters worse.

“I think they’ve dug a hole for themselves, to be honest; and I think Pat Cummins is continuing to dig it,” Doull said. “The more people come out, the worse it’s going to get for them, I think.

“They’re just going to be caught in a pretty horrendous lie and I guess Pat was supposed to be the sort of the squeaky-clean new brush for Cricket Australia and it’s sort of turning against them a little bit at the moment.”

Richardson believes the latest allegations could also make Cummins’ position as Australian test captain increasingly difficult, given the significance attached to the role in Australian cricket.

“What did they used to say in Australian cricket? Because it’s so big, the Australian captain is the second most important person in Australia, second only to the Prime Minister,” Richardson said.

“If confidence is eroded in him and people sort of don’t believe him and he’s lost that sort of good-boy look about him, then it makes that position as Australian captain quite untenable.”

The Australian test team face the Black Caps in a four-test series, starting next month.

Bancroft was also heavily critical of former Australia coach Darren Lehmann in his column, claiming he knew about the use of sandpaper.

Lehmann was on commentary duties for ABC Radio during an ODI between Australia and England when Bancroft’s account emerged, but made an early exit after the match.

Hours before Bancroft’s story, Cricket Australia said it will not re-investigate the sandpapergate scandal of 2018, but would open a private forum for people to come forward and “speak their truth”.

It issued a statement later saying it would take time to consider Bancroft’s comments, with the governing body having established a forum for those involved.

“We expect that there will be many dimensions to the events of 2018, which is why we have established the forum for individuals to give their version of events.”

Ben Francis is an Auckland-based reporter for the New Zealand Herald who covers breaking sports news.
