ADHD is over-diagnosed, say doctors

TheTools · 20.09.2026 18:06 · source · permalink

Seven in 10 GPs believe adult ADHD is over-diagnosed, with some saying private providers never reject a diagnosis.

Long NHS waits have driven patients towards private ADHD assessments, with clinics receiving fees from both people paying for themselves and NHS-funded referrals.

Some patients pay more than £1,000 for an assessment, while others access private providers at the taxpayers’ expense through England’s Right to Choose scheme, which gives NHS patients the right to pick which provider delivers their treatment.

Family doctors estimated that more than eight in 10 patients referred for specialist assessments ultimately received an ADHD diagnosis.

One GP reported only one negative assessment among more than 1,000 referrals, while another could not recall a private provider ever declining to diagnose the condition.

Doctors raised concerns about “superficial” assessments and warned that trauma and other mental health problems could be overlooked.

The findings were published before a government-commissioned review into the rising demand for ADHD, autism and mental health services.

Prof Peter Fonagy, the clinical psychologist leading the review, is expected to recommend providing more support based on people’s needs, without requiring a formal diagnosis.

His interim report found that sharply rising ADHD and autism diagnoses did not appear to reflect equivalent increases in the underlying prevalence of the conditions.

However, his report claimed that the evidence did not support a simple conclusion that ADHD was being over-diagnosed, a finding which has been disputed by doctors.

Prof Fonagy said underdiagnosis, misdiagnosis and overdiagnosis could coexist, and also highlighted that there had been historical under-recognition among women and girls.

He is expected to reject the idea that a “snowflake generation” is behind the surge in people seeking help for conditions such as anxiety, depression and ADHD.

The report said rising demand for ADHD services could also be explained by improved awareness and changes in help-seeking.

Prof Fonagy has previously argued that rising numbers of people seeking help reflect greater openness about mental health problems, rather than a decline in resilience.

He is also expected to present concerns that requiring a formal diagnosis to access support could “amplify demand” for diagnostic labels.

On Saturday, The Telegraph revealed that the number of adults prescribed drugs for ADHD had risen nearly sixfold since before the pandemic.

NHS figures show that soaring numbers of adults are being prescribed medication to help manage symptoms of the condition.

Around 268,000 adults were prescribed stimulants and other medicines used for ADHD in the latest quarter, compared with fewer than 47,000 in the same period in 2019.

The increase of 474 per cent means that almost six times as many adults are receiving the medications as before the pandemic.

One Dorset GP said NHS services occasionally concluded that patients did not have ADHD.

But they added: “I don’t think I have ever seen a private provider not give a positive diagnosis.”

A Berkshire doctor said that, of more than 1,000 patients they had referred, only one had not received an ADHD diagnosis.

However, Dr Katie Bramall, the chairman of the British Medical Association’s UK GP committee, who was herself diagnosed with ADHD through the NHS, defended the high diagnosis rate.

She said lengthy waits and demanding paperwork meant those reaching assessment were often the people most affected.

She added: “I’m sure the same is true for many other pathways to specialist referral.”

The survey of 829 GPs, conducted by Pulse, the GP publication, found almost half believed that adult ADHD was “very over-diagnosed”.

Overall, 410 respondents selected that description, while 173 considered it “slightly over-diagnosed”.

Another 148 believed it was underdiagnosed, while 50 thought diagnosis levels were about right.

GP estimates suggested that 81.3 per cent of referred patients had received a diagnosis.

‘Superficial assessments’

One doctor described being “stunned by how superficial some assessments are”, saying significant trauma and adverse childhood experiences could receive insufficient attention.

Dr Liz Clarke, a Greater Manchester GP partner, said assessments should be integrated into wider mental health services.

She warned that separate pathways focusing on individual conditions could miss overlapping problems and lead to inappropriate medication or psychological treatment.

“We should not have to pay twice for what should be the same assessment,” she said.

Dr Bramall argued that rising diagnoses reflected better understanding of ADHD, including among women whose difficulties had previously been missed.

Henry Shelford, the chief executive of ADHD UK, said the survey findings demonstrated “how carefully GPs are referring”.

“GPs know how long the waits are and many are setting the bar high to avoid making the queue worse,” he said.

The survey was conducted between April 13 and May 7 and was unweighted. Pulse described it as a snapshot of GP opinions, rather than a scientific survey.

The Government said Prof Fonagy’s final report would be published shortly and would inform its new national approach.
