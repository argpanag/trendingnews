What would a language test for new Irish citizens look like?

TheTools · 09.09.2026 08:25 · source · permalink

Analysis: Many other countries have introduced language proficiency tests for new citizens so there is ample evidence about their effects

Minister for Justice Jim O'Callaghan is set to recommend an overhaul of Ireland's naturalisation rules to Cabinet. Naturalisation is the process through which immigrants become citizens, a process that culminates in applicants making an oath at a citizenship ceremony. Amongst other measures, O’Callaghan will propose introducing a language proficiency test, to be taken in either English or Irish, as well as extending the minimum residency requirement from five to eight years.

Senior members of government have already expressed support for adopting language testing, including Taoiseach Micheál Martin, Tánaiste Simon Harris, and Minister of State Colm Brophy, who has special responsibility for migration. In pursuing this initiative, the Government is following the example of many other European countries as well as reviving a proposal outlined by Alan Shatter in 2012, during his tenure as Minister for Justice and Equality.

But what exactly is language testing for - and does it work? Many countries around the world have already introduced language proficiency tests for new citizens so there is ample evidence about their effects. In recent decades, naturalisation requirements in many European countries have become increasingly demanding. Perhaps inspired by anxieties about multiculturalism, naturalisation procedures now often feature robust civic integration programmes with an expressly cultural dimension. Language tests often run alongside programmes to familiarise immigrants with the history, values and institutions of their new society.

Those who recommend language testing, such as Aontú's Peadar Tóibín, claim that it supports social cohesion and discourages segregation and there is academic support for this view. For instance, research conducted by American political scientist Sara Wallace Goodman found that citizenship testing in Western Europe can foster cohesion, 'imbue newcomers with a sense of belonging’ and ‘empower individuals to act independently in their host society’.

Another benefit of having proficiency in the majority language is that it empowers people specifically in their capacities as democratic citizens. It enables them to influence public debates, hold politicians to account and collaborate with others in pursuit of shared political objectives. Tóibín also claims that language testing for new citizens could, for instance, reduce translation costs in legal proceedings and for medical appointments.

For a naturalisation policy to make these economic savings, it would have to encourage a significant number of people to learn one of the official languages who otherwise would not have done so, but this seems unlikely. The 2022 Census data indicates that the Irish residents who are most likely to speak English poorly, or not at all, are citizens of other European countries, and this group has little incentive to seek Irish citizenship (and so will not be required to take language tests). Dual citizens, a category that includes people who have already undergone naturalisation without taking a language test, already exhibit high levels of English language competence. In other words, the evidence suggests that the kinds of people most likely to seek to naturalise already have strong incentives to learn English.

Many academics have been strongly critical of language testing. Studies focussing on the experiences of aspiring immigrants indicate that language testing in practice is often felt to be oppressive, exclusionary or discriminatory. Others are sceptical because it is tainted by association with neoliberalism because language learning and testing are sometimes outsourced to private firms, and because the language testing itself betrays an image of migrants as little more than workers. Another objection is that the language tests themselves can be poorly designed and unscientific, not fit for their purpose of accurately measuring applicants’ language skills.

When considering if language testing is the right choice for Ireland, it is helpful to consider how it has been implemented in other societies with more than one official language, and especially countries where one of the official languages is less widely spoken, as is the case for Irish. In the UK, most choose to do so through English, although naturalisation tests can be completed in Welsh or Scottish Gaelic. Without actively encouraging newcomers to learn Irish, one might expect to see a similar pattern here.

In Finland, where there is a small Swedish-speaking population, applicants can take the test in either language and the government officially aims to increase the proportion of applicants choosing the Swedish option. However, many applicants themselves report being unaware about this opportunity, even though for many it could be an easier option. Meanwhile, Luxembourg has three official languages (French, German and Luxembourgish) and requires applicants for naturalisation to pass a language test in Luxembourgish regardless of their proficiency in the other two.

If the Government’s priority was to strengthen the position of Irish, then something like the Luxembourg solution could be an attractive option – requiring newcomers to exhibit a level of competence in Irish whilst taking it for granted that they will become proficient in English, as is already the case. If this was felt to be too ambitious, something resembling the Finnish solution could be worth considering, whereby greater numbers of newcomers could be encouraged to learn Irish in addition to English.

However, it is not clear whether language testing specifically is required under either of these proposals. The position of English, globally and nationally, is already sufficient to incentivise most newcomers to learn it, whilst for Irish the challenge is to encourage more immigrants towards the language, and this might be better met through extending additional opportunities for language learning and cultural engagement. Either way, there are plenty of reasons to be sceptical about whether the language testing proposals under consideration will have a significant effect on the use of either English or Irish.
