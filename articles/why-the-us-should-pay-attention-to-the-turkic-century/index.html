Why the US Should Pay Attention to the Turkic Century

TheTools · 29.10.2026 19:09 · source · permalink

The national flags of the Turkic-speaking states on October 10, 2022. The Organization of Turkish States (OTS) is a suitable partner for the United States’ objectives in Central Asia, writes Luke Coffey. (Shutterstock/MDart10)

A more united Turkic world in Central Asia would strengthen US competition with Russia, China, and Iran.

On October 30, leaders of the Organization of Turkic States (OTS) will gather in Ankara for their 13th summit. Held under the theme “Turkic Century: Unity in Language, Thought, and Deed,” the meeting will focus on expanding cooperation in trade, transport connectivity, digital transformation, culture, and politics. Leaders will also select a new secretary general to guide the organization through its next chapter.

The OTS is an intergovernmental organization that serves as the main platform for cooperation among the Turkic-speaking countries of Eurasia. Then-Kazakh President Nursultan Nazarbayev first proposed the idea in 2006. Three years later, the Turkic Council was established, and in 2021 it became the Organization of Turkic States. Today, it has five members—Turkey, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Azerbaijan, and Uzbekistan—and three observers: Turkmenistan, Hungary, and the de facto Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus.

Together, OTS member states and observers represent about 180 million people and a combined GDP exceeding $2.4 trillion. Together, this would make the Turkic states the world’s 11th-largest economy. These countries are also rich in natural resources and critical minerals, accounting for more than 45 percent of global uranium production (largely in Kazakhstan). Taken as a bloc, the OTS is the world’s third-largest energy provider.

Despite its growing role and expanding importance, Washington pays little attention to the OTS. That needs to change.

Since the collapse of the Soviet Union and the restoration of independence in Central Asia and the South Caucasus, these countries have rediscovered elements of their Turkic identity, language, and culture that Soviet authorities suppressed or subordinated to Russian influence. 

Combined with Turkey’s growing geopolitical ambitions, this renewed sense of identity has helped put Turkic cooperation on an upward trajectory in the 21st century. As a consequence, countries across the Turkic world have deepened cooperation in recent years, particularly in simplifying transportation and customs procedures, pursuing shared cultural initiatives such as adopting a common Turkic alphabet, and even considering closer security cooperation. 

For the United States, this matters because the OTS is becoming, whether by design or not, a de facto pole in an increasingly competitive and multipolar Eurasia. Although OTS and wider Turkic-world leaders would never admit it publicly, a stronger, more independent Turkic pole could help balance the influence of Russia, China, and Iran across Eurasia. For the United States, engagement with the OTS would therefore complement its existing bilateral relationships with countries in the region while supporting the broader geopolitical objective of preventing any single power from dominating the Eurasian landmass. 

The United States is well positioned to engage with the Turkic world. Turkey is a fellow NATO ally, and the Trump administration has been expanding ties with Central Asian countries, several of which also belong to the OTS. Failing to engage with an organization that could help ensure regional balance would be a missed opportunity.

A Turkic century across Eurasia could well be underway in the decades ahead. Two social science theories often used to explain European integration after World War II offer a useful lens for understanding why.

Neofunctionalism holds that cooperation in one area can spill over into others, deepening cooperation and leading to eventual integration. Meanwhile, constructivism helps explain how a shared identity can shape political behavior. Within the OTS, both dynamics are evident: cooperation in trade and connectivity grows alongside efforts to strengthen a shared cultural and linguistic identity.

Washington can achieve quick, easy diplomatic wins with the OTS. For example, once the OTS announces its new secretary general, the Trump administration should waste no time in inviting that person to Washington to meet with Secretary of State Marco Rubio. This would be a simple but important symbolic gesture that recognizes the OTS’ growing influence.

The United States could also establish a working group with the OTS at the officials’ level to identify shared interests and opportunities for future cooperation. Particular attention should be given to trade, energy, and connectivity—issues that already matter to the Trump administration. 

Furthermore, when senior US officials pass through Istanbul, they should set aside a couple of hours to visit the OTS headquarters. Such engagement would require little effort but could go a long way toward building closer institutional ties, mutual trust, and personal relationships.

The OTS is gathering momentum whether or not the United States is involved. Washington does not need an elaborate new strategy to begin engaging with it. It needs only to recognize the trend, take the initiative, and show some diplomatic creativity.

The opportunity is there. American policymakers should seize it.

Luke Coffey is a senior fellow at the Hudson Institute. His work at Hudson analyzes national security and foreign policy, focusing on Europe, Eurasia, NATO, and transatlantic relations. Mr. Coffey served as director of the Allison Center for Foreign Policy Studies at the Heritage Foundation from 2015 to 2022, during which he oversaw a team responsible for most of Heritage’s foreign policy and international affairs work.
