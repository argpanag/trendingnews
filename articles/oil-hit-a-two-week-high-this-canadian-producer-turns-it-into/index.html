Written by Amy Legate-Wolfe at The Motley Fool Canada

Higher oil prices can make a trip to the gas station more expensive and an energy company's cash generation stronger. The shareholder question is what happens to that extra cash after operating costs, investment spending, and debt obligations are paid.

Oil rallied last week as renewed Middle East attacks raised supply concerns. Canadian Natural Resources (TSX: CNQ) offers a way to own production behind that market, with an established dividend and recent evidence that strong prices are reaching cash flow.

Price versus payout

Canadian producers don't all receive the same benchmark price. Crude quality, transportation, market access, currency, and royalties affect what reaches the company. A Brent headline is useful context, not the exact amount CNQ receives for every barrel.

The company's mix is an important one. Oil-sands mining and upgrading, thermal production, conventional oil, and natural gas provide different sources of output. Long-lived reserves can reduce the need to replace the entire production base rapidly, but the assets still need maintenance and investment.

That makes the dividend a capital-allocation decision. Management can use cash to fund operations, reduce debt, repurchase shares, or distribute income. A higher oil price supports those choices, but it doesn't require the board to raise the dividend immediately.

The useful investment argument is therefore a business that can keep operating and funding obligations through a weaker cycle. Buying only because crude had a strong day leaves that question unanswered.

Recent cash did several jobs

Canadian Natural generated approximately $6.9 billion of adjusted funds flow in the second quarter. This is an operating cash measure before the full investment program, so it shouldn't be treated as money entirely available for dividends.

The company paid about $1.3 billion in dividends, repurchased about $1.1 billion of shares, and reduced net debt by approximately $1.6 billion. These figures show that shareholders received cash while the balance sheet also improved. The declared quarterly dividend was $0.63, or $2.50 annualized. Compared with the latest $2.20 adjusted earnings per share (EPS), the payment used about 28% of that quarter's adjusted earnings. Strong oil prices helped the result, so that ratio isn't a permanent promise.

At a recent close of $70.80, a $10,000 budget bought 141 whole shares for $9,982.80. Those shares generated $352.50 annually at an unchanged payment, or $88.13 per quarter.
