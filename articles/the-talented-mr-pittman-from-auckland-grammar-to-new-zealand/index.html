The talented Mr Pittman: From Auckland Grammar to New Zealand's biggest fraudster

TheTools · 15.10.2026 22:12 · source · permalink

Subscribe to listen

Could New Zealand’s biggest fraudster be languishing in a New York prison using an alias? Matt Nippert investigates whether a man jailed last month for running a $175 million fine wine Ponzi scheme is actually one of New Zealand’s most infamous exports, formerly hailing from Auckland Grammar School.

Stephen Burton had procured plenty of jewellery and booked a room at London’s Chelsea Harbour Hotel for Valentine’s Day in 2019. His mood must have surely deflated when Kent Police knocked on his door.

Burton was found in possession of gold bars, ingots and Krugerrands worth $1.7 million, five designer watches worth an average of $50,000 each, 500 lottery tickets, and $125,000 in pound sterling and South African rand. He also had two passports: both in his name, but with different dates of birth.

The then 55-year-old man was reportedly lost for words when police asked him to explain his presentation as an international man of mystery.

Detective Inspector James Derham of the local serious crime directorate said at the time: “The items found in Stephen Burton’s hotel room are worth a tremendous amount of money but he could offer no explanation as to where that money came from.”

From that day, looking both forward and backward, this tale grows taller still.

It would later take Burton to Her Majesty’s Prison Service, flight to – and then extradition from – Morocco, and finally to the Metropolitan Detention Centre (MDC) in New York, where Burton would plead guilty to FBI charges accusing him of running a $175m Ponzi scheme and laundering the proceeds.

And tracing the line that led to that Valentine’s Day bust of Krugerrands and false passports takes us back through the justice systems of Switzerland and Australia, and eventually winds to Auckland District Court and Auckland Grammar School, where NZME understands Burton came of age in the 1980s as a football- and rugby-playing student and subsequent credit card thief, being known then as Andrew Michael Pittman.

This is the story of a man with at least four names and a career of criminal deception that stretches over more than four decades. A man with a talent for disguise and a taste for expensive wine and jewellery. A man who has seen the insides of prisons on at least four continents.

This is a man who appears to have pipped notorious Ponzi schemer David Ross for the title of New Zealand’s biggest fraudster.

This is the story of the talented Mr Pittman.

Auckland Grammar School records Pittman as a student in at least 1980 and 1981, and he played football and rugby but never quite made the school’s first-string teams.

One friend of his from this time told the Herald Pittman was a sporty kid. “He was athletic. He had the gift of the gab, you know. Not thick,” the former friend says.

Pittman apparently came from a well-off family, from whom he developed an early taste for wine.

“I think he liked getting into his dad’s wine cellar,” the friend recalls.

“I remember him being pretty impressed by a Muller-Thurgau bottle design ... I always knew he was shady. He was a good guy, though, and we had some fun together as young chaps.”

Pittman seems to have left Auckland Grammar after completing sixth form, and is said to have begun working as a bank teller. The former friend says it was at this point he began to notice a darker side, when Pittman suddenly adopted high-class fashionwear despite his low-rank job.

“We knew something was going on with the bank. He always was dressing a bit flash, and had patent leather shoes and the best of business suits,” the friend says.

“He said he was addicted to money. He was. He loved money.”

Pittman was, apparently, a man who would do anything for love.

In 1982, the year after leaving school and aged only 18, he racked up his first fraud conviction: theft as a servant.

Pittman then spread his wings and enjoyed an OE, racking up prison time in both Australia and the United Kingdom, before returning to New Zealand in 1991 from Sydney, where he was on bail facing fraud charges.

In Auckland, he posed as a successful wine consultant and registered a company, Andrew Pittman & Associates. He bought a house in Devonport and conducted extensive home renovations – including the installation of a wine cellar – that rated a feature in Kitchen Trends magazine.

It was during this period in New Zealand that Pittman displayed the full breadth of his talents, adopting different disguises and personas when exploiting the financial system in a sustained spree of credit-card theft that netted him $124,816 (about $275,000 in 2026 dollars).

He stayed in hostels, stealing credit cards from fellow guests and donning glasses and growing a moustache to fool CCTV when using them to withdraw thousands of dollars from bank branches. For each theft, he would switch a fresh card for one he’d stolen and exploited earlier, delaying discovery.

His shenanigans prompted police and bank investigations and rated a feature in a 1993 edition of Crimewatch. He subsequently shaved off his moustache and ditched the glasses, but days after the broadcast he was arrested at Auckland Airport attempting to board a flight to Singapore.

His arrest and subsequent conviction rated a story in the Herald. The story notes he was sentenced to two years’ prison and police said they believed he had little or no income from legitimate sources.

Pittman left New Zealand shortly after release, and it is believed he has not visited the country since the 1990s. A 1996 Auckland Grammar newsletter lists him as “missing” from their alumni database and asks readers to help locate him.

His former school friend was also curious about what had happened to Pittman. “I used to search things like his name and ‘con man’ and all this sort of stuff. I knew there’d been some credit card stuff, but I knew that he was always going to be a con man. Yeah, that was his style,” the former friend says.

Court records show Pittman ended up in the United Kingdom, via Switzerland, where he racked up further criminal convictions in both countries for offences such as fraud, attempting to obtain ammunition and impersonating a police officer.

In the intervening decade, British citizen Stephen Burton appeared on the scene in London. He has turned a taste for fine wines into a business of sorts.

Burton founded Bordeaux Cellars in 2010, serving as chief executive, and he conducted investment seminars in Las Vegas and Cancun and beyond, claiming to facilitate loans for wealthy wine collectors.

Those willing to advance funds for on-lending were promised returns of 12%, secured by tens of thousands of bottles of readily saleable luxury wines warehoused by his Bordeaux Cellars.

Burton even appeared on US business television network CNBC in 2013 promoting the scheme, where he was introduced as a “Brit” who wanted to “make lots and lots of dough out of merlot”.

Burton sat behind a table stacked with bottles of rare vintages. “Collectively, on this table, there’s close to US$150,000 or $100,000 worth of wine here,” he told viewers.

The Times says Burton during this period had become well known in wine circles in New York, Hong Kong and London. “Stephen was the guy who would show up with crazy bottles at dinners to impress people with his stories,” a London wine broker told the newspaper.

Another restaurateur, who would later put six figures into Burton’s schemes, said part of the appeal was the continental sound of the Bordeaux Cellars operators. “I was totally convinced. Maybe it was the accent, maybe it was the confidence, but he had me,” he told the Times.

But to a trained ear, it seems this accent – and media before, and authorities after, would describe Burton as a British national – was just yet another disguise.

The Herald had two accent specialists analyse the 85 words spoken by Burton in the CNBC broadcast. While only a small sample size, it was enough to draw conclusions about his true origins.

“There’s a key vowel sound, or phoneme, that Kiwis find really hard to disguise … And that one Kiwi sound is presenting a few times there,” Kirstie O’Sullivan of dramatic consultancy The Voice Practice says.

“Of course I couldn’t say with absolute certainty he’s from New Zealand, but it’s very, very likely.”

This assessment concurs with that of actor and accent coach Sarah Valentine. “I’d say his accent is predominantly New Zealand, with occasional shifts into British-sounding pronunciation,” she says.

“Yeah, the dude is definitely a Kiwi.”

The CNBC host expressed scepticism about why rich people would need to pledge their wine collections to secure loans attracting 16% interest.

Burton himself seemed surprised his loan-for-wine business was viable, telling the network: “I think it’s really strange seeing these people who live in these 20 million homes wanting US$100,000, £100,000 loan or something. I think that it is crazy.”

It sounded crazy. It sounded too good to be true. It was.

That 2019 Valentine’s Day raid occurred around the same time interest payments to the scheme’s investors, who together had contributed $175m, ceased.

Kent Police prosecuted the low-hanging fruit, and Burton was charged and pleaded guilty to money-laundering over his inability to explain his hoard of gold.

While Burton was serving his resulting four-year sentence, across the Atlantic the FBI were hearing from US investors who had lost substantial sums of money with Bordeaux Cellars. In 2022, they formally charged both Burton and co-conspirator James Wellesley with fraud and money-laundering for running a Ponzi scheme that had drawn investor funds from all around the world.

Wellesley, described as Bordeaux Cellars’ chief financial officer, was said to have known aliases of “Andrew Fuller” and “Andrew Templar”. Burton was said to have operated as “Robert Allison”, “Derek Campbell” and – most notably – “Andrew Pittman”.

On announcing charges, US Attorney Breon Peace struck a droll note: “Unlike the fine wine they purported to possess, the defendants’ repeated lies to investors did not age well.”

The FBI found that both the purported borrowers to whom loans were supposed to have been made, and the wine pledged as security, did not exist. Burton and Wellesley had a largely empty cellar and had been using incoming funds for personal expenses and to pay existing investors.

But before justice could be served, there was one final twist in this tale.

Worries of overcrowding in UK prisons during the Covid pandemic led to Burton being released early in 2020. Despite being subject to parole restrictions, he promptly fled the country and was considered at large when US charges were laid.

He is reported to have travelled through Ireland and Spain before resurfacing in exotic fashion: arrested in 2022 trying to gain entry to Morocco using a false Zimbabwean passport. He languished in a North African prison for more than a year while the US pursued extradition proceedings, which ultimately led to him being flown to the United States in late 2023.

(Burton would later, successfully, argue the brutality of the Moroccan correctional system should allow him to count this as time served. “The MDC [in New York] is a high-end Marriott in comparison,” his lawyer told the court.)

Both Burton and Wellesley (who was also extradited to face justice) gave evidence claiming the other was the ringleader.

Burton was the first to cut a deal and disclosed a worldwide network of bank accounts held with Santander, Barclays, Metro Bank, Lloyds and HSBC, and the existence of bottles of wine stashed in various London cellars and real estate purchased in South Africa.

He pleaded guilty in April 2025 and agreed to forfeit assets to US authorities – including the gold found in the hotel room in 2019 – worth $44m.

Wellesley pleaded guilty in April 2026 and was sentenced to 10 years’ imprisonment.

Last month Burton was sentenced to six years in prison. Submissions filed by US authorities flagged his long criminal history and noted his first conviction was in 1982, in New Zealand.

Once this term is complete, it is reported Burton will be deported to the United Kingdom to complete the earlier lag for money-laundering cut short by his ultimately unsuccessful bid to flee that ended in Morocco.

A spokesperson for the US Department of Homeland Security described Burton as “a criminal illegal alien from New Zealand with citizenship also in the United Kingdom”.

The US Department of Justice, which prosecuted Burton, was even more direct in drawing a line linking Burton to Pittman. “It would be accurate to report that it is the same person,” a spokesperson said.

Requests this week for comment from Pittman about his New Zealand back story, sent through his New York lawyer, did not receive a response.

The court heard Bordeaux Cellars resulted in investors’ losses totalling $150m. This sum exceeds the $115m caused by notorious Wellington Ponzi schemer David Ross, a case that was until now the largest known fraud ever committed by a New Zealander.

This was the story of Andrew Pittman, also known as Stephen Burton, the story of New Zealand’s biggest fraudster.

THE LIFE AND TIMES OF ANDREW PITTMAN

  • 1981 – Andrew Michael Pittman finishes sixth form at Auckland Grammar School, achieves University Entrance and joins the workforce.
  • 1982 – Convicted in Auckland of theft as a servant.
  • 1984 – Convicted and imprisoned for fraud in Australia.
  • 1985 – Convicted and jailed in Britain for fraud.
  • 1991 – Flees Sydney for Auckland while on bail facing Australian dishonesty charges.
  • 1993 – Arrested at Auckland International Airport attempting to board a flight to Singapore. Soon afterwards is convicted at Auckland District Court of 96 charges of theft and false pretences and sentenced to two years in prison.
  • 1997 – Convicted of fraud in Zurich, Switzerland.
  • 1999 – Convicted of impersonating a police officer and imprisoned in the United Kingdom.
  • 2001 – Convicted in the United Kingdom of attempting to acquire ammunition.
  • 2010 – Presents as “Stephen Burton”, the founder and chief executive of fine wine finance company Bordeaux Cellars.
  • 2019 – Bordeaux Cellars ceases making interest payments to investors, and “Burton” is convicted in the United Kingdom of money laundering and possession of false identity documents and sentenced to four years’ imprisonment.
  • 2020 – Released from prison early due to Covid-related overcrowding concerns, he breaches parole conditions and flees to Spain and Ireland.
  • 2022 – Arrested on entry to Morocco using a false Zimbabwean passport, he is convicted and imprisoned for immigration fraud and illegal entry.
  • 2023 – Extradited to the United States to stand trial in the Bordeaux Cellars Ponzi affair.
  • 2026 – Sentenced to six years’ prison in the United States after pleading guilty to fraud and money-laundering charges totalling $175m.

Source: FBI sentencing submissions, NZ Herald historic reporting.
