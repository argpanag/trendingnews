Kevin Rennie: In change environment, Blumenthal plows ahead

TheTools · 15.11.2026 10:00 · source · permalink

Breaking News

Wherever two or more are gathered, there will be politics, the brainy British thinker Bernard Crick observed in his influential 1962 book, In Defence of Politics. Politics is everywhere and the conclusion of an election provides no rest.

Sen. Richard Blumenthal provided a reminder of the relentless nature of politics when he celebrated the victories of his fellow Democrats on Nov. 3 and added that he is looking “forward to being on the ballot in 2028….” That was the Greenwich Democrat’s way of letting anyone eyeing his seat that they will have to get through him first.

Blumenthal will be seeking a fourth six-year term, which only Democrats Joseph Lieberman and Christopher Dodd achieved in their long political careers. Each was considerably younger than Blumenthal will be in 2028 when they snagged that historic win. We, the voters, are in an agitated state about our elderly leaders.

Our senior senator’s running two races, one for re-election and the other we all eventually lose–the one with time. Louis Armstong and Jimmy Durante explained the inevitable in 1965 with a jaunty tune, Old Man Time that explains time’s tariff better than a gerontology textbook. “He gives you youth and then he steals it away…He bends your back, dims your eyes, you see less. You quake and shake. Before he’s through, you’re a mess….We never, never get away from old man, old man time.”

If re-elected, Blumenthal will turn 82 shortly after his fourth term begins, several weeks from turning 89 years old when it ends, long past when most people stop buying green bananas. That would be considerably beyond the average life expectancy of a man born in 1946. The former attorney general may have taken some encouragement from Massachusetts Senator Edward Markey winning re-election to the Senate after winning a primary over a young Democratic congressman who emphasized Markey’s age. Markey was born three months after Blumenthal and was elected to the House in 1976 and the Senate 37 years later.

Blumenthal continues to show up at events in the state and elbow his way onto the podium. With the Democrats winning eight, or maybe nine, new Senate seats, Blumenthal will be a member of the majority in January, his authority enhanced. The former U.S. attorney and state attorney general remains a sharp examiner of witnesses and nominees who appear before him in committee hearings.

Since the full-scale Russian invasion of noble Ukraine in February 2022, Blumenthal continues to meet this perilous moment in freedom’s long struggle with tyranny. Blumenthal’s eloquence in the defense of 40 million besieged Ukrainians has been exceeded only by his determination to see them properly armed and supported by other free nations. His forty-two years in elected office were a prelude to this epic battle.

The sudden death of his partner in the pursuit of victory in Ukraine provided a stark reminder that time’s toll may strike without warning. Sen. Lindsey Graham and Blumenthal were Ukraine’s principal advocates in the Senate until Graham died two days after his 71st birthday and one day after returning from a trip to Kyiv.

Blumenthal may have inadvertently provided a primary opponent with a campaign slogan. He said last Monday that Democrats in the Senate need “new energy and ideas,” raising the possibility that he would not vote to promote Sen. Charles Schumer from minority leader to majority leader. Schumer turns 76 on November 23rd. The hunt for energy to inspire and propel new ideas does not begin with a government of old men. Donald Trump, like Blumenthal and Markey, is 80 years old and often provides jarring reminders that “every year” Old Man Time “changes you.”

When Robert Byrd was planning a bid for a ninth term in the Senate from West Virginia at the age of 89 in 2006, he invited Democratic campaign consultant David Axelrod to audition for the campaign. Axelrod, the best in the business, suggested confronting Byrd’s advanced age and diminishing energy with the theme “Three Years of Byrd is Better Than Six Years of Anyone Else.” Byrd, who did not hire Axelrod, won re-election in 2006 and died in 2009, nearly three years into his final term.

The upper ranks of Connecticut Democrats are stuffed with officials elected this month to statewide offices with four-year terms. None would need give up their perch to challenge Blumenthal in 2028. Some may find the temptation to break out of the pack irresistible. While they ponder their capacity for a career gamble, Blumenthal will be reminded of the wisdom of the comedian Jackie “Moms” Mabley, who observed, “It’s no disgrace to be old, but damn if it isn’t inconvenient.”

Political talent can emerge in unexpected moments, and it is often a pleasure to observe. University of Connecticut President Radenka Maric has shown herself an expert player in the lethal arena of academic politics. What a champ at a game others at UConn believed they mastered.

Maric’s contract expires in June. Some of the 21 members of UConn’s board of trustees have not appreciated the independence that comes with experience and wanted to oust the accomplished research scientist. If they succeeded, an interim president would be required while the trustees pondered the search for a successor. Speaker of the House Matthew Ritter was on deck for that high and lucrative honor. His father, Thomas Ritter, a former speaker, has been a UConn trustee for 22 years.

Maric’s supporters among the trustees are not the ones in the small group who have fashioned themselves into an informal executive committee. Maric’s opposition to building dormitories at the school’s Avery Point campus in Groton seems to have irked her detractors into planning a lightning strike against her in December. The nature of the cabal was revealed here two weeks ago.

Major donors to UConn roused themselves in support of Maric. Wider enthusiasm for Maric burst into view in a spontaneous moment this week at the Human Spirit Awards dinner of the Denise D’Ascenzo Foundation. One of the recipients was the late Rabbi Philip Lazowski. His son Alan, a parking garage mogul, accepted the award for his father and used the occasion to deliver some rousing remarks in support of Maric, who was in attendance, to the approval of the crowd. How Denise would have loved providing the forum for a major story.

On Thursday, victory for Maric came quietly when UConn board chair Daniel Toscano resigned after seven eventful years. Gov. Ned Lamont on Friday chose West Hartford’s mayor, Shari Cantor, a Democrat, Maric ally, and longtime trustee as the new board chair, who has patiently waited for her own moment.

On election night, West Hartford Democrats punched above their weight and delivered more than 20,000 votes for Lamont, a total exceeded only by Stamford and New Haven. Cantor’s appointment provided one more reminder that politics never rests.
