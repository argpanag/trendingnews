Trump administration considering diesel export ban

President Donald Trump suggested the administration is considering a temporary ban on diesel exports when asked about some GOP senators calling for the US to stop exporting it to allies.

“I’ve said let’s not send out the diesel. We make a lot of diesel,” Trump told reporters ahead of a bilateral meeting with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky.

“I’ve called for it within my people. I’ve been talking about it,” Trump said of the idea of halting diesel exports.

He then turned to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who said the US is examining the feasibility of such a ban.

“We’re examining that, whether it’s feasible in terms of the overall refining capacity and whether a full or a partial ban would work,” Bessent said.

Some context: The price of diesel, the most important fuel on the planet, has surged 83% so far this year, and it is on track for what would easily be the biggest annual increase since AAA started tracking diesel prices in 2000.

However, researchers warn that an export ban would likely only provide a temporary reprieve from sky-high diesel prices and would backfire in the medium and long run. They said a ban would likely raise prices on friends and allies, jack up gasoline prices, crush US refiners and damage America’s reputation as a reliable energy superpower.

CNN’s Matt Egan contributed to this post.

Kast says "Chile is back" and claims his Trump-style migration plan is working

Chilean President José Antonio Kast used his speech before the UN to critique his predecessors’ security and migration policies, asserting that with his arrival to the presidency, “Chile is back.”

Kast noted that during his first six months as president, the borders have become “more controlled,” claiming that irregular migration has dropped by 86%, and the expulsion of undocumented individuals has increased by 50%.

Kast’s migration plan, dubbed “Border Shield”, was launched at the start of his presidency in March; it mirrors U.S. President Donald Trump’s immigration policy by featuring reinforced borders and increased deportations.

Regarding security, the Chilean president stated that the country’s homicide rate has fallen by 11.5% over the last six months and highlighted that the fight against organized crime is being conducted in coordination with several Latin American nations.

Like other countries in the region with right-leaning governments, Chile is part of a security alliance known as “Shield of the Americas,” an initiative promoted by Trump.

Trump says administration held "very good meeting" with Iranian delegation

President Donald Trump said that members of his administration met with the Iranian delegation at the UN General Assembly for about three hours in what he described vaguely as a “very good meeting.”

“It went very well,” Trump told reporters, sitting alongside Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky. “It was a very good meeting.”

Trump said Iran had two options: “potential greatness” through a peace deal, or “obliteration.”

“It’s two choices,” Trump said. “In one case, it’s obliteration, and the other alternative is potential greatness. It could be a great country.”

Pressed whether US envoy Steve Witkoff and Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner participated in the meeting, the US president didn’t dispute their participation.

“Well, there’s nothing to say. They had a very good meeting, a very productive meeting. And they have another one scheduled in the very near future,” Trump said before asking if Witkoff and Kushner wanted to say anything.

Witkoff, sitting on the couch next to White House chief of staff Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, widened his eyes and stared awkwardly ahead, answering only vaguely when someone asked how he felt after the meeting.

“I feel very good right now,” Witkoff said. “It’s a blessing to work for President Trump.”

Trump then argued that, ultimately, a deal would be beneficial for Iran.

“They had a very good meeting, and we’ll see how it turns out. But certainly, it’s to their advantage to make a deal, and we can do that. It’s preferable, I guess,” he said.

Trump says he thinks Ukraine and Russia will make a deal — but does not provide details

President Donald Trump repeated what has become his common refrain on Ukraine and Russia Tuesday: “I think they are going to make a deal.” But he again declined to provide details on any potential agreement.

Zelensky said he hopes that President Trump will “help us end this war. And I think we need to do it as quick as possible before winter, better before winter.”

Special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner recently traveled to both Kyiv and Moscow to discuss the ongoing conflict. At the time, the White House said those talks were “substantive” and next steps would be discussed in the coming weeks.

Fujimori says Peru joined Shield of the Americas because of its commitment to fight crime

Peruvian President Keiko Fujimori told the UN General Assembly on Tuesday that her country joined the Shield of the Americas, a US-backed security initiative, because of its “commitment” to fighting criminal groups operating across the continent.

“In Peru, we will pursue crime, from the person who carries it out to the network that supports it. We will not back down in the face of those who seek to impose terror over the law. Cracking down on these organizations with the full force of the law is not enough if, at the same time, we do not take on the challenge of developing a better education system,” she said.

Fujimori also used her speech at the UN General Assembly to call on the international body to demonstrate a “genuine commitment to dialogue.”

“The UN must continue to be a meeting place for peoples — a meeting that respects the diversity of realities and traditions, always within the framework of peace, development, and human rights. Therefore, we hope that, in this effort to regain the trust of states, the United Nations will offer an independent, impartial response rooted in a genuine commitment to dialogue — demonstrating that it can forge paths forward even when mutual understanding seems impossible,” Fujimori said.

Fujimori, who has held the presidency for nearly two months, was the second Latin American leader to speak before the UN General Assembly today. She followed Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, who said in his address that the UN was “failing” in its mission to prevent global wars.

In a tone less harsh than that of Lula da Silva, Fujimori urged UN members to “highlight our organization’s most important reason for being: uniting around shared issues, even amidst our differences.”

Lithuanian president: Ukraine’s victory over Russia key to global security

Lithuanian President Gitanas Nausėda said “Russia’s war of aggression against Ukraine” is “the one conflict that poses the greatest threat to the world order.”

Nausėda said that Russia has seen minimal territorial gains and has endured immense losses. Macron, advocate for global cooperation, to give last speech to UNGA as French president

As Emmanuel Macron prepares to make his final address to the UN General Assembly, the world is very different from his first speech in the UN’s New York headquarters nearly 10 years ago.

At the altar of multilateralism, he has become one of the most vocal advocates of international rule of law and cooperation, using France’s diplomatic and military weight to counterbalance the rapid erosion of the norms and architecture of the pre-Trump era.

“We need to restore the terms of trust and respect between peoples,” he proclaimed in his 2024 speech.

From the wars in Ukraine and Israel’s bloody campaign in Gaza, to protecting the oceans and changing the West’s relationship with the Global South, he has been a firm believer the importance of cooperation, rather than competition, on the world stage.

In 2017 he defended the post-WW2 international order – France was indebted to the foreign liberators who had saved her from the grip of Nazism, he said. In 2026, as war rages anew on the European continent, it is France that now leads the way in keeping Ukraine fighting – and the country’s lights on – amid Russia’s constant attacks.

He leaves a complicated legacy. He faced strong headwinds during his decade in power, perhaps most of all the Donald Trump’s attacks on allies, alliances and the UN itself – stripping much of the funding the body’s agencies rely upon, in an era of intense need. Throughout, Macron’s message was clear and frank.

As he told world leaders in 2018, “Nationalism always leads to defeat.”

Qatar emir warns Gulf crisis threatens global economy, urges diplomacy with Iran

Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani warned Tuesday that the Middle East is passing through one of its “most dangerous phases” and called for an urgent diplomatic solution to the Gulf crisis triggered by the war between the US and Iran.

Addressing world leaders at the UN General Assembly, Sheikh Tamim said the conflict was the result of years of postponing solutions through “temporary arrangements and unrealistic policies,” arguing that the region lacks a collective security framework capable of preventing wars and protecting the sovereignty of states.

The Qatari leader said the crisis had demonstrated how instability in the Gulf could have far-reaching global consequences, including disruptions to energy supplies, trade routes and international markets.

“It has become possible for an international waterway through which nearly a quarter of the world’s energy trade passes to be closed,” he said, referring to the Strait of Hormuz.

Sheikh Tamim said Qatar remains convinced that the crisis can be resolved through diplomacy, including reopening the strategic waterway to navigation and returning to negotiations to prevent a broader regional war.

“The opportunity for a peaceful diplomatic solution, which we believe is both possible and realistic, must not be missed,” he said, adding that any lasting settlement must provide security and stability for the entire region, including Iran.

The emir also warned that the economic consequences of the conflict extend well beyond the Gulf, driving up food and medicine prices and affecting people “who have nothing to do with the causes of the conflict.”

Qatar serves as a diplomatic broker between Washington and Tehran, hosting indirect talks, facilitating sensitive prisoner swaps, and managing frozen Iranian funds to de-escalate tensions.

In departure from earlier messaging, Trump calls Venezuela operation a "war"

President Donald Trump spoke about Venezuela in his UN speech as if it were a military foe that the United States had conquered. But that’s not at all how the administration billed the US operation in January.

It instead pitched the operation as a much more limited one focused on law enforcement — namely arresting indicted Venezuelan leader Nicolas Maduro.

“In January, at my direction, the United States military conducted a massive military operation, penetrating deep into the Venezuelan capital, Caracas,” Trump said Tuesday.

He said the military penetrated a “heavily defended and fortified military base, one that was supposed to be flawless against attack.”

He added later that “it was a war,” and he cast the US taking Venezuela’s oil as a situation akin to “to the victors belong the spoils.”

But this bears little resemblance to what the administration initially said.

In his own United Nations appearance on January 5, US Ambassador to the UN Michael Waltz said there was “no war against Venezuela or its people.”

“We are not occupying a country,” Waltz said. “This was a law enforcement operation in furtherance of lawful indictments that have existed for decades.”

Others echoed that line, including Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

“We just don’t believe that this operation comes anywhere close to the constitutional definition of war,” Rubio said in testimony in late January.

The Justice Department also said in a legal memo that a president could order such an operation because he is not constrained in carrying out law enforcement operations overseas.

Gaza has become "most inhumane and shameful concentration camp of our time," Erdogan says

Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan concluded his address at the United Nations General Assembly a short while ago, saying that Gaza “has become the most inhumane and shameful concentration camp of our time.”

Erdogan began his speech by expressing his “sadness” that the president of the Palestinian Authority, Mahmoud Abbas, had been banned from traveling to New York for the assembly.

“This injustice against our Palestinian brothers and sisters deeply offends the conscience of humanity,” Erdogan said, telling the gathered world leaders that his country will “continue to be the voice of a free and sovereign state of Palestine.”

The Turkish leader stressed the dire humanitarian situation in Gaza and the fact that more than 70,000 people have been killed by Israeli attacks there in less than three years.

“Today, Gaza is not only the world’s largest graveyard for children; it is also the place with the highest rate of child amputations in the world,” Erdogan said.

The Turkish leader acknowledged the ceasefire mediated by the US that Israel and Hamas agreed to last year, but he pointed out that this has stalled, and that Palestinians are still being killed in Gaza.
