Goodbye Rejection, Hello Integration

TheTools · 23.11.2026 08:51 · source · permalink

Philadelphians are learning to opt out of AI, while Penn researchers and local educators make the case for bringing it into the classroom responsibly.

It’s become difficult for students to approach an academic assignment and avoid entertaining the question: Can artificial intelligence do it for me?

A growing number of people are answering with a firm no. As of 2025, around 50% of Americans reported feeling more concerned about the prevalence of AI than excited, up from 37% in 2021. As this technology advances at a rapid speed, so does public concern about its usage. In Philadelphia, that unease is reshaping conversations across public education, from library branches to classrooms. 

This past June, Charlie Bailey, a librarian at Free Library’s South Philadelphia branch, held a workshop titled “How to Not Use AI,” propelled by the notion that teaching digital literacy does not have to mean accepting this technology. All 50 spots filled almost immediately. Bailey has since teamed up with the Library Freedom Project, a national network of librarians focused on privacy and information access, to expand the workshop into a “Critical AI Literacy” series. Its sessions cover AI’s limitations, use in fraud, and growing reach into city neighborhoods and public policy.

Inside the classroom, educators are grappling with the same questions. The School District of Philadelphia limits student AI use to grades 9 through 12 and has published guidelines on acceptable use. Despite these safeguards, the shift of schoolwork online since the pandemic leaves these tools a click away, and the burden of policing them still falls on teachers. Parents say the district has left them in the dark, even as New York City and Los Angeles, under pressure from families and activists, each imposed a one–year moratorium on student AI use this fall. A recent Brookings Institution report put it bluntly: for now, the risks of AI in classrooms outweigh the benefits.

But not everyone shares that sentiment. At Penn’s Graduate School of Education, researchers argue that keeping AI out of classrooms is the wrong goal, and that the better one is teaching students to use it well.

Instead of finding ways to deter students from engaging with AI, Penn GSE has facilitated initiatives that encourage responsible AI usage in education. One of these is Pioneering AI in School Systems (PASS), which launched in January 2025 and aims to provide resources for district administrators, school leaders, and educators to incorporate AI into their classrooms by teaching students how to use the technology ethically. In October 2025, GSE used a $1 million donation from Google to expand PASS to five school districts across Pennsylvania, Delaware, and New Jersey. The initiative follows a three–tiered system: guiding district administrators through the “transformative potential” of AI, mentoring school leaders in building implementation plans, and facilitating hands–on exploration of AI tools with educators. These intensive training and workshop methods seek to integrate AI in learning spaces intentionally and responsibly.

PASS works in direct partnership with Catalyst @ Penn GSE, a project that prioritizes an innovative mindset in educational progress. In January, Catalyst was named one of four core partners in the K–12 AI Infrastructure Program, a $26 million effort led by the nonprofit Digital Promise to develop publicly accessible generative AI tools for primary and secondary schools. The program plans to help educators strengthen daily operations and navigate uncertainties through initiatives such as the Philadelphia Writing Project, the Coalition for Educational Excellence, and the Penn Learning Network. Catalyst also guides entrepreneurs through developing plans to impact education. Through Catalyst Community Conversations, the innovation–centered project convenes stakeholders to discuss and create plans for action by connecting leaders with actors responsible for implementing change.

Another project under Penn GSE that evaluates how AI advancements can intersect with educational practices is the Living Laboratory project. Given the rapid pace at which AI has been developing, Living Laboratory constantly collaborates with researchers, educators, learners, and other educational stakeholders to synthesize diverse perspectives into innovative approaches to promoting AI literacy in classrooms. The project, co–directed by Seiji Isotani and Bodong Chen, is guided by five pillars: a people–first approach, responsible techniques for AI usage, a continuous cycle between research and practice, identification of the role of AI for different settings and stakeholders, and the interaction between different programs, research, and partnerships. Through these guiding principles, Living Lab seeks to use AI to advance human potential and serve the public good.

Isotani acknowledges the potential overuse of AI by students, emphasizing the need to find the “sweet spot” where students can balance human agency and decision–making with AI–generated answers. Considering the temptation that students may feel when it comes to having AI do class assignments for them, Isotani doesn’t blame educators who choose to enforce classroom policies that outright ban AI. The problem surfaces, however, when educators ban AI out of fear of its harms without considering its growing prominence in the job market and society as a whole. “It’s interesting for us to understand different perspectives, and check if those perspectives are based on fears that connect to evidence, or fear that just shows observations without facts,” Isotani says. 

The real threat to student success, according to Isotani, isn’t the overuse of AI alone. What educators often overlook is the mindset that students exhibit towards the learning process in general—not thinking learning is important. “Students want to just get to a school … many of them don’t think that doing homework or participating is learning itself,” he says.

Schools, Isotani notes, are built to reward the best grade—prioritizing the final result over the learning process itself. The propensity to cheat in order to achieve success is tempting, and students find ways to cut corners regardless of the technology they have. The solution, then, isn’t to ban new technologies outright. “Everything is related to optimizing your performance, no matter the means,” Isotani says. “What if instead of [fighting] with the technology, we change the incentives of students to actually learn?”

Living Laboratory strives to do exactly this, viewing AI as a tool to help students pursue knowledge while also maintaining critical thinking skills. According to Isotani, ignoring the prevalence of AI ignores the reality that students will be citizens in a society that progressively depends on technological advances. The stakes extend beyond the classroom. Isotani notes that employers increasingly expect applicants to have both AI literacy skills and critical thinking abilities, not one or the other. As AI takes over entry–level tasks, schools and universities have a new responsibility: to prepare students for a workforce that takes advantage of the capabilities of AI, thus expecting a higher skill set from human employees. AI literacy, then, is a critical factor that will prepare graduates for success in the job market. 

The tendency to view AI as a partner and even a facilitator of critical thinking development among students is reflected in Philadelphia–based initiatives. 

Heights Philadelphia is a nonprofit organization that brands itself as being an economic mobility catalyst for Philadelphia youth through promoting education. The organization strives to bridge students from high school to college or directly into career paths that increase their earning potential. Stephanie Pérez–Jarmul, Managing Director of College Success at Heights Philadelphia, believes that AI should not be negated in classrooms. According to Pérez–Jarmul, instead of having a “black and white vision” of the implications of AI, Heights ensures that students are exposed to AI tools in an ethical manner. 

Heights Philly works closely with youth through various initiatives, including providing an annual summer bridge program. The program serves about 60 students, most of whom are first–generation college students, and prepares them for college–level work through rigorous assignments. One of the courses offered specifically hones in on teaching students college–level writing. 

Instead of negating the use of AI, Heights is intentional about setting policies for AI use. With the organization’s central mission surrounding achieving economic mobility for marginalized students, Pérez–Jarmul recognizes how AI could contribute to career success, specifically regarding technical abilities that increase overall efficiency when working with spreadsheets and software. 

At the same time, Pérez–Jarmul still views the individual development of critical thinking skills as being important to career success, and believes that critical thinking and AI use can coexist. “We can encourage students to use [AI], but we can encourage students to be good critical thinkers,” she says.

Balancing AI with student skill development is a task the nonprofit acknowledges as they seek to uplift the socioeconomic standings of marginalized communities. “If you can really hone in on thinking critically, giving honest and clear feedback to others, giving people your true ideas, not ideas that have been filtered through AI systems … this is how you show an employer who you are and what you bring to the table,” Pérez–Jarmul says.

Similar to Living Laboratory, Heights views AI literacy as being a key characteristic of an attractive applicant in a job market that thrives on efficiency.

Despite various organizations’ systemic implementation of AI literacy, embracing the advanced technology still faces significant backlash from instructors and students. From criticizing Penn’s expansion of Claude enterprise access to advocating for the complete prohibition of AI in critical writing seminars, many Penn students’ opinions on AI don’t seem very favorable.

Religious Studies professor Jolyon Thomas echoes similar anti–AI sentiments. Thomas teaches a first–year seminar course, “Penitentiaries to PILOTs: Religion and Institutions in Pennsylvania,” that includes a strict no–AI policy. Thomas describes his class as being a “slop-free course,” meaning he doesn’t want students consuming or producing any AI–generated content. 

Thomas asserts that AI not only leads students to negate the process of thinking critically and developing intentional arguments, but it can also provide factually wrong syntheses of information from various sources. “When a large language model is trying to summarize things, it’s drawing not only from the content of that book, but it’s also drawing from things like what random people say on Reddit, and what random people say on Reddit is usually precisely the opposite of what the scholarly literature has found,” he says. Even when AI has derived information only from scholarly sources, Thomas describes still observing blatant inaccuracies in its synthesis of information. 

Thomas explains that he hasn’t just observed AI giving inaccurate data—he’s also heard from students who feel it impaired their development at Penn. “I get students who tell me that they’ve wasted their time—that they’re only just now figuring out how they could have put in more effort early and learned a lot more … and they’re like … ‘I just ChatGPT’d my way through college,’” Thomas says. At the moment, it was convenient. Later on, however, they realized they had missed out on the very core aspect of learning: the process. 

For these students, it seems that AI usage was a limiting factor rather than a supporting one. “Coming to an elite institution is an opportunity to take whatever privilege you have and to maximize it … and to turn it into even more social privilege, so that you can do good in the world. Why on earth would you squander that opportunity and just do mediocre stuff?” Thomas asks. “Why be average when you could be excellent?”

Thomas, Isotani, and Pérez–Jarmul all recognize the importance of critical thinking development and prioritizing the process of learning instead of focusing solely on results. They also acknowledge the growing presence of AI and seek to respond to the propensity students have to use the technology when it’s readily available. When AI enters the equation, strategies for developing students’ learning and critical thinking become much more nuanced. 

With AI becoming commonplace in students’ toolkits, debates on whether it should be incorporated into classrooms have shifted. Educators seem to be at odds about whether to abolish the technology or to devise meaningful methods to interact with it. As AI becomes widespread across classrooms and workplaces, the relevant question appears to be: What can responsible AI usage look like in practice?
