The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping review – surreally awful prequel to the Katniss Everdeen era

TheTools · 17.11.2026 14:00 · source · permalink

Overwrought and boring, this nadir of a prequel to the Games’ death match saga is nothing but a cash grab for an exhausted franchise

Here is yet another iteration of the utterly exhausted, unbearably extended and now surreally boring Hunger Games franchise, based on the YA bestsellers by Suzanne Collins. This one has another of the series’ convoluted faux-mythic subtitles: Sunrise on the Reaping. What, “harvest dawn” not old-timey enough?

Well, “reaping” in fact refers to an annual process by which ordinary smudgy faced, raggedy clothed people are chosen at random for compulsory participation in this futurist dystopia’s brutal, televised survival contest to the death: a bread-and-circuses nightmare with which the rulers keep the populace in a cowed and pacified state. In this Darwinian process, competitors strategically team up with (and then betray) some players against others in the short term, to stay in the game.

The previous episode had an equally tiresome subtitle, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes – and these two films are a prequel to the Katniss Everdeen era with which we were first introduced to the series, although the actual process by which the Games came into being in the first place is still not dramatised. Joseph Zada is 16-year-old Haymitch Abernathy (played in later-set episodes as a grown man by Woody Harrelson), a cheeky kid who finds himself picked to play. He infuriates the chilling potentate President Snow (played by Ralph Fiennes and resembling George Washington) with a gesture of defiance at the opening ceremony and then with an apparent conspiracy to destroy the Games with explosives. The ruling class are once again a bafflingly bizarre pantomime gallery of grotesques, including Drusilla Sickle (Glenn Close) and smarmy young TV presenter Caesar Flickerman (Kieran Culkin). These characters are unconvincing and unfunny in their unreality, and a bewildering contrast to the Steinbeck-style realism of the rustbelt depression in which the ordinary folk live.

As ever, the “televisual” aspect of this is represented in the retro-style TV screens, but no one is ever shown actually watching these Games; Haymitch and his proletarian, quasi-hillbilly community don’t have TVs, but where is the middle class who are supposedly glued to their sets? Jesse Plemons is marginally less caricatured and tiresomely ridiculous as the organiser Plutarch Heavensbee, the younger version of the role earlier played by the late Philip Seymour Hoffman. But, really, this film has no actual interest and no authentic reason to exist, other than to gouge an income stream out of its fanbase.

The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping is out on 19 November in Australia, and 20 November in the US and UK.
