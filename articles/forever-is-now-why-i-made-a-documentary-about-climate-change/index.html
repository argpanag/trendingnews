Forever Is Now - why I made a documentary about climate change

TheTools · 07.10.2026 15:40 · source · permalink

Acclaimed filmmaker Neasa Ní Chianáin introduces her new documentary Forever Is Now, an immersive, observational documentary that balances a high-stakes look at climate politics with a portrait of a tender, private family life.

I was increasingly alarmed by how few people were talking about climate change. I became the bore who kept raising it, only to be met with glazed eyes or a weary "I just can't deal with that right now."

Most of us believe in climate change, yet we live as if it isn't real. We educate our children as if the future will look like the past, but that world no longer exists.

Watch the trailer for Forever Is Now

We're living in a perfect storm. Weather systems are unreliable, world order is breaking down, and polarisation is unprecedented. Across Ireland, the UK, the US and Europe, populism and the far right are rising, social media is hijacking the news, and truth seems not to matter. How did we get here? Is there a way back?

Beneath the noise, we have far more in common than divides us. We want to protect our families, breathe clean air, drink clean water and give our kids a safe, healthy future. We don't want greedy actors destroying the planet for profit. The challenge is how to unite around those goals

.With Forever Is Now, I wanted to lift the curtain on how politics works and where it meets climate science. With the Greens in government, it seemed the perfect time to see action. It took years to get access, but eventually Eamon Ryan invited us to pitch to his team, and most agreed to take part.

What I didn't realise was that we began filming in 2023, just as the Green wave began to crash and climate had become a cultural trigger. War had broken out in Europe, inflation was soaring, and attention had shifted to paying the bills. The far right seized the moment with vacuous promises, fear and division, blaming immigrants and successfully hijacking the conversation on social media. Their climate denial was helped by Russian and American accounts tied to fossil fuel interests, muddying the waters.

I've known Eamon socially for 30 years. Before politics, he founded Irish Cycling Safaris, a brave move given our weather. I knew he lived and breathed protecting the environment, yet many in rural Ireland seemed to hate him and the Greens. Shouldn't they be on the same side?

Coverage frustrated me. New environmental policies were framed as "How much will this cost the taxpayer?" and never "How much more will this cost if we don't act?" I was also struck by the gap between how highly Ryan is regarded in European political circles and how he's viewed at home. What was really behind the narratives we were being fed?

I hope the film answers some of these questions, shows that there are good people fighting for our planet, and explains a little more about how democracy works. It's on us, the 82% of people in Ireland who are worried about climate change (and I suspect a similar share worldwide), to come together and give our representatives the political capital to fight our common enemy: fossil fuels and those profiting from them.

There are more of us than there are of them. We can, and must, win this. One conversation, one action, one vote at a time.
