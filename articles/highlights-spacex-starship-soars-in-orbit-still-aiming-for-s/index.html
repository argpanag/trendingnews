Highlights: SpaceX Starship soars in orbit, still aiming for ship catch in unprecedented test flight | CNN

TheTools · 13.11.2026 18:47 · source · permalink

What we covered here:

• SpaceX’s 171-foot-tall Starship spacecraft, the upper or second stage of its massive Starship launch system, successfully pulled off an unprecedented landing maneuver back at the company’s Mechazilla launch tower in South Texas. Such a feat has never been attempted in the history of rocketry.

• SpaceX launched this 22-hour mission at 5:47 p.m. ET on Friday during a nationwide ground stop issued by the FAA. Rockets rely on different tracking and navigation equipment than aircraft.

• A few minutes after yesterday’s launch, SpaceX attempted to guide the Super Heavy rocket booster, the bottom portion of the launch system, back to a pinpoint landing at the same launch tower, but the maneuver was ultimately unsuccessful. The company redirected the booster to splash down in the Gulf.

• The Starship spacecraft reached Earth orbit, where it deployed Starlink internet satellites and soared for 22 hours. The ship made 14 total laps of the planet before landing.

Updates

We’ve wrapped our live coverage of SpaceX’s 15th Starship test flight and historic ship catch. Keep following along with CNN’s space reporting here.

Here's what's next for Starship

Flight 15 — as this eventful and historic mission was called — was a massive milestone for SpaceX.

SpaceX’s stunning recovery of the Starship spacecraft marked the first time in history that the upper stage of a rocket has ever been brought back to a pinpoint landing after flight so that it could potentially be flown again.

Mission teams have already begun the process of gathering data from the ship.

Simply having the vehicle back in the company’s possession allows engineers to unlock troves of data. Analysts can look at how the heat shield — a vital component that helped the vehicle withstand temperatures exceeding 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit — held up as Starship came blaring back into the atmosphere. And engineers can get their hands on all the hardware, from the engines to the nose cone, to search for clues about how to improve the vehicle’s performance.

Also ahead: figuring out how quickly a Starship might be able to go from soaring back into Earth’s atmosphere to launching to orbit once more.

Reusability is crucial for SpaceX. And today’s safe return for Starship — and the 22 hours it spent in orbit without any major issues — was a monumental success. It paves the way for Starship to tackle more ambitious projects, including launching a Starship test article next year that will dock with a NASA spacecraft to practice for a moon landing.

SpaceX will also need to make quick headway on figuring out how to land the Super Heavy rocket booster consistently. It’s done so three times in the past but had to forgo an attempt yesterday because the vehicle was short on fuel, according to SpaceX.

A SpaceX spokesperson told CNN this week that the company intends to be reliably reusing Starship vehicles by the time it flies Artemis IV — the NASA mission currently slated for 2028 that could return astronauts to the moon for the first time since Apollo.

SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell celebrates "alien" technology

Gwynne Shotwell, one of SpaceX’s earliest employees who is currently the company’s president and chief operating officer, marveled at today’s spectacle on social media — calling Starship “alien-level technology.”

Musk biographer: This is a big deal for humanity

Famed biographer of tech moguls Walter Isaacson took to social media to marvel at SpaceX’s accomplishment today.

He recalled a moment from six years ago when Elon Musk told his employees about his outlandish plan to return a gargantuan spacecraft from orbit and capture it within the metal arms of a launch tower.

Today, SpaceX realized that vision.

FCC chair Brendan Carr watched today's launch from Starbase

Brendan Carr, chair of the Federal Communications Commission, took to social media to congratulate the company. He appeared to be physically present for the launch, sharing images of the launch tower as seen in Starbase, Texas.

The FCC does play a big part in spaceflight regulation because of its role in policing the use of spectrum, or the radio frequencies that allow spacecraft and aircraft to communicate.

Carr — whose tenure at the helm of the FCC has been marked by by political controversy — has long heaped praise on SpaceX CEO Elon Musk.

Elon Musk congratulates workers

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk took to social media to congratulate the engineers at the company who made today’s sci-fi-esque show possible.

Closed-door meetings and NDAs: SpaceX's Louisiana plans are already controversial

SpaceX is planning to construct a massive, $100 billion spaceport in Louisiana, where the company wants to launch its gargantuan Starship rocket thousands of times per year.

Construction of the spaceport is expected to begin by the end of next year near the southern coast of Louisiana’s Vermilion Parish, with the first launch targeted as soon as 2029, according to an economic development website.

Some locals are already raising concerns.

Rebecca Triche, executive director of the Louisiana Wildlife Federation, said she’s concerned local politicians are “fast-tracking a process that ends up causing harm that can’t be mitigated and certainly can’t be reversed.”

The approval process was also mired in controversy. Already there’s a lawsuit alleging that local authorities violated transparency laws by approving the project in closed-door meetings before the state’s governor even publicly announced the sprawling spaceport.

It’s difficult to know what exactly has been authorized, according to one law firm tackling the issue. And “the nondisclosure agreements signed by public officials, and a new lawsuit over the parish’s decision-making, make those distinctions especially important.”

The campus will be dubbed “Starbase, Louisiana” — borrowing the name of SpaceX’s facilities in South Texas. And while the Louisiana location would be far larger, there are numerous parallels with the Texas location. Much like Texas’ Boca Chica Village was bought out and transformed by SpaceX, a similar fate could await some small communities in Louisiana’s Vermilion Parish.

NASA chief praises "almost magic" landing

NASA administrator Jared Isaacman took to social media to heap praise on SpaceX’s achievement.

NASA has a lot riding on Starship as well. SpaceX has a multibillion-dollar contract with the space agency to retrofit a Starship spacecraft to be used as a lunar lander — potentially for the first moon landing since the Apollo program concluded in 1972.

SpaceX just made history

Never in history has the second stage of an orbital rocket made a controlled plummet back to Earth and landed upright on a launchpad.

In fact, virtually every rocket ever flown has had to discard its upper half, leaving rocket parts to become spaceborne garbage or to burn up in the atmosphere.

SpaceX is trying to change all that.

The company has long said its goal is to make rockets more like airplanes. It’s difficult to push the boundaries of space exploration when you have to throw away multimillion-dollar pieces of hardware after every flight.

SpaceX already has a long history of reusing the first stage of its rockets.

But keep in mind, the Super Heavy booster — which is the first stage of the Starship launch system — reaches speeds of only about 6,000 kilometers per hour (3,728 miles per hour) before it heads back in for landing.

The Starship spacecraft, on the other hand, was traveling at orbital velocities — topping 17,600 miles per hour. That’s nearly 23 times the speed of sound.

STARSHIP LANDS!

In an unprecedented maneuver, the Starship spacecraft just came sweeping down to a pinpoint landing within the arms of SpaceX’s Mechazilla launch tower.

This marks the first time in the history of rocketry that the upper stage of a rocket has returned home from orbit to a safe, upright landing — potentially paving the way for it to be used again.

Today’s success comes after SpaceX engineers sought to caution the public that the company could abort the attempt at any point if issues arose. Company spokespeople repeatedly emphasized that flight controllers would be taking a conservative approach, considering the risks.

Starship had to fly over populated areas of Texas and Mexico to get to the launch tower. It also had to pass over South Padre Island, a popular tourist destination. And the launch tower, Mechazilla, where it made its final touchdown, lies less than 5 miles from the nearest spectators.

Here's how four flaps will guide Starship on a safer flight path

As Starship descends, it will make good use of the four small wings — or “flaps” — on its exterior.

“During reentry, Starship can alter its flight path by moving its four control flaps. This will permit Starship to maintain a safe and known trajectory while guiding itself with high precision control toward Starbase,” SpaceX said in a statement.

Here's the path Starship will take during its nail-biting return

SpaceX said that Starship will do some bobbing and weaving as it makes its final descent back toward the Mechazilla launch tower in Starbase, Texas.

It will pass just north of South Padre Island — a popular spring break and summer vacation destination for Texans across the state.

Then the ship will briefly continue flying over the Gulf before making one final turn home toward Starbase. “This banking maneuver has been demonstrated several times on previous Starship flight tests,” SpaceX said in a statement.

Even as it makes its final descent, SpaceX can still call things off.

“During reentry, flight controllers will have several options to end the reentry early and safely by intentionally tumbling Starship to ensure that it breaks up in the atmosphere before reaching land,” the company said on its website.

“There is also a late-reentry option to send Starship into the Gulf,” SpaceX said, “if any of its systems or the tower itself encounter an issue after reentry that would preclude a safe catch at Starbase.”

SpaceX braces for 21 minutes of terror

Perhaps the most tense moment of the day will come as Starship begins flying over land.

Passing over or near populated areas is extremely risky business.

“Once the rocket is flying over land, there’s 21 minutes where the operators can’t do anything, and so we’re just going to be holding our breath for that time,” said SpaceX’s Lars Blackmore during the company’s webcast on Friday.

“As we get over to the other side of the continent, everyone’s going to breathe a sigh of relief,” he said.

Starship glows with plasma during reentry

The Starship spacecraft is now plunging back into the thick inner part of Earth’s atmosphere while still traveling thousands of miles per hour.

The process of reentry involves violent physics: Starship’s sheer speed means that, when it hits air particles, they begin to rip apart at the molecular level, creating a mesmerizing — but extremely hot — coating of plasma to build up on the vehicle’s exterior. Temperatures can reach more than 2,500 degrees Fahrenheit.

Starship completes its deorbit burn

The Starship spacecraft is nearing the end of its 22-hour voyage through orbit, as mission controllers gave the green light for the vehicle to light up a single one of its engines for a very brief burn.

That engine fire put the vehicle on a path to begin reentry — the most dangerous stretch of mission, during which Starship must plunge back into the thick inner atmosphere while still traveling more than 17,000 miles per hour.

A little-known Texas office has sway in how often Starship can close beaches

Benjamin Flores, Texas’ land commissioner-elect — who is set to become the first Democrat to hold the post in three decades — told CNN that he recognizes “most Texans don’t know the office exists.”

But “the land commissioner basically manages the whole coastline,” Flores added.

That means he has some say in when and how SpaceX shuts down local public beaches — a topic that has long angered residents who are worried that Starship operations are squeezing out their access to beloved Boca Chica Beach.

Currently, rules approved under Flores’ predecessor state that SpaceX has to follow several guidelines:

  • Close the beach no more than five weekends per year and no more than 500 hours annually.
  • Closures are also restricted on holidays, including Memorial Day weekend, Fourth of July, Labor Day weekend, Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Presidents’ Day, Texas Independence Day (March 2), Juneteenth, Veterans Day, Thanksgiving and several other holidays.
  • Starbase officials must provide at least 48 hours’ notice of road and beach closures on its official website.

SpaceX may have “received a lot of leeway” in the past, Flores said. He intends to ensure the company is “compliant with the spirit of the” Texas Open Beaches Act.

“If they’re not, then we will try to work with them as much as possible,” Flores said.

He noted that he has no interest in stifling innovation.

“I think SpaceX has done some amazing things,” Flores said. “And I don’t like doing antics, political stunts or grandstanding. My office should be the most apolitical office in the state of Texas.”

“But my campaign was Texans first,” Flores added.

Starship will fly over populated areas of the US and Mexico. What are the risks?

Launching rockets near beaches is a matter of safety — that’s why most major spaceports across the US from California to Florida are near the ocean.

While SpaceX’s Starship soared over the Gulf of Mexico (also called the Gulf of America by the Trump Administration) as it launched, the ship will come from the opposite direction on the return home.

That path will require Starship to pass over land — across northern Mexico and southern Texas.

SpaceX says Starship will use its flaps to make “small turns” to intentionally veer over “sparsely populated” areas.

But it’s still risky business. The Federal Aviation Administration required that SpaceX demonstrate in its applications for approval for this flight that there would be a less than 1 in a million chance of a casualty.

Notably, however, the company also had to prove it would meet that same standard before a launch in 2025 when Starship exploded over Turks and Caicos during a test flight — and debris struck a car. No injuries were reported.

The company said it’s confident it can bring Starship home without issue.

The company also pointed to NASA’s Space Shuttle as evidence that spacecraft returning from orbit can happen safely. Though, the Space Shuttle Columbia did break up during reentry from the STS-107 mission in 2003, raining debris over parts of Texas and Louisiana.

Starship passes its third and final return checkpoint

Before launch, SpaceX mapped out three potential opportunities for Starship to make an early exit from space if there were signs of issues.

Passing these crucial benchmarks ensures that Starship can make a controlled return home. If mission controllers were to lose contact with the vehicle as it soars through Earth orbit, Starship would be subject to the unpredictable whims of atmospheric drag and space weather — making it extremely difficult to know how and where it would make a final plunge home. That could pose a risk to people or property.

But the spacecraft has made it through each of its milestones: Starship has now passed the third major checkpoint, with flight controllers deeming it’s in good enough health to continue its 22-hour mission.

The next important milestone will come this afternoon as Starship plunges back through the atmosphere and — perhaps — attempts to land back at SpaceX’s launch tower in Starbase, Texas.

If successful, it would be the first time the upper stage of a launch vehicle has ever made a controlled touchdown from which it could potentially be reused.

Starship will begin an engine burn at 3:23 p.m. ET (2:23 p.m. local), if all goes according to plan. The landing attempt would occur just under an hour after the burn.

SpaceX still has multiple opportunities to abort the landing attempt. If there are signs of error, mission controllers can opt to explode the vehicle as it flies over land. SpaceX could also chose to send the vehicle to a splashdown along the Texas coastline.

SpaceX's booster catch has been done before. But it's still novel

On three previous occasions, SpaceX has brought the Super Heavy rocket booster back to the Mechazilla launch tower for a pinpoint landing.

But the last time that happened was in March 2025.

SpaceX has never accomplished this move with the latest version of Super Heavy, called V3, which made its flight debut earlier this year.

Super Heavy V3, like Starship V3, has had stubborn engine issues, which have occasionally caused a few of the booster’s 33 engines to flame out prematurely. Sometimes, that’s affected how softly the booster has landed in Gulf waters as it’s tried to practice the “chopstick” catch maneuver over the ocean.

That happened again on Friday, when one of Super Heavy’s 33 rocket engines went out. SpaceX initially said it could have attempted the landing maneuver with one engine down, but the attempt was ultimately aborted.

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk said on social media Saturday that the booster had been “low on fuel.”

“Slight underperformance of engines on this flight is the reason,” Musk said.

The booster is meant to land with only small amounts of fuel on board.

“So the solution is to improve engine performance and performance consistency,” Musk said, adding that another update will be implemented on the rocket engine production line. The company also plans to adjust how long the booster fires during ascent — perhaps cutting that initial burn one second short to ensure enough propellant remains on board for a clean touchdown.

It's peak migration season for birds in South Texas. Did Starship affect them?

Choruses of chirping birds are unmissable in South Texas right now, as virtually every tree is weighed down with flocks of migrating aviators.

“The Lower Rio Grande Valley (the region where Brownsville is) is one of the most famous areas for birding in the US,” said Dr. Nancy McIntyre, a senior curator of birds at Texas Tech’s Natural Science Research Laboratory.

Spectators from far and wide have gathered in the area this weekend for the Rio Grande Valley Birding Festival.

But yesterday’s Starship launch — and the subsequent explosion of the Super Heavy rocket booster over the Gulf of Mexico — certainly caused a stir.

A bevy of birds could be seen flying away as an ear-splitting sonic boom emanated from the rocket just before it erupted into a massive fireball.

McIntyre said rocket launches can impact birds’ health and migration patterns.

“This is fall migration season, when birds are undertaking a Herculean journey. Anything that disturbs them along their migratory stopover sites causes them to burn energy that they should be using for the journey,” she said via email Saturday.

“I understand that we need satellites, which need launch sites, and that those launch sites cannot just be put anywhere. I’m not sure if launches can be timed so as to not coincide with spring or fall migration or not, but if so, that would be ideal,” McIntyre said.

Local environmental groups have long been seeking legal avenues to curb SpaceX activities in this area.
