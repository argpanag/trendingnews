6 Shows Like 'Game of Thrones,' But Better

TheTools · 26.11.2026 00:11 · source · permalink

There's a reason Game of Thrones became such a television phenomenon. Between its ruthless power struggles, complicated family dynamics, shocking betrayals, and sprawling worlds, it delivered the kind of storytelling that's hard to find elsewhere. But as grand as Westeros can be, it isn't the only place to find gripping tales of ambition, loyalty, and people fighting for control — especially since its final season completely derailed its momentum.

From medieval kingdoms to a pirate stronghold to a modern-day billionaire's boardroom, there are plenty of shows that take those familiar ingredients and create something even better with them. So, if you're still chasing that same Westerosi high, these shows are well worth your time.

1 'Black Sails' (2014–2017)

Set in the early 18th century, Captain Flint (Toby Stephens) and his crew fight to protect New Province Island, a pirate stronghold in the early Bahamas, from the encroaching British Empire. But with rival captains, wealthy merchants, and colonial powers all vying for control, survival depends on shifting alliances and a healthy dose of betrayal.

If what you loved about Game of Thrones was ruthless power struggles, morally complicated characters, and alliances that can collapse overnight, Black Sails should be right up your street. For one, its pirates are every bit as politically calculating as Westeros' noble houses, with the added bonus of naval battles, high-stakes heists, and plenty of double-crossing. Plus, the show even builds towards a surprisingly thoughtful exploration of freedom, loyalty, legacy, and the appetite for power. It's a truly underrated gem.

2 'The Last Kingdom' (2015–2022)

Set during the Viking invasions of England, when a collection of Anglo-Saxon kingdoms are struggling to survive against Danish forces, Uhtred of Bebbanburg (Alexander Dreymon) — a Saxon nobleman — is captured as a boy and raised by Vikings. But now, in adulthood, torn between two cultures, he allies himself with King Alfred (David Dawson), hoping to reclaim his ancestral home while helping to forge a united England.

Like Game of Thrones, The Last Kingdom is packed with shifting loyalties, bloody battles, political maneuvering, and characters whose personal ambitions often clash with the fate of entire kingdoms. Uhtred's divided identity gives the story an especially compelling edge, as he repeatedly finds himself caught between the people and causes he cares about. There are also tons of relationships that bring plenty of the tense political chess matches that made Westeros so entertaining. And with five great seasons and a concluding film, there's plenty of medieval drama to get stuck into.

3 'Rome' (2005–2007)

As Julius Caesar's (Ciarán Hinds) rise to power sparks civil war, Lucius Vorenus (Kevin McKidd) and Titus Pullo (Ray Stevenson) find themselves caught up in a world of political rivalries, shifting allegiances, and bloody struggles for control. But as their lives become intertwined with some of the most consequential figures in history, the show brings the collapse of the Republic and the birth of the Roman Empire to life.

With its ruthless political scheming, complicated family dynamics, and no shortage of brutal violence, Rome feels like a spiritual predecessor to Game of Thrones. The difference lies in how the power struggles are rooted in real history, giving the betrayals and battles an added sense of consequence. Voreus and Pullo also provide an engaging ground-level perspective, balancing the grand political drama with a friendship that's often as messy as the world around them. It's a shame the show only lasted two seasons, but at least it still delivers some of the best historical drama television has to offer.

4 'Succession' (2018–2023)

When aging patriarch Logan Roy (Brian Cox) begins considering his eventual departure from his global media and entertainment conglomerate, Waystar Royco, his children — Kendall (Jeremy Strong), Shiv (Sarah Snook), and Roman (Kieran Culkin) — start maneuvering for control of the family empire. What follows is a vicious battle of egos, backroom deals, shifting alliances, and betrayal, with each sibling convinced they're the rightful heir.

If Game of Thrones was a fantasy about noble houses fighting for the Iron Throne, Succession is easily its painfully recognizable modern-day counterpart. Indeed, the Roy children are every bit as ruthless and dysfunctional as Westeros' most notorious families, while the corporate maneuvering is often just as (if not more) gripping than any sword fight. Add in the sharp writing and extraordinary ensemble cast, and you've got four seasons of backstabbing and distressing family warfare that prove you don't need medieval fantasy to make a succession crisis utterly addictive.

5 'A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms' (2026–Present)

Returning to Westeros roughly a century before Game of Thrones, Ser Duncan the Tall (Peter Claffey) is a humble hedge knight who sets out to prove himself at a jousting tournament in Ashford. Along the way, he takes on an unlikely squire: a clever young boy named Egg (Dexter Sol Ansell), whose true identity carries considerably more significance than Dunk initially realizes.

While Game of Thrones thrives on sprawling storylines, A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms takes a more intimate approach, focusing on an unlikely friendship and those living beneath the realm's grand political machinery. And yet, that smaller scale is precisely what makes it such a refreshing return to Westeros. There's still plenty of danger, humor, and Targaryen intrigue, but the show places greater emphasis on character development and the everyday realities of knighthood. So, if you love George R. R. Martin's world, but can't seem to commit to the eight seasons, this is a welcome change of pace, with every short episode packing a real punch.

6 'Shōgun' (2024–Present)

In 1600s Japan, English sailor John Blackthorne (Cosmo Jarvis) becomes stranded in a country on the brink of civil war, with his arrival catching the attention of Lord Yoshii Toranaga (Hiroyuki Sanada), a powerful feudal lord facing a dangerous political alliance that threatens to destroy him. But as Blackthorne becomes entangled in Toranaga's plans, he forms an unexpected bond with translator Toda Mariko (Anna Sawai), whose own loyalties and personal struggles make her a crucial player in the unfolding conflict.

For anyone who misses the intricate political maneuvering of Game of Thrones, Shōgun is essential viewing. Its competing lords, fragile alliances, succession disputes, and carefully calculated betrayals make every conversation feel like a potential turning point, while its richly realized world gives the story an incredible sense of scale. But what really sets the show apart is its patience. Rather than relying on constant battles and shocking deaths, it builds tension through strategy, cultural conflict, and characters who rarely reveal their true intentions. Think of it as the kind of intelligent, absorbing drama that makes you forget you're watching a historical series. Frankly, we can't wait for Season 2 to arrive.
