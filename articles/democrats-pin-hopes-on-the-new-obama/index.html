Democrats pin hopes on the new Obama

TheTools · 04.11.2026 02:03 · source · permalink

With mainstream appeal and Kennedy-style looks, senator Jon Ossoff is tipped to go far

Jon Ossoff looked every inch the president. Or at least, so thought Barack Obama. After the 39-year-old Democratic senator for Georgia addressed the crowd at one of his final rallies before the midterm elections on Nov 3 – as handsome as ever, with dark eyes and a shock of black hair – the older man grinned. Among the many reasons to vote for Mr Ossoff, Mr Obama told the audience, was the fact “he looks like a Kennedy”.

Cue ecstasy inside the 8,000-seat Southlake Arena in suburban Atlanta. Up in the top stands, a red-jacketed marching band seemed on the verge of blaring their trumpets once again. Women screamed, men shouted, and clips of the speech ricocheted across social media within minutes. (“I am convinced Jon Ossoff should run for president,” posted the singer Nancy Sinatra, whose father was a friend of JFK; “well, now we know who Obama backs,” added Nikolaj, a 26-year-old Serbian physicist.)

Here, perhaps, was the man who could fulfil the dreams of the assembled party faithful, not only of winning a second term in the historically Republican southern state of Georgia, but of returning the Democratic Party to the White House - and the glory days of old.

They could be forgiven a moment of fantasy. Democrats are likely to secure major victories as the midterm results emerge in the coming days, according to the final polling averages. Anger at Donald Trump – driven by high prices, accusations of corruption and the war in Iran – could see Republicans lose control of the Senate, where they currently hold 53 seats to the Democrats’ 47. That would leave Mr Trump a lame duck, trapped inside the Oval Office while the “radical Left Democrats” held both houses of Congress, kiboshing his policies and launching investigations into his friends and cabinet members.

Even if the polls prove wrong, and the “blue wave” fails to materialise, Mr Ossoff is not expected to suffer. He holds a 10-point lead over his rival, Trump ally Mike Collins. The Republican Party long ago redirected its spending to closer races.

The centrist playbook

Mr Ossoff represents a trend, analysts say. “If you look at where the Democrats are doing best, it is with centrist candidates,” said Josh Freed of Third Way, a Washington-based think tank that campaigns for moderate politics. “Ossoff is running away with a state people thought would be incredibly competitive.”

Across the country, “pragmatic, moderate” Senate candidates headed into the final day of voting with a shot at flipping Republican strongholds – from Mary Peltola in Alaska (up 3 points, per the average compiled by Nate Silver, founder of 538), to Roy Cooper in North Carolina (up 10 points), Richard Hamilton in Kansas (up 3 points) and – perhaps most surprisingly – James Talarico in Texas (up 3 points).

They may, of course, fall short. But the playbook Mr Ossoff and other “normal” Democrats have used to make their races competitive, at the very least, could chart the way forward for a party many voters considered hopelessly out of touch after Kamala Harris’s failure to win in 2024.

“The direction of the party is going to be shaped by the people who get elected to office,” Mr Freed said, as well as “whoever the presidential nominee is.”

Fighter with a studied cadence

“Hello Atlanta,” Mr Ossoff bellowed on stage, leaning into the microphone and dragging out the “o” like a boxing announcer. Turning sombre, he asked if there were military veterans, service members or spouses in the audience (“hold your hands high if you can, so we can honour you,” he said). And then came a demonstration of his careful, Janus-faced political style, speaking at one moment to the liberal Left and another to the conservative Right.

Throughout his career, the US’s first millennial senator has excited that portion of the Democratic base that craves a “fighter” – ready to plant one on the jaw of Mr Trump.

His deft touch and Democratic rising stardom invite comparisons to Mr Obama. Ezra Klein of The New York Times said Mr Ossoff had a “visual grammar” that “looks like Obama” in the way that he spoke and positioned himself. Puck said the 39-year-old had a “studied, Obama-like cadence”, while Lily Geismer, a Claremont McKenna College expert on the history of Democratic Party politics, said he had an Obama-like ability to unite the party’s Left and centre factions. Mr Trump, on the other hand, likes to compare him to Pee-wee Herman.

In August, he ignited an entire news cycle around Natalie Harp, the president’s aide (all the president wants to do is “build his ballroom and travel with Natalie,” he said). After this, Davis Ingle, the White House spokesman, described him as “a cringeworthy, feminine theatre kid cosplaying as Barack Obama”.

Earlier, Mr Ossoff coined the phrase the “Epstein class”, suggesting the Trump administration’s populist rhetoric masked deep roots in the paedophile’s world of high-finance, low morals and dodgy corporate dealing.

At the Southlake Arena, each new insult drew cheers. “It’s the most corrupt administration of all time, and everyone knows it,” Mr Ossoff said. “And while the crook president and his crook family rake in billions from all around the world, you pay more for everything.”

Repackaged for social media, attacks like that have helped Mr Ossoff become a fundraising superstar: he took more than $33m (£25m) in the last quarter alone, more than 10 times as much as his challenger. But the money does not go to furthering a liberal agenda. Instead, the senator has long practised a form of political alchemy – using the cash generated by anti-Trump grandstanding to pay for quiet concrete outreach to Republican voters.

It’s always been a difficult balance, admitted Sacha Haworth, a senior aide on Mr Ossoff’s 2017 House campaign, in Flipped: How Georgia Turned Purple and Broke the Monopoly on Republican Power. “You want to persuade Republicans without p---ing them off,” she said.

He has a dedication to his community. “Just about every week, I call up Georgia mayors and pastors and farmers to find out how I can help,” he said. “Because, you see, hurricanes and diabetes and gas leaks don’t check your party affiliation, and when it comes to serving Georgia, I don’t either.”

While other Democrats focus on world-changing programmes (such as “Medicare for all”) or complex social issues and identity politics, Mr Ossoff planted his flag in a relatively unglamorous field: constituency service.

Across the state, residents testify to phone calls from the senator or his office. Outside the Atlanta rally, Gwen McAlpine said he had helped her friend out of Dubai at the start of the Iran war. The federal assistance number didn’t work, and “nobody else in the government would pick up the phone”.

About 180 miles south, in Tifton town hall, city clerk Jessica White said his office was always on hand to help them navigate the labyrinth of federal bureaucracy. “They show up,” she said.

America ‘thirsty for problem-solvers’

In 2024, Mr Trump won Tift County by 36 points. But the city’s mayor, Julie Smith, joined a long list of lifelong Republicans to endorse Mr Ossoff this cycle, stretching from peanut farmers struggling with the high cost of fertiliser to conservative talk-show hosts.

“I think America is thirsty for problem-solvers,” said Geoff Duncan, the former Republican lieutenant governor of Georgia, who split with the party after fighting back against Mr Trump’s claims of voter fraud during the 2020 election. “I think the most important reason Jon Ossoff is so far out in the polls, is that he’s done his job. He has stayed focused on the issues that mattered to Georgians: veterans’ affairs, the post office, affordability issues, healthcare costs, and he did it in a bipartisan way.”

Mr Duncan had his own tales of the senator’s “feverish” work ethic. Every time the Republican governor’s office passed a constituent’s complaint over to the Democratic senator’s team (say, over a bounced cheque, or a struggle with a federal agency) it would get resolved. “Jon would personally call me and tell me the issue was being dealt with,” Mr Duncan told The Telegraph. At one point, the young senator phoned Mr Duncan at 11pm to follow up on the case of a man who was missing his medication in jail. “It was beyond the call of duty, times 10.”

In their own ways, each of the “normal” Democratic Senate candidates has cast themself as focused on local issues, first, foremost and firmly above any ties to the national party. Ms Peltola loves guns. Mr Hamilton backs cuts to Medicaid. Mr Talarico supports drilling for oil and tough border controls. Together, the three come from conservative-coded backgrounds – a no-nonsense fisherwoman, a pastor and a seminarian.

“There’s a lot of evidence that the voters who decide elections thought that the Democratic Party was more extreme than the Republican Party in 2024,” said Mr Freed. To win elections again, the party needed to “focus on meeting their electorate where it was”, remembering that American voters skew broadly “centre-Right”.

Not everyone is convinced. Sitting in a barn filled with pea shellers, scales and baggers, Howard Brown gave Mr Ossoff short shrift. “He represents nothing that I support,” the 61-year-old, fifth-generation farmer said, looking out onto a grassy field dotted with tractors and other machinery. “A lot of people vote [for him] because they like the government hand-outs and they don’t want that to change.”

Fields of cotton and peanuts stretch for miles around the town of Montezuma, punctuated by the white spires of clapboard churches. Georgia farmers such as Mr Brown have endured a hard year: crops were blighted by frost; Mr Trump’s tariffs prompted retaliatory measures against, for example, American pecans; and the price of the diesel needed to run trucks and tractors has shot up by around 70 per cent since the start of the Iran war.

In reply, Mr Trump has promised hand-outs of his own. Some $15bn (£11.5bn) in federal support has been dangled before farmers in recent weeks, including $8bn to offset the soaring fuel price.

“I think Trump’s idea is getting this industry back online,” Mr Brown said. “Tariffs are bringing all this industry back,” he added, citing as-yet unfulfilled plans to build a car-manufacturing plant within 30 miles. “We desperately need it, but it takes time,” he added. “You have to pretty much start over because all our industry is decimated because it went to China or Mexico or wherever.”

‘Weird and dangerous ideas’

Around here, the spectre of “socialism” lurks like a Hallowe’en ghoul – a scare story told, not to children, but to wavering conservative voters. Lacking an obvious attack line against Mr Ossoff, Republicans have painted him as close to the Democratic Socialists of America, the Left-wing group whose members include Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Zohran Mamdani, the mayor of New York.

The senator’s voting record in DC is “far-Left”, said JD Vance, the vice-president, who accused him of only “pretending” to be a moderate while in Atlanta. Mr Ossoff “doesn’t represent us,” chimed in Mr Collins during a televised debate. “He wants to take over the country with his weird and dangerous ideas and his radical friends.”

These friends, according to Mr Collins, include Dr Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic candidate in Michigan’s Senate race, and his associate Hasan Piker, a far-Left streamer who has praised the Chinese Communist Party (he later said he had “issues” and “disagreements” with how it operates) and described Hamas as a “thousand times better” than the “fascist” state of Israel.

In a typical rhetorical move, Mr Ossoff ignored the accusations and went on the attack – Mr Collins, he said, was simply a stooge for Mr Trump and a bigot who had employed “half of the Third Reich to your congressional staff,” a reference to various aides outed for anti-Semitic posts and connections to white nationalists.

Jewish himself, Mr Ossoff has condemned Israel’s conduct of the war in Gaza, without labelling it a genocide. That stance poses no obstacle to a potential run for the White House – neither too hot for moderates, nor too cold for progressives.

And that race begins in earnest later this week. Ms Ocasio-Cortez, who is considering a run herself, transferred $1m of her campaign’s money to Dr El-Sayed, hoping to spur him to victory that would bolster the wider progressive movement. The gamble could well pay off: the latest polling average puts Dr El-Sayed four points ahead of his rival, Mike Rogers.

Yet Democratic strategists expressed fears, as the race tightened, that he was dragging down their chances in a favourable environment for the party – down-ballot candidates, including Jocelyn Benson, the would-be governor, enjoying leads closer to 10 points.

‘We’ve seen this movie before’

On Oct 24, ten days before the election, Mr Obama flew into Detroit for an apparent rescue mission. Polls showed Dr El-Sayed’s support flagging among Jewish and black voters.

On the one hand, the quick-witted and muscular 42-year-old had faced racist attacks from Mr Trump, who called him a “jihadist” who would have to go through security to enter the Senate. On the other, he had neither convincingly allayed fears that he was soft on anti-Semitism, nor that his policies were too Left wing for the traditionally more conservative black voters that make up much of the Democratic base in Michigan.

“We’ve seen this movie before,” Mr Obama told a crowd of thousands inside Cass Tech High School. “I starred in it.” Whenever someone tried to change the “broken status quo”, opponents of that vision would use your “name, or your faith” to suggest that you were “dangerous” and somehow “less trustworthy” and “less American” than they were. Sure, he might not agree with Dr El-Sayed on everything, Mr Obama admitted. But Democrats needed to pull together, like a family, where not all members see eye to eye.

One week later, the reconciliation efforts broke apart in dramatic fashion. Angered at Dr El-Sayed’s refusal to condemn an advert blaming US aid to Israel for high grocery prices, Haley Stevens, his defeated rival in the Democratic primary, endorsed the Republican candidate Mr Rogers.

“It’s gut-wrenching,” said Michael Curhan, a 69-year-old lawyer and lifelong Democrat who had just cast an early ballot for Mr Rogers when he spoke to The Telegraph outside the public library in West Bloomfield, a largely Jewish neighbourhood northwest of Detroit.

“I did it reluctantly – capital ‘R’ reluctantly,” he said. “I hate the attacks on Sayed because he happens to be a Muslim. I just wished he had taken a more moderate position.”

Fresh in his mind, when he voted, were Dr El-Sayed’s initial comments after a 41-year-old man drove a pick-up truck into the nearby Temple Israel synagogue, where 140 children were attending pre-school, and opened fire. No children were hurt; the gunman killed himself after exchanging fire with security guards.

It later emerged that he had family members who were killed in Israeli bombing raids on southern Lebanon. “It was wrong and he never should have done it,” said Dr El-Sayed, before adding the caveat: “Hurt people hurt people.”

If Dr El-Sayed wins by a wide margin and the centrists fall short of expectations, the party may be persuaded to embrace a more hardline stance on Israel, higher taxes on the rich and another push for a healthcare system free to all citizens at the point of use.

Either way, Mr Ossoff will face greater media scrutiny – and Republican attacks – as a torch-bearer for the party’s moderate wing and potential nominee for president. “I think that people think he and [Kentucky governor] Andy Beshear may have cracked the code for how to speak to people in the middle of the country,” said Andra Gillespie, an associate professor of political science at Emory University. “They offer another path for Democrats to take into 2027, which people think might be more viable” than the coastal model of New York progressives.

‘He’s a fake, he’s a phoney’

How Mr Ossoff responds under that glare remains to be seen. An erstwhile producer of investigative documentaries, the senator rarely grants interviews. “Certainly, his image is very controlled,” said Charles S Bullock III, a professor of political science at the University of Georgia.

One Republican attack ad highlights that Mr Ossoff spent $2,900 on hair and make-up this year, according to campaign-finance disclosures, more than any other US candidate. “He’s a fake, he’s a phoney,” Mr Trump said.

In the wealthy Atlanta suburb East Cobb, one man who had just cast a vote for Mr Ossoff – persuaded by the senator’s work to reduce the cost of insulin – nevertheless said he lacked authenticity. “I think it’s not sincere, his act,” said the man, who did not wish to be named. “I think he’s setting himself up for the presidency in 2028.” Even some of his body language struck the 61-year-old, who usually voted Republican, as stagy. “I’m in sales, I read people,” he said. “He’s a poser. He’s always pausing and looking up, like he’s speaking to a higher power.”

While many supporters find Mr Ossoff inspiringly eloquent, critics feel he reaches too often for a $10 word where a $1 option might do (in Southlake Arena, he displayed a fondness for alliteration, hitting out at the “crypto payoffs and the crooked ballroom, the pay-to-play pardons and the billions of your tax dollars backing Prince Don and Prince Eric’s investments”.)

There may be years to practise. Although 82 per cent of likely Democratic voters told a recent survey by the Atlanta Journal-Constitution that they wanted Mr Ossoff to run for president in 2028, he has repeatedly said he has “zero interest” in the White House – much like a young Mr Obama did when he was a senator for Illinois. During the final TV debate, Mr Ossoff promised to serve a full six-year Senate term if he wins re-election (again, much like Mr Obama back in early 2006). He has two small children, whom he would effectively have to “kiss goodbye” to run, Mr Bullock pointed out (Mr Obama’s daughters, Malia and Sasha, were 10 and seven years old respectively when they moved into the White House).

But none of this has been enough to stop the rumours. At the end of the day, he was “hot,” said Asia Lee, a 35-year-old researcher, who felt the party would be wise to pick Mr Ossoff, despite being a socialist herself.

“He’s a white, young man. He’s attractive. He gives folk in the Bible Belt a Kennedy feel. We haven’t had someone like that run for president in a little while.”
