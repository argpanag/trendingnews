The suicide of Manuel Otto, creator of Stick Run, following an incident during a live stream on Twitch.

GENEVA, LONDON, PARIS, MADRID, NEW YORK, LOS ANGELES, SÃO PAULO, BUENOS AIRES, SEOUL, TOKYO, SYDNEY, DUBAI, October 23 — AIRE BSF

The Bavarian police confirmed to the World Organization Bullying Without Borders (Bullying Sin Fronteras) that Otto was found dead on Tuesday in an attic apartment in Breitbrunn am Chiemsee, Bavaria, after emergency services responded to a fire.

The *Stick Run* team had announced his passing on October 21, noting that they were aware of a Twitch broadcast during which Otto had apparently taken his own life. Twitch confirmed that it removed the channel and the video to prevent them from continuing to circulate.

The death of Manuel Otto, a German developer and creator of the popular video game Stick Run, brings an urgent issue back into the spotlight: mental health in the digital world and the responsibility of platforms that connect millions of people.

Otto was 30 years old. He created Stick Run in 2010, when he was just 14, and his game became part of the childhood memories and shared experiences of numerous players. The Stick Run team confirmed his death on October 21 and expressed its condolences to his family, friends, and community.

The news attracted international attention after the game’s team reported that it was aware of a Twitch livestream during which Otto had apparently taken his own life. Twitch stated that it had acted quickly to remove the channel and video to prevent others from continuing to watch the material.

The circumstances surrounding a death of this nature demand caution. It is not appropriate to speculate about what may have led someone into crisis, nor to assume that a public livestream can explain the suffering of an individual. Images, clips, and graphic descriptions should not be circulated in ways that expose the deceased or cause further harm to those who encounter them.

A Responsibility That Goes Beyond Removing a Video

A platform’s response to a livestream showing an emergency is important. But the discussion does not end when the content is removed.

It is also necessary to ask how high-risk situations are detected, what emergency response protocols are in place, how people who witness traumatic events are protected, and what support resources are made available to users who need help.

Otto’s case does not, by itself, establish a causal relationship between digital platforms and his death. It does, however, provide an opportunity to examine—through verified information and without prejudice—the responsibilities that arise when a personal crisis unfolds before a live, connected audience.

Digital communities can offer friendship, creativity, belonging, and extraordinary opportunities. At the same time, they need protective tools, emergency response mechanisms, and a culture that takes the well-being of the people behind every screen seriously.

Mental Health Is Also a Global Awareness Issue

For Bullying Sin Fronteras (BSF), this news belongs to a broader agenda that includes bullying, cyberbullying, suicide, adolescent mental health, addiction, and the risks associated with digital environments.

These issues do not all have the same causes and should not be confused. Yet they share a fundamental need: societies must learn to recognize distress, act responsibly, and strengthen prevention before situations become irreversible.

Awareness does not mean turning every tragedy into a universal explanation. It means investigating, listening, preventing harm, and promoting responses that respect human dignity.

AIRE BSF will continue to follow international cases that can help deepen understanding of these challenges, distinguish confirmed facts from unverified accounts, and encourage responsible public discussion.

Manuel Otto’s death must not become a digital spectacle. It should prompt us to reflect on how we care for people in an increasingly connected world.
