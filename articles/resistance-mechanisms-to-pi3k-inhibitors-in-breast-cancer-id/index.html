Resistance Mechanisms to PI3K Inhibitors in Breast Cancer Identified

TheTools · 23.11.2026 20:49 · source · permalink

Next-generation PI3K inhibitors offer a more targeted approach to treating certain breast cancers, but tumors can still develop resistance. Now, researchers at Mass General Brigham have identified genetic changes that allow cancer cells to evade these therapies, uncovering potential opportunities for earlier detection of resistance and more effective treatment combinations.

The study, published in Cancer Discovery, focuses on hormone receptor-positive metastatic breast cancers carrying mutations in PIK3CA, a gene involved in regulating cell growth and survival.

Although approved PI3K inhibitors such as alpelisib and inavolisib can target this pathway, their use is often limited by side effects, including elevated blood glucose levels. Newer mutant-selective inhibitors, including zovegalisib and tersolisib, are designed to target mutant PI3Kα more selectively, potentially improving tolerability without compromising antitumor activity.

However, understanding how tumors escape these treatments remains essential to extending their clinical benefit.

Blood tests reveal emerging resistance mutations

The researchers analyzed circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) collected before and after treatment from 32 patients receiving investigational PI3Kα inhibitors.

They found that approximately 70% of patients developed additional alterations affecting the PI3K signaling pathway, including mutations in PIK3CA, PTEN, and AKT1.

Several newly acquired PIK3CA mutations appeared in the region where the investigational drugs bind, potentially preventing effective inhibition.

In a subset of patients, these resistance-associated mutations were detectable in blood samples more than two months before clinical or radiographic disease progression.

This suggests that longitudinal liquid biopsies could eventually help clinicians identify emerging resistance before conventional monitoring reveals tumor growth.

“Understanding how tumors evolve under treatment-selective pressure is critical if we want to anticipate the emergence of resistance and extend treatment benefit and overall patient well-being,” said co-senior author Dejan Juric, MD, of the Mass General Brigham Cancer Institute.

Targeting PI3K from different angles

A particularly promising finding was that mutations causing resistance to mutant-selective PI3K inhibitors did not necessarily confer resistance to existing PI3K inhibitors.

The explanation lies in how these drugs interact with their target. While conventional inhibitors bind the enzyme’s catalytic pocket, newer allosteric inhibitors bind elsewhere, creating distinct vulnerabilities to resistance mutations.

In laboratory experiments, combining inhibitors targeting these different sites prevented resistant cancer cell populations from expanding.

The combination also produced more sustained tumor suppression in mouse models than either treatment alone. Notably, the researchers achieved these effects using lower doses, raising the possibility of limiting treatment-related toxicity.

What this means for precision oncology

The findings illustrate how understanding the molecular mechanisms of resistance could inform more individualized cancer treatment.

Rather than relying solely on the mutations identified at diagnosis, repeated ctDNA testing might eventually guide treatment adjustments as tumors evolve.

However, the study involved a relatively small patient cohort, and the benefits of combination therapy have so far been demonstrated in preclinical models. Whether these approaches improve survival or tolerability in patients remains to be established.

Furthermore, resistance involving downstream pathway components such as AKT1 or PTEN may require additional therapeutic strategies.

Nevertheless, the research provides a foundation for developing treatment combinations informed by the specific resistance mechanisms emerging in individual tumors, potentially making PI3K-targeted therapy more durable.
