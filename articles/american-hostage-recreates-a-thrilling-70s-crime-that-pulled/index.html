'American Hostage' recreates a thrilling 70s crime that pulled attention from the Oscars

TheTools · 23.09.2026 05:38 · source · permalink

by Elizabeth Stanton

TOPICS:

In 1977, Tony Kiritsis took Dick Hall hostage for 63 hours, spending much of that time calling into the local Indianapolis radio station WIBC and speaking with journalist Fred Heckman.

The crime was both shocking yet tantalizing to people struggling in an economic recession, as Kiritsis felt he’d been wronged by his mortgage broker Hall, creating a media circus that rivaled Hollywood at the time.

“American Hostage,” the new Prime Video series based on the true story and the scripted podcast of the same name, stars Giovanni Ribisi as Kiritsis and Jon Hamm as Heckman, releasing two episodes every Sunday.

“This is a guy who took another man hostage in in real life, and he was holding press conferences with people standing this close to him, and a loaded shotgun, and we were kind of giggling about it, about just how ridiculous and absurd that was, and how the public and the news corporations bought into it,” Ribisi told PauseRewind’s Elizabeth Stanton.

Ribisi noted the real-life drama was so gripping, “John Wayne was on stage giving his acceptance speech, and they cut away from John Wayne in the middle of that to Tony Kiritsis, who gave a press conference for I think 45 minutes, which is just it says so much to go from Academy Awards, right?”

Kristoffer Polaha, who plays Hall, highlighted the contrasts between kidnapper and victim as sympathies turned back and forth for Kiritsis.

“It's really the tale of two Americas, and so you've got the rock and roll, let's just call it to the left end of it spectrum. Tony Kiritsis’, and he garn[ered] all this favor. People were like, ‘Go, Tony, go stick it to the man!’ And you've got Dick, who's representing this Norman Rockwellian silver spoon, like deep roots, Plymouth, America, that's like this is the right way to do it,” Polaha explained. “And it's the clash of these two, and it's fascinating because it's rock and roll versus classical, and it still happens today. You get like America's divided between these two sides of like, we want tradition, we want this, we want that, or no, let's burn the whole thing down and let's start fresh.”

Mireille Enos, who plays Heckman’s wife Barbara, added, “I think actual life is very, very rarely black and white. We all live in the gray all the time, and we're just kind of stumbling along, trying to make the best choice in each moment, and trying to be of service or not, trying to serve our own interests, So, I think a story like this actually, the preparation is just to be alive and to show up and to try to just keep it as rooted in reality as possible.”

Recreating the look and feel of 1977 was no small task for production designer Caroline Hanania, who actually tracked down equipment used in the era to accurately replicate the radio station.

“Apparently the production designer found someone in Winnipeg whose passion was 1970s radio station equipment, and [they had] like a garage full of it, and repairing it and making sure it works. So all of that stuff in the station worked and was connected,” Ribisi revealed. “The guy came in, and they could have actually broadcast a lot of the stuff that that Jon [Hamm’s Fred Heckman] was saying.”

He added, “They're one of the best production designers I've ever seen and worked with. We were all walking around with our phones when we were back with our families, and were like, look how amazing!”

Learning about how an old school radio station worked before internet or other higher-tech options were available was also a fun exercise in getting into the characters and the era.

“There's a sequence where Ben, played by William Jackson Harper, has gone out to kind of get information, and then he calls into the station on a payphone, and he's like reporting from the payphone, and then he presses the recorder and plays, and like the quote, and like everyone on the radio is hearing this happening from a payphone,” Enos shared.

Ribisi also discovered a love for Indianapolis history in the 1970s and its contributions to pop culture.

“There was there was a lot going on because of the racetrack and the race, it was kind of like a second Hollywood in a way. It was a destination for a lot of celebrities, and so you can think of the nightlife. Apparently, the funk scene in Indianapolis was exploding, and there were a lot of like playboys running around,” he said.

“American Hostage” streams Sundays on Prime Video, with the first two episodes available now.
