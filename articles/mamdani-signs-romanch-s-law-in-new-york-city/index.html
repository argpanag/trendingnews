Mamdani Signs Romanch's Law in New York City

TheTools · 19.11.2026 20:18 · source · permalink

Allie Taylor, Director, Mayor's Office of Animal Welfare: Welcome. Thank you for being here, and I'm proud to introduce Mayor Mamdani.

Mayor Zohran Kwame Mamdani: Good morning, and welcome to City Hall. Before we begin, I would like to take a moment to recognize my dedicated partners in government who have made today possible. I want to thank City Council Speaker Julie Menin, Council Member Chris Marte for having brought this bill to passage. And I would also like to recognize my predecessor, Mayor Bill de Blasio, who is here with us today and who has long called for change to the horse carriage industry.

We are also joined by a number of leaders, both inside and outside of City Hall. With us, we have our Manhattan borough president, Brad Hoylman-Sigal. We have the chair of the Animal Welfare Caucus, Council Member Harvey Epstein. We have the vice co-chair of the same caucus, Council Member Shahana Hanif. We have the chair of our Health Committee, Council Member Lynn Schulman. And as you all saw, I was introduced by the director of the Mayor's Office of Animal Welfare, Allie Taylor.

And as you all know, we are signing a bill named after Romanch Mahajan, and we have been joined by Sovia and Anuj Thukral, family of Romanch Mahajan, who are here with us. We have Betsy Smith, the president and CEO of the Central Park Conservancy. And we have leaders from the ASPCA, Voters for Animal Rights, NYCLASS, PETA, Humane World for Animals and Animal Legal Defense Fund.

Now, for generations, horses lived and worked here in New York City, powering nearly every facet of urban life. They hauled food, coal and mail to New Yorkers across the five boroughs. They pulled omnibuses and streetcars over cobblestones. They lugged the bricks, stone blocks and steel girders that built buildings like this one and transformed our city into the global epicenter of industry and innovation that it is today.

And since 1863, they have been a mainstay of Central Park, carrying New Yorkers and visitors on carriage rides for hire across the iconic landscape.

But over the past two centuries, our city has changed. We associate horsepower today most with the buses and cars that crisscross the five boroughs. When New Yorkers say that they're going to hop on the saddle, they typically mean a Citi Bike, not a living, breathing creature.

Progress is good. It has allowed our city to grow, to modernize, to remove manure from our streets and expand our industrial might. But throughout all of this change, horses have remained in Central Park.

And this summer, our city was heartbroken as we witnessed the tragic death of Romanch Mahajan. Romanch was just 18 years old, a young man who was smart and full of life. I just spoke with his parents, and what they want most is for the world to know who he was as he lived.

He was an avid sneaker collector. He was the best friend of his younger brother. He was someone who was determined to carve out a bright future for himself, for his family, for those that he loved. And he was someone who always treated others with unparalleled kindness and generosity. To hear his uncle say it, it doesn't matter if he knew someone or not, he would always pitch in to help people.

Romanch was taking a horse carriage ride with his family in honor of his high school graduation when the horse that was pulling their carriage bolted. When his mother fell out of the runaway carriage, he leapt out to save her, losing his life in the process.

It is impossible to describe the pain that Romanch's family has endured. No one should ever have to reckon with such suffering.

Our city has changed, and now it is time for our laws to change, too. That is why today I am signing Romanch's Law, legislation that will phase horse carriages out of New York City once and for all by June 1, 2028.

As I sign this bill into law, I do so with a deep sense of both gratitude and obligation toward the more than 200 New Yorkers who make up the horse carriage industry today. Those who have devoted themselves to welcoming the world to our city, to showing visitors to Central Park and who hold a deep love for their horses.

I have the utmost respect for the work that carriage drivers and stable hands do, and for the more than 160 years of contributions they have made to our city.

I had the privilege of meeting with a number of them at the Clinton Park Stables just two weeks ago, where they shared their concerns about what this legislation will mean for their families, for their futures, for their livelihoods. City Hall has heard their stories, and we will continue to do so.

We understand the responsibility that we hold to facilitate a transition that is both fair and respectful to this industry. That is why, as we implement this legislation, we will release a comprehensive transition plan in April 2027.

That plan will include medallion buybacks for owners; a stipend for drivers and stable hands to attend workforce training and career development classes; as well as career counseling and entrepreneurship support. And as we hammer out the plan's details, we will work directly with horse carriage workers to incorporate their feedback at every step of this process.

Throughout this transition, we will take care of the working horses of Central Park as well. The Department of Health and Mental Hygiene will oversee where each horse is sold or placed as they retire.

This is a new day for our city, one that will make the crown jewel of Manhattan's parks safer, while also recognizing the lasting legacy and contributions the horse carriage industry has made in our city. Thank you all so much.

Now, I will pass it back to our emcee for the morning, our director, Allie Taylor.

Director Taylor: Now, please welcome Council Member Chris Marte.

Council Member Christopher Marte: Good morning, everyone. One hundred sixty years ago, the ASPCA was formed right here in New York City, the nation's first animal welfare organization. It was started to address one specific issue: the condition of carriage horses in Central Park.

Nearly two centuries later, we are here to complete that mission. It hasn't been an easy road to get here. Decade after decade, politician after politician, promises were made to end the horse carriage industry, but it's all been talk. But today, we're finally closing this chapter.

The days of horses marching through Midtown traffic and collapsing on the streets will soon be over. No more lives will be lost to this tourist attraction.

From the beginning, I've said that protecting horses has never been at odds with protecting workers. In passing Romanch's Law, we will ensure that the owners and the workers can maintain good-paying jobs while, at the same time, allowing these horses to live out their lives in pasture.

I look forward to continuing this work with the administration and advocates to make sure we see this through.

Speaking of the advocates, I'd like to thank Edita from NYCLASS, Ashley from PETA, Allie Taylor and the thousands of New Yorkers who spent decades fighting for these horses when no one else would.

Now, Mr. Mayor, let's get this bill signed. But before we do that, I'd like to introduce the speaker of the New York City Council, Julie Menin.

Council Speaker Julie Menin: Thank you so much. First of all, I want to thank my colleague, Council Member Marte, who sponsored Romanch's Law. I want to thank all of my colleagues in the Council who are here: Council Member Schulman, Council Member Epstein, Council Member Hanif, and great to see you, Borough President.

And I want to thank the advocates, so NYCLASS, PETA, the ASPCA, VFAR, all of the organizations that have fought for years for this. And Mr. Mayor, I want to thank you for signing Romanch's Law into law.

This is a momentous moment for our city. With over 100,000 people flocking to it each and every day, totaling up to 42 million people a day, Central Park is really an iconic gem in our city. But back in June, two tragic events happened that dimmed its luster.

First, a horse named Denise collapsed and died while giving a ride to passengers. And then, literally two weeks later, Romanch tragically lost his life.

I really want to thank Romanch's family. You came to our first hearing. You've showed up with us at every rally we've had for passage of this bill. I want to assure you that Romanch's life, we will never forget it, and that's why we named this bill after him. And he did not die in vain. And we really want to thank you and your family for your compassion and your courage in working with us on this bill.

And one thing became abundantly clear after these tragedies. Unless we act, and unless we act with urgency, unless we do something to prevent another fatality from happening in our great Central Park, this would continue.

In the days and weeks and months since then, and in the 19 years since the first bill was introduced in the City Council to ban the horse carriage industry, we have not just fought for this moment today. We have fought for all the moments after, when our city will be more humane and safer for it.

And I am so proud of this City Council. This is a City Council that came through to deliver on that humanity, to deliver on that safety for animals and for residents alike.

So I do just want to say that we are also thinking about, as the mayor said, the workers. The workers who have worked for years in this industry. And that is why our bill put forward a thoughtful plan to work with the administration to ensure that the workers have jobs, to ensure, as the mayor said, that the medallion owners are fairly compensated for what they have put into it, to the stable hands, to all that are involved in this industry.

The legislation puts forward a path to ensure that those workers and medallion owners and the stable hands are protected.

So, in closing, I just want to say today is nothing short of a monumental moment because today we usher in a new chapter for safety, for security and, most importantly, for humanity for our great city. Thank you.

Director Taylor: Thank you, Mayor Mamdani. My name is Allie Taylor, and it is an extraordinary honor to stand here as the director of the Mayor's Office of Animal Welfare and as somebody who got their start in animal rights as an organizer working to end horse-drawn carriages in New York City.

Fifteen years ago, I was in Central Park collecting petition signatures in blistering summer heat every single weekend. I would talk to anyone who would stop and listen because I knew that each conversation was a chance to help somebody understand what the horses endured and ask them to be a part of changing it.

Ten years ago, I started Voters for Animal Rights to help people turn their passion for animals into political action. We held meetings in living rooms and basements, we canvassed outside subway stations, we held phone banks, knocked on doors, we built teams in every borough and trained volunteers to lobby their City Council members.

Thousands of people joined this work along the way, and they kept showing up even when progress felt very, very far away. Together, we turned those clipboards of petitions into a movement and that organizing into the law that Mayor Mamdani is signing this morning.

I have the privilege of speaking on behalf of the animal advocates who carried this work for decades.

For the animal rights movement, this moment has meaning far beyond the horse carriage industry. It affirms that animals are individuals whose lives have value beyond what they can do for us, and that our city has a responsibility to protect them. That practice being a tradition does not put it beyond change.

Romanch's Law honors a young man whose life was tragically cut short during his trip to celebrate his high school graduation. To his family, nothing can undo your loss.

For the horses, this law means a future beyond the traffic and noise and the demands of pulling carriages through our streets. A future where their days can be shaped by their needs, with room to graze, run and simply be horses. I know that signing this bill brings a responsibility to see it through, and I take that responsibility personally.

I want to be clear. We have loving, permanent homes available for all of the horses. Sanctuaries and rescue organizations have stepped forward with offers to welcome them and to provide the care they deserve for the rest of their lives at no cost to their owners.

Our office will work to connect owners with those homes and support the horses' transition. We will also support the drivers and the stable hands. They and their families deserve care, respect and practical assistance as they prepare for what comes next. Our responsibility is to help make change work for everybody affected.

To every animal advocate who ever wondered whether your persistence would make a difference, today is your answer. Thank you for holding on to the belief that these horses deserve a different life and for organizing to make it possible. Now, together, we get to bring the horses home.

Without further ado, Mayor Mamdani will now sign this bill into law.

[Mayor Mamdani Signs Bill Into Law]
