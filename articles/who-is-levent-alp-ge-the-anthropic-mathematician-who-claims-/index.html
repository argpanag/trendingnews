Who is Levent Alpöge? The Anthropic mathematician who claims he solved the Riemann hypothesis with Claude

TheTools · 20.11.2026 17:25 · source · permalink

Levent Alpöge is a 34-year-old Turkish-American mathematician and member of the technical staff at artificial intelligence company Anthropic. Already a respected figure in number theory and arithmetic statistics, he became the center of global scientific attention on November 20, 2026, after posting a statement claiming he had discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis—and the broader Generalized Riemann Hypothesis—while collaborating with Anthropic’s AI model, Claude.

While independent mathematicians are still verifying the massive archive of formal Lean 4 verification code behind the claim, Alpöge’s credentials—and his astonishing streak of AI-assisted mathematical results throughout 2026—have forced the academic world to take his work seriously.

Early life, education, and an asteroid named in his honor

Born Levent Hasan Ali Alpöge on April 1, 1992, on Long Island, New York, Alpöge holds dual United States and Turkish citizenship. He grew up attending Half Hollow Hills High School West in Dix Hills, New York, where his scientific talent drew national recognition early on:

  • Teenage prodigy: In 2010, he was named a finalist in the Intel Science Talent Search for a computer-science project using algorithms to analyze MRI scans of blood vessels. A main-belt asteroid—25898 Alpöge—was later named after him.
  • Harvard University (2014): Graduated summa cum laude with a Bachelor of Arts in mathematics and a Master of Arts in physics. His undergraduate thesis on elliptic curves was supervised by Jacob Tsimerman.
  • University of Cambridge (2015): Attended as a Churchill Scholar, completing Part III of the Mathematical Tripos and earning a Master of Advanced Studies (M.A.St.).
  • Princeton University (2020): Earned an M.A. (2016) and a Ph.D. in mathematics (2020) with a dissertation titled Points on Curves, supervised by Fields Medalist Manjul Bhargava.
  • Fellowships and awards: Before joining Anthropic, Alpöge held a National Science Foundation postdoctoral fellowship affiliated with Columbia University and Harvard (2020–2022) and served as a Junior Fellow in the prestigious Harvard Society of Fellows (2021–2025). His honors include the Morgan Prize (2015), Barry Goldwater Scholarship, Sophia Freund Prize, Captain Jonathan Fay Prize, and Thomas T. Hoopes Prize.

Major breakthroughs before joining Anthropic

Long before teaming up with large language models, Alpöge built a strong reputation in pure mathematics, specializing in number theory, arithmetic statistics, modular forms, and points on curves.

  • Sums of two rational cubes (2022): Alongside Manjul Bhargava and Ari Shnidman, Alpöge established the first proven upper bounds on the proportion of integers that can be expressed as the sum of two cubes of rational numbers (showing at most roughly five-sixths can be written that way).
  • Hilbert’s tenth problem (2025): In a landmark paper published in Inventiones Mathematicae with Bhargava, Wei Ho, and Shnidman, Alpöge provided a second proof that Hilbert’s tenth problem has a negative answer over the ring of integers of every algebraic number field—meaning no general algorithm can decide whether polynomial equations have solutions in those rings.

Levent Alpöge’s 2026 AI math streak with Claude

After joining Anthropic, Alpöge became the public face of AI-assisted mathematical discovery, rolling out a rapid-fire series of high-profile announcements—often posted directly to X (formerly Twitter) outside traditional peer review:

  • July 19, 2026 — Jacobian conjecture: Announced an explicit counterexample in three-dimensional complex space to the 87-year-old Jacobian conjecture, discovered using Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5 model. Other mathematicians independently checked the arithmetic within days.
  • August 12, 2026 — Hadamard matrices: Alongside Philippe Voinov and Saul Reynolds-Haertle, used Claude to construct Hadamard matrices for all 12 remaining open orders below 2,000, including order 668—the smallest open case since 2005.
  • August 19, 2026 — Carathéodory conjecture: Announced an explicit counterexample to the Carathéodory conjecture, checked with John-Paul Smith and Claude.
  • August 23, 2026 — Hopf’s problem: Posted a 100-page proof claiming a positive solution to Hopf’s problem by constructing a complex manifold structure on a six-dimensional sphere using an Anthropic model.

The Navier–Stokes priority controversy with OpenAI

Alpöge has also been caught in the crossfire of the escalating rivalry between Anthropic and OpenAI. Working with New York University mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, Alpöge tackled the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem—one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems.

In August 2026, the pair verified a finite-time "blow-up" result for the related 3D Euler equations using both Anthropic’s Claude and OpenAI’s Codex. When OpenAI announced its own full solution to the Navier–Stokes problem on September 8, 2026, using a similar approach, a public priority dispute erupted after Buckmaster alleged OpenAI researchers may have accessed his private pre-publication work stored in a Codex session. On November 20—the same day as the Riemann announcement—Alpöge and Buckmaster posted a new 79-page Claude-assisted paper claiming finite-time breakdown for the unforced Navier–Stokes equations.

The Riemann hypothesis claim: Where things stand

Alpöge’s November 20 claim that he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis—and the broader Generalized Riemann Hypothesis—represents his most ambitious announcement yet.

Rather than submitting a conventional journal manuscript, Alpöge released a four-page overview alongside 14 archive files totaling roughly 239 megabytes and 175,187 Lean source files (with the full written argument reportedly exceeding 250,000 pages).

While an independent verification project confirmed the files compile against Lean’s standard math library, the full automated check of the proof itself remains underway. Even if the Lean verification succeeds, under Clay Mathematics Institute rules, any Millennium Prize contender must still be published in a qualifying venue and withstand at least two years of scrutiny from the global math community.
