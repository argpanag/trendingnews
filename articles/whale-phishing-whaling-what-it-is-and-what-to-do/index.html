Whale Phishing (Whaling): What It Is and What to Do

TheTools · 11.11.2026 09:25 · source · permalink

Whale phishing, also called whaling, is a targeted scam message sent to a senior person, such as a chief executive or finance chief, or written in their name to someone who can move money or data. It is a real, documented kind of fraud, not a virus you remove; if you already paid or clicked, call your bank and your IT team now and follow the order on this page.

What whale phishing is, and what we know about it

Whale phishing is a scam method, not a program and not one website. A criminal picks a senior person (the "whale") or picks someone who works for one, researches them and sends a message that looks personal and urgent. The aim is money, a password or confidential data. Nothing is installed on your device by the name itself, so there is nothing to uninstall; the work is to stop the damage a message has already done.

This page has no website of its own to test, because the subject is a name for a type of attack. Our usual server request to an address was therefore not made (our record says: not requested, not a plain DNS name).

We built the page from a security glossary, a security vendor's explainer, the US Federal Trade Commission's advice and what the FBI says about business email compromise. We say plainly where a source is a search summary rather than a full page.

  1. 1

    The name

    "Whale" means a big target. TechTarget describes a whaling attack as a type of phishing that goes after high-profile employees such as the chief executive officer or the chief financial officer. Other writers also include board members and other senior people with access to money or secrets, so the exact line is not fixed.

  2. 2

    How it differs from ordinary phishing

    Ordinary phishing is sent to thousands of people with a generic text. Whaling is personal. The sources we read describe messages that use the target's name, job title and other facts gathered from public places, and in some cases a custom web page built just for the victim.

  3. 3

    How it differs from CEO fraud

    The two terms overlap and writers use them loosely. In CEO fraud the criminal pretends to be the executive and writes to an employee. In a classic whaling attack the executive is the one who receives the message. Both belong to the family the FBI calls business email compromise.

    What this means for you

How to tell the whale phishing (whaling) message is fake

Why executives and their staff are chosen

Leaders are valuable because they can approve money, open doors and speak with authority. Their staff are valuable because they will act on a leader's word. Abnormal AI notes that lower-level staff can also indirectly expose an executive through a security lapse, so training only the top is not enough.

  1. 1

    Authority is the lever

    People rarely question their chief executive. A message that appears to come from one gets less checking than one from a stranger.

  2. 2

    Public information makes the message believable

    Titles, travel, deals and names of colleagues are published online. The sources say executives can limit this by setting privacy limits on personal social media.

  3. 3

    Assistants and finance teams are the usual path

    The person who pays invoices, handles payroll or manages an executive's mailbox is a target of the same campaign, because they can act without the executive seeing it. This is our reading of how CEO fraud is built.

  4. 4

    Hacked accounts are better than fake ones

    A message from a real mailbox passes the sender check. When one account falls, the attacker can write to the whole circle.

Check a suspicious message before you act

Start with one question: did the message ask for money, a login or confidential data? If yes, do not reply to it and do not use any link, number or attachment in it. These checks do not delete anything and need no tools.

  1. 1

    Call the person on a number you already have

    Use the company directory or your own phone contacts, not the number in the message. The FTC gives the same advice for companies: contact them using a phone number or website you know is real.

  2. 2

    Look at the full sender address

    Open the details of the message and read the address, letter by letter. Compare it with the real company domain. A single changed letter is common. Remember that a hacked real address would pass this check.

  3. 3

    Ask yourself the FTC question

    The FTC asks whether you have an account with the company or know the person who contacted you. If the answer is no, treat it as likely phishing.

  4. 4

    Hold the payment

    A real executive will accept a short delay for a call. A scammer will push. Any change to bank details of a supplier should be confirmed by a call to a known number.

  5. 5

    Do not open attachments or sign in through the message

    Go to the site by typing its address yourself or by using a saved bookmark. Never type a password into a page opened from the message.

  6. 6

    Report it inside the company

    Tell your security or IT team, even if you did nothing. A message sent to one executive was probably sent to others.

What a company can do so one message cannot move money

The strongest defence is a rule that does not depend on spotting the fake: nobody can move money or send staff data on an email alone. The ideas below are ours; the sources we read stress training beyond the executives and limiting public information.

Habits that make you a harder target

The rule that would have stopped most of the cases above fits in one line: a request for money or secrets is checked on a second channel before it is done.

Do

  • Verify money and data requests by calling a number you already had.
  • Read the full sender address on anything unusual.
  • Go to sites by your own bookmark, not by a link in a message.
  • Use a password manager and a second sign-in step.
  • Report suspicious messages, even when you did nothing.
  • Limit what you share about your role and travel in public.

Don't

  • Do not reply to a message that asks for secrecy and speed.
  • Do not buy gift cards or change bank details on an email alone.
  • Do not trust a display name by itself.
  • Do not open an attachment you did not expect, even from a known name.
  • Do not treat a perfect looking message as proof it is real.
  • Do not stay silent out of embarrassment: speed saves money.

How a whaling attack works, step by step

Almost every case follows the same order: research, a believable identity, an urgent request, and a payment or file that cannot easily be taken back. Seeing the order helps you stop it at any step.

  1. 1

    The criminal picks a target and studies it

    Abnormal AI says attackers choose a specific target and research their life and work to find a trusted person to imitate. The material is public: a company website, a professional network profile, a press release, a conference talk, a post on social media.

  2. 2

    A believable sender is built

    The message may come from a lookalike domain that differs by one letter, from a hacked real account, or from a free mail address with the executive's display name. The display name is easy to fake. The address behind it is where the difference shows.

  3. 3

    The request is urgent and private

    Typical wording asks for speed and secrecy: a confidential deal, an overdue invoice, a payment that cannot wait, a file the sender needs now. The pressure is the tool. It is meant to stop you from calling someone to check.

  4. 4

    The money or the data moves

    The target, or the employee who obeys, makes a wire transfer, buys gift cards, changes the bank details of a supplier, types a password into a fake page or sends a file of staff records. Bank transfers are hard to reverse once they have cleared.

  5. 5

    The criminals keep going

    A paid request is often followed by a second one. A stolen password is used to read mail and to write to others from the real account. The FBI's own congressional report on this fraud notes incidents in which state actors used compromised executive email accounts to set up fake virtual meetings; we saw only that one line of it.

What a whaling attack can cost

Reading about it costs nothing. The risks below apply when someone answered, paid, clicked or typed a password.

  • High

    A payment that cannot be recovered

    A wire transfer to a criminal's account can be moved on within hours. The FACC and Crelan cases in the sources involved tens of millions of dollars.

  • High

    Loss of an executive's mailbox

    If a leader typed a password into a fake page, the attacker can read mail, see who pays whom and write from the real account, which makes later messages far more believable.

  • High

    Leaked staff or customer data

    A message that pretends to be the chief executive can ask payroll for a list of staff tax forms. That data is then used for identity theft against many people at once. This is our reading of the pattern; the sources we read describe the data and money requests in general terms.

  • Medium

    Malware, if an attachment or link was opened

    The FTC says that if you opened a link or attachment that may have installed harmful software, you should update your security software, run a scan and remove what it flags. Not every whaling message carries malware; many carry only words.

  • Medium

    Damage to the person and the firm

    At FACC, the chief executive and finance chief lost their positions. Insurance, banks and regulators may all ask questions.

  • Low

    Nothing, if you only read the message and did nothing

    An unopened or deleted suspicious message, with no reply, no click and no payment, has done no harm by itself. Report it so that others are warned.

How to recognise a whaling message

No single sign proves a message is false, and a perfect message may still be false. The signs below are the ones the FTC and the vendors describe, and a few that we add from the pattern and mark as our reading.

What to do after the whale phishing e-mail

If you only received the message and clicked nothing, step 3 is all you need.

If you clicked the link or typed anything on the page it opened, do every step, starting with the password.

  1. 1

    Step 1: Change the password you typed on the fake page

    If you typed a password on the page the whale phishing message opened, assume the sender has it. Go to the real site by typing its address yourself and change the password there, choosing one you have never used.

    Change it anywhere else the same password was used, and sign out all other sessions if the service offers it. Any browser on Windows 11 or Windows 10 will do, as long as you do not follow the e-mail's link.

If you already replied, paid or clicked: do this in order

Speed matters most in the first hours, and most of all for money. The order below is our judgement from the FTC's advice and from how the fraud works. We did not have the FBI's own page to quote, so check your bank and your national fraud service for the time limits that apply to you.

  1. 1

    If you sent money, call your bank at once

    Ask the bank to try to recall or freeze the transfer and give them the account details you paid to. Do not wait to see whether the person replies. Every hour lowers the chance of getting money back. We do not know the exact time windows in your country, so ask the bank for them.

  2. 2

    Tell your IT or security team and your manager

    Do this even if it feels embarrassing. The firm may need to warn its bank, its insurer, its lawyers and the other staff who got the same message.

  3. 3

    Change passwords from a different, clean device

    If you typed a password into a page from the message, change it now, starting with email, then banking and work systems. Turn on sign-in with a second step. Ask IT to end all open sessions, because a stolen session can survive a password change.

  4. 4

    Check the mailbox for hidden rules

    Attackers who get into a mailbox often add a rule that forwards or hides mail. Ask IT to look at forwarding and rules on the account. This is our reading of the pattern, not a step from a source we read.

  5. 5

    If you gave personal numbers, follow the FTC

    The FTC says that if you gave a scammer your Social Security, credit card or bank account number, you should go to IdentityTheft.gov for steps based on what you lost.

  6. 6

    If you opened a link or an attachment, scan the device

    The FTC says to update your computer's security software, run a scan and remove anything it flags. A clean scan is one data point and does not prove the device is safe. If the device is a work machine, give it to IT.

  7. 7

    Report it

    The FTC asks people to report phishing at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, to forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org, and to forward phishing texts to 7726 (SPAM). In the US, business email compromise is also reported to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center; outside the US, use your national cybercrime service.

  8. 8

    Warn others and keep the evidence

    Keep the original message with its full headers and any payment receipts. They are what the bank, the police and the insurer will ask for. Tell the people who deal with the same sender name.

Does it matter whether you use a phone or a computer?

The method works the same on both. A phone adds one problem: the small screen often shows only the display name, which hides the sender's address. Tap the name to see the address before you act.

Questions about whale phishing (whaling)

What is whale phishing?

Whale phishing, also called whaling, is a phishing scam aimed at senior people such as a chief executive or finance chief, or sent in their name to staff who can act on it. TechTarget describes it as a type of phishing that targets high-profile employees.

The message is personal: it uses the target's name, job title and other facts found in public. The goal is usually a money transfer, a password or confidential data. It is a method of fraud, not a program, so there is nothing on your device to uninstall unless you also opened a link or an attachment.

Is whale phishing a scam or a virus?

It is a scam. The message tries to talk you into paying, signing in or sending data. It is not a virus by itself, because words in an email cannot infect a device.

Some messages also carry a link or an attachment that can install malware, so the two can meet.

The FTC says that if you opened a link or an attachment that may have installed harmful software, you should update your security software, run a scan and remove anything it flags. If you did nothing but read the message, no harm has been done.

What is the difference between whaling and spear phishing?

Spear phishing is a message aimed at one person or a small group, using real details about them. Whaling is described as a form of spear phishing where the target is senior:

  • an executive
  • a board member
  • another high-value person

Ordinary phishing goes to thousands of people at once with a generic text. In everyday use the words overlap, and the sources do not agree on exactly how senior a target must be. For your own safety the difference does not matter much: the checks and the first steps after a mistake are the same.

What is the difference between whaling and CEO fraud?

In CEO fraud the criminal pretends to be the chief executive and writes to an employee, often in finance, asking for a payment or data. In a classic whaling attack the executive is the one who receives the message. The sources use both terms loosely and some treat them as the same thing.

Both belong to what the FBI calls business email compromise, which it also calls email account compromise. If you got a message that seems to come from your chief executive and asks for money, treat it as this scam and call the person on a known number.

What are real examples of whale phishing attacks?

The sources we read name several from 2016. Crelan Bank in Belgium is said to have lost 75 million dollars in a whaling attack built on CEO fraud. The Austrian plane parts maker FACC is said to have lost 56 million dollars, and its chief executive and finance chief lost their jobs.

A Snapchat employee received a fake email from someone posing as the chief executive. Mattel reportedly almost lost 3 million dollars. We did not check these figures against court records, so treat them as reported.

How can I tell if an email from my boss is real?

Do not judge by how it looks, because a well made message can be perfect and can even come from a hacked real account. Call your boss on a number you already have, not the one in the message, and ask.

Read the full sender address, not only the display name. Be careful with urgency, secrecy and any request for a payment, gift cards or changed bank details. The FTC says to contact a company using a phone number or website you know is real, and the same rule fits a person.

I sent money after a message like this. What now?

Call your bank immediately and ask them to try to recall or freeze the transfer, giving the account details you paid to. Then tell your IT or security team and your manager, so the firm can warn its insurer and others.

Every hour lowers the chance of getting money back, and we do not know the time limits in your country, so ask the bank. Keep the message with its headers and the payment proof. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or, in the US, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, and to your national fraud service elsewhere.

I clicked a link or typed a password. Do I need to remove something?

If you typed a password into a page from the message, change it from a different, clean device, turn on a second sign-in step and ask IT to end all open sessions.

If you opened an attachment or a link that may have installed harmful software, the FTC says to update your security software, run a scan and remove anything it flags.

A clean scan does not prove the device is safe, so give a work device to IT. If you entered a Social Security, card or bank number, the FTC points to IdentityTheft.gov.

How big is the problem and where do I report it?

The FBI counts business email compromise, the family that contains CEO fraud and whaling, in the tens of billions. Summaries of FBI data give over 43 billion dollars in exposed losses for June 2016 to July 2019 and 51 billion for 2013 to 2022, with 2.7 billion in 2022.

Exposed losses include attempts as well as real losses. We did not find a newer figure. Report phishing to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, forward emails to reportphishing@apwg.org and texts to 7726 (SPAM).
