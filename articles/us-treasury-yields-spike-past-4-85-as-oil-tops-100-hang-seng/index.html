US Treasury Yields Spike Past 4.85% as Oil Tops $100; Hang Seng Index Plunges Below 25,000 — BigGo Finance

TheTools · 10.09.2026 04:25 · source · permalink

Hong Kong stocks, after weakening through early September, faced renewed pressure from external factors in early trading on September 10. The Hang Seng Index opened down 289 points at 24,985, breaching the psychologically important 25,000 level with a decline of approximately 1.15%; the Hang Seng Tech Index opened 1.39% lower. Intraday, the Hang Seng Index's decline widened to approximately 1.38%, while the Hang Seng Tech Index fell as much as 1.75%.

The core pressure behind this sharp selloff came from external sources: US long-term Treasury yields surged to their highest since November 2023, compounded by escalating Middle East tensions pushing international oil prices above the $100 mark. Market expectations that the Federal Reserve would maintain its hawkish stance—or even hike rates—this month intensified sharply, with offshore dollar-denominated assets bearing the brunt.

Surging US Treasury Yields Emerge as Primary Killer

The US Treasury Department recently announced an expansion of its long-term Treasury buyback program, planning to purchase up to $6 billion in longer-dated Treasuries. However, this scale was notably below market expectations of $8 billion to $10 billion, and yields rose rather than fell following the announcement. Around September 9, the 10-year Treasury yield briefly hit 4.857%, while the 30-year yield touched 5.315%; in early trading on September 10, both eased slightly to 4.843% and 5.294% respectively, but remained elevated. The 2-year Treasury yield stood at approximately 4.425%.

With long-end rates hitting their highest since November 2023, valuations of Hong Kong-listed tech and growth stocks came under direct pressure. CICC analysis noted that Hong Kong tech and innovative drug sectors—the most sensitive to risk-free rates—saw their valuations compressed first under upward pressure on discount rates.

Oil Above $100 Reinforces Hawkish Expectations

Middle East tensions continued to escalate, with US-Iran tit-for-tat strikes intensifying and Iran establishing a so-called "sanctions zone" at sea. Brent crude settled up 3.36% at $101.21 on September 9, breaking through the $100 psychological barrier; WTI crude hovered near $96. In early trading on September 10, Brent crude rose another 0.13% to $101.34, while NYMEX crude gained 0.56% to $96.59.

US President Trump stated he would not alter his Iran strategy for the midterm elections and was not currently seeking negotiations with Iran. He also claimed oil prices would fall sharply after the midterms, but the market clearly did not ease its concerns.

Rising oil prices pushed up inflation expectations, in turn reinforcing market pricing for "the Fed to remain hawkish or even hike." Some institutions reassessed the probability of rate hikes after the Jackson Hole meeting, giving a range of 57% to 60%. For Hong Kong stocks, this constituted a double blow: risk appetite declined on one hand, while cost-side and imported inflation expectations rose on the other.

Three-Day US Equity Slide Drags Down Chinese ADRs and Tech Stocks

All three major US indices fell on September 9, with the Dow dropping 405 points, or 0.77%, to 52,380; the S&P 500 falling 37 points, or 0.48%, to 7,636; and the Nasdaq declining 168 points, or 0.64%, to 26,253. The Nasdaq Golden Dragon China Index dropped 2.09%.

Hong Kong tech stocks, highly correlated with Chinese ADRs, opened broadly lower: Alibaba (9988.HK) fell 2.4%, Baidu (9888.HK) dropped 2.4%, Meituan (3690.HK) declined 2%, Xiaomi (1810.HK) lost 1.3%, while Tencent (700.HK) and JD.com (9618.HK) both fell 0.9%, directly weighing on the Hang Seng Tech Index.

Notably, Apple (AAPL) officially launched its first foldable phone, the "iPhone Duo," but the stock closed down 0.3% at $315.34 on September 9. Hong Kong-listed handset component makers showed no improvement, with Sunny Optical (2382.HK) down 2.3%, BYD Electronic (285.HK) down 1.2%, Cowell e Holdings (1415.HK) down 0.4%, while AAC Technologies (2018.HK) was flat.

Domestic Sector Headwinds Compound Pressure

The consumer and dining sector saw notable declines on September 9. Haidilao (6862.HK) plunged more than 9%, with Jiumaojiu, Super Hi International, and Helens following lower. Haidilao's interim results showed revenue growth but nearly flat net profit, with its flagship brand posting negative revenue growth and a table turnover rate of 3.9. Multiple institutions downgraded the stock, dragging down sentiment across the services consumption sector.

Haidilao subsequently confirmed that SP NP, a member of its controlling shareholder group, sold relevant shares representing 4.65% of total share capital. Following the sale, the controlling shareholders' combined stake dropped to approximately 45.49%. The company emphasized that the sale was purely for the shareholder's own funding needs and financial arrangements—a private matter at the shareholder level with no connection whatsoever to the group's business, operations, financial condition, or development prospects. The stock traded at HK$10.4 (approximately $1.3) in early trading on September 10, up 0.6%.

In tech and AI hardware, memory semiconductors saw significant declines on September 9, with Chinese vendors Jiangbolong and Fourier falling more than 10%. A UBS report showed global chip sales fell 9.8% month-over-month in July, with memory chips down 16.3%. While long-term DRAM and NAND outlooks remain positive with potential shortages, near-term earnings expectations have been revised downward. Optical communications and PCB sectors showed divergence rather than broad-based selling, reflecting a market that is screening based on "high valuations plus earnings verification."

In the Chinese property sector, early September saw the market digesting the "828" real estate policy adjustments, with higher thresholds for existing home sales and pre-sales raising concerns about developers' cash flow turnover and declining internal rates of return. Yuexiu, C&D, Longfor, and China Resources Land all weakened. Bank stocks, by contrast, showed relative resilience and even hit new highs, indicating capital rotation between "property chain selloff" and "dividend defensiveness."

Resources and energy sectors bucked the trend. Oil prices above $100 lifted CNOOC (883.HK) against the market, while copper and aluminum strengthened as LME copper prices hit new highs. Overall, Hong Kong stocks were not experiencing indiscriminate selling, but rather a structural adjustment with "tech, consumer, memory, and property weak; energy, non-ferrous metals, and select high-dividend names strong."

Liquidity Pressure Intensifies

Southbound capital inflows have slowed markedly. According to CICC statistics, cumulative net southbound inflows in 2026 to date total approximately HK$370 billion (approximately $47.2 billion), less than half of the same period last year. Historical data shows that when southbound inflows accelerate, the Hang Seng Index averages a 1.7% gain over the subsequent 20 days; when inflows shift from accelerating to decelerating, the index averages a 1.6% decline. While August still saw net inflows in the tens of billions of Hong Kong dollars, the pace was clearly below 2025 levels, weakening the support for heavyweight stocks.

On the foreign capital front, EPFR data showed overall foreign net outflows from Hong Kong stocks of approximately $50 million in a given week, compared to net inflows of $880 million the prior week. Passive foreign capital turned to outflows while active foreign capital saw modest inflows, suggesting this was not a comprehensive replenishment by long-term capital, but rather short-term capital reducing positions amid interest rate uncertainty.

Share unlockings and financing dilution are also squeezing existing liquidity. Hong Kong's unlock peak in the second half of 2026 is concentrated in July, September, and December, compounded by IPOs, refinancing, and large placements, creating localized pressure on existing capital. Xunzhong Communications (2597.HK) announced on September 10 plans to place 11.19 million new H-shares, representing approximately 8.42% of the enlarged share capital, at HK$28.2 (approximately $3.6) per share—a discount of more than 19% to the previous closing price—raising net proceeds of approximately HK$314 million (approximately $40.0 million). Approximately 38% of proceeds will be used for computing power business infrastructure and R&D environment construction, with about 22% for computing power platform and product development. The stock opened at HK$35 (approximately $4.5), unchanged.

On September 9, southbound capital net bought HK$3.72 billion (approximately $474.4 million) of Hong Kong stocks, with Baidu receiving the largest net buying at HK$1.735 billion (approximately $221.3 million), CNOOC receiving HK$451 million (approximately $57.5 million), and Alibaba receiving HK$438 million (approximately $55.9 million). Innovent Biologics (1801.HK), Zhipu (2513.HK), and CSPC Pharmaceutical Group (1093.HK) saw net selling of HK$737 million (approximately $94.0 million), HK$387 million (approximately $49.4 million), and HK$352 million (approximately $44.9 million) respectively.

Sharp Correction or Trend Reversal

The current Hong Kong stock decline more closely resembles a high-beta sharp correction triggered by "external rates plus geopolitics" rather than a comprehensive earnings collapse. Post-interim results, non-financial earnings expectations saw slight upward revisions in recent weeks, with innovative drug and high-dividend sectors revised upward, while the downward revision in the internet sector is narrowing.

Under CICC's base case scenario, the Hang Seng Index range is seen at 26,000 to 27,000 points. A break below 25,000 would warrant monitoring whether liquidity deteriorates further. A true "trend reversal" would require multiple conditions to materialize simultaneously: US Treasury yields breaking above 5%, sustained net selling by southbound capital, continued downward revisions in internet earnings, and another collapse in property sales. At present, these conditions have not yet converged.

The resilience of energy and high-dividend sectors indicates this is not a systemic credit crisis, but rather a liquidity- and risk-appetite-driven selloff in the weighted index. The market's transmission mechanism is clear: rising US Treasury yields push up discount rates, hitting the Hang Seng Tech Index, innovative drug names, and high-valuation growth stocks first; a stronger dollar prompts foreign capital to return to dollar assets, pressuring Hong Kong stock liquidity.

Policy moves by other central banks also warrant attention. The market is simultaneously pricing in rate hike expectations for the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan. If the yen continues to hike rates, it could trigger carry trade unwinding and deleveraging, with Hong Kong's high-beta positions being reduced first.
