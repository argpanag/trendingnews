Permanent daylight saving time is not the fix we think it is - The Boston Globe

TheTools · 19.10.2026 19:12 · source · permalink

Congress is on the verge of getting rid of the current policy. But morning light and evening light are not interchangeable.

Susan Kohl Malone is an assistant professor at NYU Rory Meyers College of Nursing.

“Spring ahead, fall back” may soon be a thing of the past. In July, the House of Representatives, Republicans and Democrats alike, voted to end the twice-a-year ritual. I share this instinct as a nurse and a sleep scientist. Changing clock times is not good for us, but the bill now in the Senate would lock Americans out of sync with the sun.

The Sunshine Protection Act would make daylight saving time permanent. That sounds wonderful: more light after work and school, longer evenings. But there’s a catch — making daylight saving time permanent does not create a single additional minute of daylight. The sun does not change when it rises and sets; the clock simply relabels the hours. Permanent daylight saving time would take an hour of light from winter mornings and shift it to the evenings.

That trade is the problem, because morning light and evening light are not interchangeable. Our bodies run on internal clocks that are set by the sun, not the clock on the wall. Morning light is the master signal: It resets our internal clocks each day and tells the brain to trigger a surge of cortisol. Cortisol, a hormone released by the adrenal glands, is the body’s built-in wake-up call that primes the body for activity by raising heart rate and blood pressure, and releasing blood sugar into the bloodstream for energy.

Because our internal clocks run slightly longer than 24 hours, they depend on this daily reset and it works best in the first few hours after we wake. Permanent daylight saving time would move clock time an hour ahead of sun time. A December sunrise in Boston would slip past 8 a.m., and past 9 a.m. in parts of the country like Indianapolis, long after most people have started their commute or school day. Thus, our internal clocks would drift out of step with our work and school schedules.

We have run this experiment before. In 1974, amid an energy crisis, the United States made daylight saving time permanent. Enthusiasm collapsed after a single winter of children waiting for school buses in the dark, and Congress reversed course by a landslide. Human biology hasn’t changed since then, either.

Under permanent daylight saving time, early-shift workers and commuters would start their days in darkness, out of sync with their body clocks, a mismatch linked to drowsy driving and workplace injuries. But the people who would pay the steepest price are the ones with the least say in the matter.

Adolescents, whose biological clocks naturally run late, would be hauled to school before their brains are ready to learn. As a high school nurse, I saw the consequences of this in a district spanning a large geographic area — where 7:30 a.m. start times meant bleary-eyed teens were boarding school buses as early as 6 a.m. Forced awakening during critical REM-rich sleep in the early morning impairs memory consolidation, mood regulation, and executive functioning. It undermines academic performance and is linked to poor mental health.

The fix for those 6 a.m. bus stops, some argue, is later bells, not standard time. But we need both. Later school start times give teens back sleep their biology demands, and standard time keeps that gain real. Under permanent daylight saving time, an 8:30 a.m. bell would feel like 7:30 by the sun, quietly erasing years of hard-won progress.

None of this is a defense of the status quo. The switch itself does real harm. In the days after we spring forward, heart attacks rise. One analysis found a 24 percent jump in heart attacks on the Monday after the change. Strokes, traffic crashes, and workplace accidents climb, too. Ending the switch is worth doing. The only question is which time we keep.

On that, the scientific community is united. The Society for Research on Biological Rhythms and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, along with sleep and circadian researchers across the country, have reached the same conclusion: If we are going to lock the clock, we should lock it on permanent standard time. Standard time is the setting that keeps our schedules aligned with the sun and with our own biology. It is, quite literally, the natural choice.

The Senate now has a chance to get right what the House did not. Senators should ditch the switch and land on standard time. A brighter winter evening is a poor trade for a darker, groggier, unhealthier winter morning. Our clocks should serve our biology, not override it.
