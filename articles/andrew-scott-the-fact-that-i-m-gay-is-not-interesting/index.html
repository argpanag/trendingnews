Andrew Scott: The fact that I’m gay is not interesting

TheTools · 12.11.2026 23:30 · source · permalink

Irish actor says playing field for LGBT performers is ‘starting to level out’

Andrew Scott has said that being gay “isn’t interesting” as he prepares to portray fellow actor Ian Charleson in a new film.

He stars in Elsinore, which depicts Charleson’s performance as Hamlet at the National Theatre in 1989 while he was dying from Aids-related complications.

Speaking to British Vogue about how his sexuality has rarely been discussed despite his portrayal of significant gay characters on screen, the 50-year-old said: “Well, it isn’t very interesting.”

“What was so insidious was that Ian and his predecessors had something to contribute to the film and television industry but weren’t given the opportunities in the same way, or else they were given the opportunities but they were also given a sort of silent choice: You can have that, but you have to conceal a huge part of your identity in return, while their straight counterparts didn’t have to make that sacrifice,” Scott added.

The Irish actor, who also starred as the “hot priest” in the TV series Fleabag, explained that at the time, straight actors were “celebrated” for their sexuality, rather than that part of their identity being “concealed or derided”.

“I do feel that the playing field is starting to level out,” he added, stating: “There’s that great expression that prejudice doesn’t survive proximity. If you see people, you become less frightened of them.”

Elsinore, which opened the London Film Festival last month, will be released in the UK on Jan 29.

Critics who saw the film hailed Scott’s performance as Charleson, who died at 40 years old in 1990. He has become a favourite to win the Oscar for best actor next year.

Charleson’s final role was as Hamlet at the National Theatre, in which he appeared in 18 performances. He was primarily a theatre actor, but is best remembered on screen for playing Eric Liddell in the 1981 film Chariots of Fire.

Speaking about honouring Charleson with his depiction, Scott said: “In some ways my fear was that we would fetishise Ian’s death rather than celebrate his life.

“And actually his decision to play Hamlet at the very end of his life, to nail his colours to the mast in that way, when time was running out, is so life-loving.

“As he says, I’d rather die of Hamlet than of Aids. That’s somebody who wants to live. That’s important for me to say.”

Scott, who has taken on other gay roles in All of Us Strangers opposite Paul Mescal and in the Netflix series Ripley, added: “The fact that the current generation of young gay people feels a freedom from that is wonderful.”

He also said the institutional silence surrounding the Aids crisis should be where “the shame lies”.

“There was no information, no help, all this silence. The terror of that silence is the thing that offends. That should be where the shame lies,” he said.

See the full feature in the December issue of British Vogue, available from Tuesday Nov 17.
