The 10 Best Gangster Movies That Don't Star Robert De Niro or Al Pacino (#2 Won the Grand Prix at Cannes)

Robert De Niro and Al Pacino have become so associated with gangster movies that imagining the genre without them feels impossible. From De Niro's cold-blooded performance in Goodfellas and his unforgettable turn as Vito Corleone in The Godfather Part II to Pacino's explosive performances in Scarface and The Godfather, both actors helped define expectations for a great crime saga. Their commanding presence can elevate any story about larger-than-life mob bosses and tragic downfalls.

However, the gangster genre has never belonged to only two actors. Across decades and continents, filmmakers have explored criminal empires through different lenses, from the ruthless streets of 1930s Chicago to the underworld of Hong Kong. These movies prove that a great crime movie can be just as gripping without De Niro and Pacino. Some turn the genre into art-house prestige, while others remain criminally overlooked despite influencing countless other crime thrillers.

'White Heat' (1949)

Cody Jarrett Is a Gangster Who Can't Be Tamed

What Makes It Great: James Cagney doesn't just play Cody Jarrett as a dangerous criminal; he makes the character feel like a live wire that could spark at any moment. His performance feels volatile thanks to his physical energy, sudden mood swings, and unsettling attachment to his mother. Director Raoul Walsh blends the hard-edged aesthetic of 1930s crime movies with modern character beats, turning White Heat into an unpredictable film.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: The gangster movie had already become a major Hollywood genre by the time White Heat arrived, but Cody Jarrett presented a ferocious version of the criminal antihero. It's number four on the American Film Institute'slist of the greatest gangster films​​​​​​, and Cagney's climactic line, "Made it, Ma! Top of the world!" remains one of cinema's most recognizable gangster quotes.

'The Long Good Friday' (1980)

The British Gangster Epic That Saw the Future

What Makes It Great: The Long Good Friday has a remarkable setting. John Mackenzie captures London at a moment when its old industrial identity is giving way to ambitious redevelopment, and the movie's locations make that transition feel real. The contrast between glamorous restaurants, casinos, and yachts and the battered East End streets gives the movie its own text. The screenplay also features dry, distinctly British humor.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: Many American mob movies focus on the rise and fall of a criminal empire, but The Long Good Friday makes the gangster's ambition collide with a changing city. Harold's plan to redevelop London's Docklands anticipates the area's transformation into a major commercial center. That makes it more gripping. Later movies like Layer Cake and Sexy Beast owe something to Harold Shand's blueprint.

'Eastern Promises' (2007)

Cronenberg Gives the Russian Mob Its Own Visual Language

What Makes It Great: David Cronenberg's Eastern Promises is a crime thriller with a tactile sense of place. Its muted color palette contrasts Anna's restrained world with the crime family's garishly opulent spaces. The tattoos add another memorable visual detail, as they carry information about rank, criminal history, and belonging. While the violence here is brutal, the real achievement is Cronenberg's control over tone and detail.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: Eastern Promises stands out among the familiar Italian-American mafia movies by examining the Russian criminal fraternity known as the Vory v Zakone. Instead of revisiting the mythology perfected by The Godfather and Goodfellas, it explores the mob through rituals and traditions that are rarely depicted with such seriousness in Hollywood.

If movies like Casino dazzle viewers with excess before exposing the rot underneath, Eastern Promises skips the glamor altogether. It shows that the modern gangster movie can thrive without nostalgia.

WHERE TO WATCH

'Scarface' (1932)

The Gangster Blueprint Everyone Else Borrowed From

What Makes It Great: Watching Scarface today is fascinating because so many of its conventions have become genre staples​​​​​​​. Howard Hawks' classic still crackles with the same energy. He uses sharp editing, bold compositions, and bursts of machine-gun violence to make the criminal world feel both chaotic and organized. Paul Muni is also unforgettable as Tony Camonte, an ambitious, reckless, funny, and dangerous protagonist who influenced Pacino's Tony Montana decades later.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: Scarface is one of the foundational gangster movies of the sound era, and its influence reaches far beyond the 1983 remake. Produced by Howard Hughes and directed by Hawks, the movie drew on real Chicago gangsters, especially Al Capone, while turning the criminal rise-and-fall story into a template. Brian De Palma's version is more famous than the original, which has overshadowed the importance of Hawks' movie.

WHERE TO WATCH

'Gangs of Wasseypur' (2012)

A Gangster Epic With a Soundtrack All Its Own

What Makes It Great: Directed by Anurag Kashyap, Gangs of Wasseypur builds an entire criminal civilization for the screen. The story spans multiple generations while focusing on themes of revenge and shifting power structures.

Rajeev Ravi's cinematography captures the beauty and crime of small-town northern India, while the production design marks the passage of time through changing posters, household objects, and everyday details. It stars Manoj Bajpayee and Nawazuddin Siddiqui, whose characters are funny, petty, and frightening​​​​​​​.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: Gangs of Wasseypur is overlooked outside Indian cinema circles because its scale can be intimidating. It was released in two parts, runs for more than five hours, and follows rival families in a coal-mining town. Instead of concentrating on a single gangster's rise, the movie follows a society with its own history and politics. It was selected for Cannes' Directors' Fortnight in 2012, and its international reception challenged assumptions about contemporary Hindi movies.

'Infernal Affairs' (2002)

The Gangster Thriller Built on an Unusual Premise

What Makes It Great: Infernal Affairs locks two men into a deadly game where every conversation carries life-or-death consequences. Andrew Lau and Alan Mak use editing and music to make that premise feel like a psychological trap. The casting is impeccable, too. Tony Leung and Andy Lau, two of Hong Kong's most charismatic stars, deliver performances that turn the cat-and-mouse thriller into a poignant examination of loneliness and self-erasure.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: Infernal Affairs has a brilliant double-mole structure, which flips the usual police-versus-gangster setup into a duel of wits. Both men are living false lives, and the movie explores how prolonged deception damages one's identity, beliefs, and loyalty. Its success also helped reinvigorate Hong Kong cinema at a difficult moment for the industry, spawning a forgotten trilogy and inspiring Martin Scorsese's The Departed.

WHERE TO WATCH

'Miller's Crossing' (1990)

The Coens Make Every Conversation a Puzzle

What Makes It Great: Miller's Crossing is a gangster movie built entirely on language and atmosphere. The Coen brothers' screenplay draws on the hard-boiled tradition of Dashiell Hammett, filling the movie with clipped exchanges, dry wit, and characters who rarely reveal what they actually mean. The supporting cast is genuinely enjoyable, too, with John Turturro's twitchy Bernie and Marcia Gay Harden's quietly complicated Verna deserving praise​​​​​.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: Miller's Crossing is easily one of the Coens' most underrated crime movies. The duo's later projects, such as The Big Lebowski, Fargo, and No Country for Old Men, still dominate conversations about their work. Unfortunately, Miller's Crossing remains overlooked.

Its initial theatrical run was a commercial disappointment, earning around $5 million only(via Box Office Mojo)​​​​​​​. However, its influence and craftsmanship are undeniable. It's a richly written, beautifully controlled classic gangster movie worth rewatching.

WHERE TO WATCH

'The Departed' (2006)

Scorsese's Crime Thriller Is in Permanent Panic Mode

What Makes It Great: Martin Scorsese made several masterpieces before The Departed. Rather than revisiting the criminal empires of Goodfellas and Casino, he constructs a pressure cooker here. Every character is lying, every institution is compromised, and every scene feels seconds away from falling apart. The South Boston accents, neighborhood loyalties, and uneasy relationship between police and organized crime give the movie a personality that goes beyond its undercover premise.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: The Departed is one of those rare movies to achieve both massive commercial success and awards-season acclaim​​​​​​. It won Best Picture, Best Director, Best Adapted Screenplay, and Best Film Editing at the 2007 Oscars, and Scorsese won his first Oscar for directing after years of nominations. It represents the endpoint of a genre evolution that began with White Heat. The gangsters are no longer battling rivals; they're fighting their inner demons​​​​​​​.

WHERE TO WATCH

'A Prophet' (2009)

Prison Life Becomes a World of Its Own

What Makes It Great: Directed by Jacques Audiard, A Prophet feels realistic. It turns the prison into a living system with its own rules, economy, and shifting alliances. Stéphane Fontaine's cinematography uses cramped corridors and harsh lighting to create an ongoing sense of pressure. Tahar Rahim delivered a phenomenal performance as Malik El Djebena, whose actions are marked by moral compromise.​​​​​​​

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: A Prophet won the Grand Prix at the 2009 Cannes Film Festival. It is a major recognition for a movie that reimagines the gangster origin story from inside a French prison.

The prison setting gives the movie a fresh perspective on organized crime, while its focus on assimilation and survival makes it more than a conventional rise-to-power story. Crime movies have often been dismissed as commercial entertainment.​​​​​​​ A Prophet showed that gangster storytelling could achieve artistic depth, too.

WHERE TO WATCH

'City of God' (2002)

A Crime Epic With the Energy of a Street Fight

What Makes It Great: While some crime movies immerse audiences in their criminal worlds, City of God traps them inside one. Fernando Meirelles directs with astonishing energy, combining kinetic camerawork, rapid editing, and documentary-style realism.

The opening sequence is enough to hook anyone watching. It features a chicken darting through the favela and announces the movie's visual style before the story even settles in. Every technical element works in harmony, the young ensemble is brilliant, and the non-professional performances are even better.

Its Place in Gangster Movie History: City of God is one of the most essential international crime movies of the 21st century. Based on Paulo Lins' novel and inspired by real-life experiences in Rio de Janeiro's favelas​​​​​​, it chronicles the growth of organized crime across decades. Rather than asking what power does to gangsters, it asks what gangsters do to a society. That shift changed the possibilities of the genre and influenced several other crime dramas.
