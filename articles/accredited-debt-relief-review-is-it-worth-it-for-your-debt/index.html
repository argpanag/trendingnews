Accredited Debt Relief review: Is it worth it for your debt?

TheTools · 15.11.2026 16:19 · source · permalink

Accredited Debt Relief combines a relatively low $5,000 debt minimum with strong customer reviews, but fees can reach 25% of enrolled debt.

  • Accredited Debt Relief helps consumers settle unsecured debts like credit card bills and personal loans for less than the full amount owed.
  • The company requires a minimum of $5,000 in eligible debt and charges a settlement fee of 15% to 25% of the enrolled amount.
  • ADR also connects clients with third-party lenders for debt consolidation loans as an alternative to settlement.

If you're struggling with credit card bills, personal loans or other unsecured debt, Accredited Debt Relief (ADR) may be able to help you settle your balances for less than you owe. The company also connects eligible borrowers with debt consolidation loans through third-party lending partners.

ADR stands out for its relatively low $5,000 minimum debt requirement, free financial wellness resources and strong customer reviews. It also says clients can reduce their monthly payments by 40% or more, although that doesn't necessarily mean they'll save the same percentage on their total debt.

In this review, we'll examine ADR's services, fees, eligibility requirements and customer experience to help you decide whether it's the right fit for your financial situation.

Accredited Debt Relief at a glance

What Accredited Debt Relief does and how it works

Accredited Debt Relief specializes in debt settlement, a process in which a company negotiates with your creditors to accept less than the full amount you owe. Its goal is to help you resolve eligible unsecured debts, such as credit card balances and personal loans, typically within 24 to 48 months.

ADR also connects customers with third-party lenders offering debt consolidation loans. Unlike settlement, consolidation involves taking out a new loan to pay off existing debts, ideally at a lower interest rate.

Here's how ADR's debt settlement process typically works:

  1. Get a free consultation. Apply online or call ADR to discuss your debts, income and financial situation. The company may perform a soft credit check, which doesn't affect your credit score, and provide an estimate of your potential savings.
  2. Review your options. If you qualify, ADR will propose a debt settlement plan based on how much you owe and what you can afford. If a consolidation loan may be a better fit, it can refer you to a lending partner.
  3. Start saving for settlements. Once enrolled in the settlement program, you'll typically stop paying participating creditors. Instead, you'll make monthly deposits into a dedicated savings account administered by a third party. These funds will be used to pay approved settlements and program fees.
  4. ADR negotiates with creditors. As your savings grow, ADR contacts your creditors to try to negotiate settlements for less than your outstanding balances.
  5. Review settlement offers. If a creditor agrees to accept a reduced amount, ADR will present the offer to you. You can accept or reject it.
  6. Pay approved settlements. If you approve an offer, funds from your dedicated savings account are used to pay the agreed-upon amount. The process continues with your other enrolled debts.
  7. Pay ADR's fees. ADR charges 15% to 25% of enrolled debt, depending on your state and debt amount. The company collects its fee for a settled debt only after you've approved the agreement and made at least one payment toward it.
  8. Resolve your debts. Once a settlement is completed, the creditor should report the account as having a zero balance. However, your credit report may still show that the debt was settled for less than the full amount owed.

According to ADR, most clients complete the program within 24 to 48 months. However, debt settlement isn't guaranteed. Creditors aren't required to negotiate or accept reduced payments. Stopping payments can also damage your credit, trigger late fees and collection efforts, and potentially lead to lawsuits.

ADR's debt consolidation loans are offered through third-party lenders, not directly by ADR. If you pursue this option, you'll be subject to the lender's credit requirements, interest rates, fees and repayment terms.

Accredited Debt Relief fees and total cost

Accredited Debt Relief charges 15% to 25% of your enrolled debt, depending on your state and how much you owe. Enrolled debt is the total amount you hire ADR to negotiate, not the amount you ultimately pay to settle it.

ADR doesn't charge upfront settlement fees. Instead, the company collects a fee for each debt only after three conditions are met:

  1. A creditor agrees to a settlement.
  2. You approve the settlement offer.
  3. You make at least one payment toward the settlement.

You may also pay fees for the dedicated savings account used to fund your settlements. ADR lists a $9 setup fee and a $9.75 monthly maintenance fee for the third-party account.

How much could you save with ADR?

Let's say you enroll $30,000 in debt, and ADR successfully negotiates settlements totaling $20,000. At a 25% fee, you'd pay ADR $7,500, leaving you with $2,500 in savings before taxes and other potential costs.

However, forgiven debt is generally considered taxable income by the IRS, which could reduce your savings further.

For example, if the $10,000 in forgiven debt is taxable and you're in the 12% federal income tax bracket, you could owe an additional $1,200 in federal income taxes.

This example doesn't include dedicated account fees, additional interest or late charges that may accrue while you're enrolled.

Not everyone owes taxes on forgiven debt. If a creditor cancels $600 or more, you may receive IRS Form 1099-C, Cancellation of Debt. You may also qualify for an exclusion if you were insolvent immediately before the debt was canceled. This means your total debts exceeded the fair market value of your assets, or what those assets could reasonably sell for. You can generally claim this exclusion using IRS Form 982.

These figures are illustrative, not quotes or tax estimates. Your actual savings will depend on the settlements ADR negotiates, its fees, your tax situation and other costs. Consider consulting a qualified tax professional to understand how debt settlement could affect your taxes.

Debt consolidation loan costs

If you pursue a debt consolidation loan through one of ADR's lending partners, the pricing works differently.

The original review reports annual percentage rates (APRs) ranging from 4.9% to 35.99%, with possible origination fees of 1% to 6% and repayment terms of four to 84 months. APR represents the yearly cost of borrowing, including interest and certain fees.

An origination fee is a charge for processing the loan. It's often deducted from the loan proceeds, meaning you receive less money than the amount you borrow.

Actual loan offers depend on the lender, your credit profile and other eligibility factors. These advertised ranges should be confirmed against current partner disclosures before publication.

Accredited Debt Relief graduation review

One feature that distinguishes ADR from some competitors is its graduation review.

According to ADR's program guarantee, the company reviews the accounts of clients who complete its settlement program to compare their original enrolled debt with the amount they paid creditors plus ADR's program fees.

If that total exceeds the original enrolled debt, ADR says it will refund the difference from its program fees.

This offers some reassurance about the program's overall cost, but it's important to understand the limitations. The review applies to clients who complete the program and doesn't guarantee a particular savings percentage. It also shouldn't be interpreted as protection against credit damage, taxes on forgiven debt or other costs outside the guarantee's terms.

Accredited Debt Relief eligibility requirements

To qualify for Accredited Debt Relief's settlement program, you'll generally need at least $5,000 in unsecured debt, be experiencing financial hardship and have enough income to make monthly deposits into a dedicated savings account.

ADR works with several types of unsecured debt, including:

  • Credit card debt
  • Personal loans and lines of credit
  • Medical bills
  • Accounts in collections
  • Certain private student loans, depending on the lender and loan terms

ADR doesn't settle secured debts, such as mortgages, home equity loans and auto loans, which are backed by property a lender can repossess or foreclose on if you don't pay. Federal student loans, tax debt, child support and alimony are also ineligible.

ADR doesn't publish a minimum credit score requirement for its debt settlement program. However, you'll need enough room in your budget to fund settlements and program fees through monthly deposits.

Debt consolidation loans have separate eligibility requirements set by ADR's lending partners. Your credit score, income and debt-to-income ratio (DTI) may affect whether you qualify and the interest rate you're offered. DTI is the percentage of your gross monthly income that goes toward debt payments.

A formal loan application may also require a hard credit inquiry, which can temporarily lower your credit score.

Accredited Debt Relief reviews and reputation

Accredited Debt Relief is accredited by the Better Business Bureau (BBB) and holds an A+ rating. Its BBB customer rating was 4.85 out of 5 as of November 9, 2026.

Positive BBB reviews frequently mention helpful representatives, knowledgeable specialists and clear explanations of the debt settlement process. Some negative reviewers express frustration over fees and charges they didn't fully understand.

ADR also holds a 4.8 out of 5 rating on Trustpilot, based on more than 12,000 reviews as of November 9, 2026. Approximately 95% of reviewers gave the company five stars.

Positive Trustpilot reviews highlight customer service and assistance navigating debt problems. Negative feedback tends to focus on program costs and disappointment with the amount ultimately saved.

Customer service awards

ADR has received more than 20 industry awards, including two Gold Stevie Awards in the 2026 American Business Awards for Customer Service Department of the Year and Customer Service Innovation of the Year.

The company also received the 2025 Excellence in Customer Service Award from Business Intelligence Group and the 2025 Financial Wellness Champion Award from Banking Tech Awards USA.

These awards reinforce ADR's emphasis on customer support, although they don't guarantee a successful settlement or a particular level of savings.

ADR has also faced legal complaints. In Sapan v. Accredited Debt Relief, LLC, a plaintiff alleged that the company violated federal telemarketing rules. The case was dismissed with prejudice in November 2023 at the plaintiff's request, without a court finding that ADR had violated the law.

Customer reviews and awards can help you assess a company's reputation, but they don't predict your individual outcome. Pay particular attention to recent feedback about communication, fees and completed settlements.

Accredited Debt Relief vs. competitors

Accredited Debt Relief isn't the only company that negotiates with creditors to settle debt for less than you owe. Competitors such as Freedom Debt Relief, National Debt Relief and Americor offer similar programs, but they differ in fees, minimum debt requirements and financing options.

Accredited's $5,000 minimum is lower than the reported thresholds for the other three companies, making it potentially accessible to borrowers with smaller unsecured debt balances. Its graduation review and financial wellness resources are also worth considering when comparing providers.

When Accredited Debt Relief may make sense

Debt settlement isn't the right solution for everyone. If you're keeping up with payments and have reasonably good credit, a debt consolidation loan or balance transfer credit card may help you pay off debt at a lower interest rate without the credit damage associated with settlement.

You can also try negotiating directly with your creditors. Some may offer hardship programs, reduced interest rates or settlement arrangements without requiring you to hire a debt relief company.

However, if you're struggling with significant unsecured debt and can't realistically repay what you owe, ADR may be worth considering.

ADR may be a good fit if you:

  • Have at least $5,000 in eligible unsecured debt
  • Are experiencing financial hardship or falling behind on payments
  • Can afford monthly deposits into a dedicated savings account
  • Don't qualify for affordable debt consolidation loans or other repayment options
  • Want professional help negotiating with multiple creditors
  • Value access to financial wellness tools and educational resources

ADR may not be right for you if you:

  • Have less than $5,000 in eligible unsecured debt
  • Can't afford the monthly deposits needed to fund settlements
  • Are current on payments and can realistically repay your debts
  • Primarily need help with secured debts, federal student loans or tax debt
  • Want to avoid the credit risks associated with stopping creditor payments

If ADR sounds like a good fit, start with its free consultation. You can review your debts, discuss your financial situation and get an estimate of your monthly deposits and potential savings before deciding whether to enroll.

How to get started with Accredited Debt Relief

Getting started with ADR begins with a free consultation online or by phone. A representative will ask about your debts, income, expenses and financial hardship to determine whether you qualify and explain your options.

You don't necessarily need paperwork for the initial consultation, but having the following information handy can make the process easier:

  • Debt information: Recent statements, account balances and creditor names for the debts you want to settle. You can also review your credit reports to make sure you haven't overlooked any accounts.
  • Income records: Recent pay stubs, tax returns or documentation of self-employment income or unemployment benefits.
  • Monthly expenses: An estimate of your housing costs, utilities, groceries and other essential expenses to help determine what you can afford to set aside each month.
  • Personal information: Basic identifying and contact information. ADR may request additional documentation if you decide to enroll.

The consultation is also your chance to ask questions before committing to a program:

  • How much will I pay in settlement fees and dedicated account charges?
  • How much will I need to deposit each month?
  • How long could it take to settle my debts?
  • What happens if a creditor refuses to settle or sues me?
  • What happens if I can't keep up with my monthly deposits?
  • How does the graduation review work, and which costs does it cover?

Before enrolling, make sure you understand the estimated total cost, potential savings and risks. You should also receive a written agreement outlining the program's terms and fees, which you can review before signing.

Bottom line

Accredited Debt Relief is worth considering if you're struggling with unsecured debt and want professional help negotiating with creditors. Its $5,000 minimum debt requirement, free financial wellness resources, strong customer reviews and graduation review help distinguish it from competitors.

However, settlement fees of 15% to 25% of enrolled debt can reduce your savings, and stopping payments to creditors can damage your credit. If you've exhausted more affordable repayment options and can commit to monthly deposits, ADR's free consultation is a good place to explore whether debt settlement makes sense for you.

Frequently asked questions

How does Accredited Debt Relief work?

Accredited Debt Relief negotiates with your creditors to settle eligible debts for less than you owe. After enrolling, you'll typically stop paying participating creditors and instead make monthly deposits into a dedicated savings account. ADR uses those funds to pay settlements you approve. The program generally takes 24 to 48 months, although results aren't guaranteed.

Is Accredited Debt Relief legitimate?

Yes. Accredited Debt Relief is an established debt settlement company founded in 2011. It's accredited by the Better Business Bureau, holds an A+ BBB rating and belongs to the Association for Consumer Debt Relief. However, these credentials don't guarantee successful settlements or make the program right for everyone.

What are the downsides of Accredited Debt Relief?

The biggest drawbacks are settlement fees and potential damage to your credit. ADR charges 15% to 25% of enrolled debt, plus possible dedicated account fees. Stopping payments to creditors can also lead to late fees, collection calls or lawsuits. Creditors aren't required to accept settlement offers, and forgiven debt may be taxable.

Will Accredited Debt Relief hurt my credit score?

Your credit score will likely suffer if you stop paying creditors while enrolled in ADR's program. Missed payments can remain on your credit report for up to seven years, and accounts settled for less than the full balance can also negatively affect your credit. The impact depends on your credit history and whether your accounts were already delinquent.

How much does Accredited Debt Relief charge?

ADR typically charges 15% to 25% of enrolled debt, depending on your state and debt amount. It doesn't collect settlement fees upfront. Instead, fees are charged after you approve a negotiated settlement and make at least one payment toward it. Separate dedicated account fees may also apply.
