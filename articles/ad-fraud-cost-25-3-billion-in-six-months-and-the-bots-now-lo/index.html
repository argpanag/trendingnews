Ad Fraud Cost $25.3 Billion in Six Months and the Bots Now Look Human

TheTools · 19.09.2026 15:10 · source · permalink

Ad fraud, the practice of billing advertisers for clicks and views generated by bots instead of people, cost $25.3 billion in the first half of 2026. That is the estimate from Spider Labs in a report released September 2, which puts the fraud rate at 5.58 percent and says "advanced" bots, the ones that mimic a real user's behavior, now make up 64.4 percent of invalid traffic of automated origin.

The Tokyo-based company, which sells fraud-detection systems for a living (its platform is called Spider AF), analyzed 4.006 billion paid ad clicks. Worth saying up front, because whoever sells the burglar alarm has an interest in painting the neighborhood as dangerous. The figures are a vendor's estimates, so read them as an order of magnitude. They are no census.

In the same weeks, another dossier showed how fraud gets manufactured in practice. On July 30, Bitsight, a cybersecurity company, published its research on Fuyao, a network of cheap Android TV boxes that, with the television off, open sites stuffed with AI-generated text and click the ads while posing as a smartphone. The case, picked up by Krebs on Security and Fox News (August 14), hits close to home for anyone running ads through Google AdSense, since that was one of the circuits touched.

How Much Does Ad Fraud Cost in 2026?

According to Spider Labs, ad fraud cost advertisers $25.3 billion in the first half of 2026, or 5.58 percent of the paid clicks analyzed. At that pace the annual total would reach about $50.6 billion, against the $32.6 billion the same company attributed to all of 2025.

Put more plainly, one dollar in every 18 spent on digital advertising ended up in fake traffic. The rate is up 1.26 points from 4.32 percent in 2025, while overall bot presence has more than doubled (2.2 times) over the same comparison. Analytics firm EMARKETER forecasts global digital ad spend of $907 billion for 2026. That is the scale of the target.

The Hardest-Hit Sectors

Automotive tops the sector ranking with a rate of 15.49 percent, almost triple the global average. Telecommunications comes next, then consumer electronics and appliances, then gaming.

These are categories where a single click costs plenty, so it stands to reason they draw more fraud. My reading, not the report's.

Bots Mimic Humans

The figure that weighs most sits in the makeup of automated traffic. In January 2025, sophisticated bots (the kind that move the cursor believably and space out clicks with irregular timing) accounted for 20.4 percent of invalid traffic attributed to bots. By June 2026 they are at 64.4. Filters that hunt for a single anomalous clue, like a click that lands too fast or a data-center IP address, effectively no longer see them.

The second figure concerns how ads get bought. Some 71 percent of losses on MFA sites (made for advertising, built to harvest ad revenue more than to be read) flowed through AI-optimized channels, against roughly 62 percent in 2025. In these campaigns the advertiser no longer picks the sites where the ad appears. The platform's algorithm does. The report itself makes the point. As media buying shifts to automated, opaque environments, fraud moves from repetitive scripts to operations that imitate the user.

The field, meanwhile, is widening. Since August 24, ChatGPT has been showing ads in 31 European countries, and legitimate AI agents, such as xAI's Grok Bot, which logs in on the user's behalf, blur the line between human and automated traffic even further. The more agentic browsers become routine, the less it helps to spot a machine behind the click. What matters is whose machine it is and on whose behalf it acts.

Fuyao, the TV Boxes That Click With the Screen Off

Bitsight's research, signed by researcher Pedro Falé and released July 30, starts from an expired domain. That domain served as a factory backdoor (a service entrance left open by the manufacturer) on certain TV boxes. Bitsight registered it to see who came knocking. Within 24 hours, 65,957 beacons arrived from roughly 38,000 unique MAC addresses, with at least 400,000 devices potentially exposed to the same domain.

The boxes, sold under the H96 brand (the most frequent model in the telemetry is the H96 MAX V11), presented themselves to ad servers as Samsung, Vivo, Huawei or Xiaomi smartphones. With the TV on they work as a residential proxy, renting out the home internet connection to anyone who wants to route traffic through a domestic IP. When the HDMI signal drops, they switch jobs and start clicking ads, never both at once.

The modus operandi is, however, more refined than you'd expect from a cheap box. The software uses a computer-vision model (YOLOv8s, trained on 12 classes that include "Adv" and "Taboola") plus Google's text recognition to find the banners on screen. The fraud routines are written in Blockly, the block-based language used to teach kids to code. Bitsight captured about 166 modules, 56 of them devoted to ad fraud.

The pages visited belong to 144 mapped sites, packed with AI-generated articles on finance, health, education, gaming, music and cooking, with ads shown only to the fake mobile profiles. Bitsight names Google AdSense and Taboola as the networks involved (84 domains carried the Taboola tag). Steering it all, according to the research, is China's Zhejiang Fengwo IoT Technology, founded in 2019 and holder of 20 patents filed in China, with shell companies registered in Hong Kong and Singapore to collect the money.

On the money, the research works from estimates. Bitsight calculates between $1 and $1.25 per day per device, so about $47,500 a day across the 38,000 confirmed devices and up to $150,000 on the fleet of 120,000 "digital humans" (the network's own label). The top scenario reaches $40 million a year. Read these as estimates built on active devices and ad fill rates.

Two caveats before anyone cries mass infection. Bitsight specifies that the data do not prove every H96 contains the software. The brand alone can't tell you whether a box is clean. Google, asked by Fox News, clarified that the devices involved run on the Android Open Source Project rather than on Play Protect-certified Android TV. H96 and Fengwo, for their part, did not respond to requests for comment before publication.

Same Recipe, Different Hardware

The method is not isolated. In August, Kaspersky described the first malware for connected cars, a downloader for Android head units with the stated aim of ad fraud and building a proxy botnet. The target changes (the onboard computer in place of the TV box), the logic stays put. Cheap Android hardware, loosely controlled updates, a connection to rent out, clicks to invoice.

At home, for that matter, you don't need a knockoff box to get surprises. The case of LG smart TVs caught recording audio with the screen off and mapping the home network shows as much. So does every botnet that has leaned on cameras and routers, like the old Sality, dismantled after 23 years.

What It Means for Ad Publishers

Anyone who shows ads on their own site sits on the other side of the counter, and doesn't always notice. Every fake click on Fuyao's pages ends up as revenue in the operator's pocket and as a cost on an advertiser's bill. Honest publishers feel it by rebound, since invalid traffic is one of the reasons Google can limit or close an account, as AdSense's guide to invalid traffic explains. For those who live on this revenue, it pays to know how AdSense payments and invoicing work, before finding themselves arguing about it with a suspended account.

From direct experience, fake traffic shows up even where you don't look for it. On August 7, Google Analytics logged 192 active users on my blog, 108 of them in Singapore, while over the same interval the server had served just one Singapore IP address able to actually load the page. A Chinese residential proxy accounts for the two figures. The swarm downloaded pages from home IPs in China, and the call to Google left from the bot's real infrastructure, in Singapore. I can't say the Fuyao TV boxes were behind it, but the ingredient was the same.

A proof-of-work challenge (a small computation the browser must solve before receiving the page) was passed by over half of those clients, between 54 and 57 percent. Once China was blocked, traffic reappeared from Brazil, Ukraine and Vietnam, on genuine home networks. On ad pages the estimate was roughly 3,300 invalid impressions a day.

Three checks I've used since then (none needs paid tools):

  • Compare Google Analytics active users with the IP addresses that also download CSS and JavaScript in the logs. For those who genuinely run the page, the two numbers come out almost equal (188 against 192 in my case).
  • Look at the languages read. A real Chinese reader reads the Chinese version, whereas 2,300 Italian-language pages downloaded in six hours from Chinese IPs (against 213 in Chinese) amount to the site's archive under scan.
  • Skip the country block. The swarm rotates proxies, so shutting out one nation moves it without stopping it.

Who Pays the Bill

The advertiser pays first, buying fake clicks. Honest publishers pay next, with diluted revenue and accounts at risk. Then, among others, whoever bought the TV box, since their connection serves as an exit for someone else's traffic and, with the TV off, the device keeps working anyway. None of the three chose it.

When it comes down to it, ad fraud is no longer a specialist's trade. A block-based language from a coding class, a model that spots banners, hardware that costs next to nothing. Together they suffice to stand up 120,000 "digital humans". The more bots learn to look like people, the more it pays to be a verifiable person, with a real site and real readers. A $907 billion market that still, in large part, trusts a click.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. How do I know if my H96 TV box is infected?

An H96 TV box can't be recognized by its brand. Bitsight found the Fuyao apps mostly on older H96 MAX V11 units, but no complete list of affected models has been published. The practical check is to see whether the device is Play Protect certified, following Google's procedure. If it isn't, Krebs advises sticking to well-known brands and, when in doubt, unplugging the box from the network and replacing it.

2. Does Google refund advertisers when clicks are fake?

Google says it filters invalid traffic before billing. According to Google Ads' guide to invalid traffic, whatever slips through is credited back automatically after invoicing. Spider Labs' estimate, though, covers the entire advertising market and not just Google, so the $25.3 billion shouldn't be pinned on a single platform.

3. What is a residential proxy and why do fraudsters care?

A residential proxy is a service that routes traffic through a real home connection. The IP address belongs to some ordinary subscriber rather than a data center, so to anti-fraud systems the visitor looks like a normal user. That is why Fuyao rents out the home lines of TV box owners while the television is on.

4. What are MFA sites and why do they matter in ad fraud?

MFA (made for advertising) sites are pages built to harvest ad revenue rather than to be read. According to the 2025 report from Spider AF, their number grew 1,409 percent in a year. In the first half of 2026, 71 percent of losses on these sites flowed through AI-optimized campaigns.
