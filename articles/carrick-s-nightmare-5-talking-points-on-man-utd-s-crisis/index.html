Carrick's Nightmare: 5 Talking Points on Man Utd's Crisis

TheTools · 28.09.2026 15:13 · source · permalink

Solskjaer 2.0? 5 Alarming Talking Points from Michael Carrick’s Nightmare Start at Manchester United

Michael Carrick was supposed to be the safe pair of hands. Instead, four months into a permanent contract, Manchester United are living through their worst start to a Premier League season since Louis van Gaal’s infamous 2014-15 campaign — and the numbers, the noise and now the transfer rumours are all pointing in one direction.

It is still only five games in. That much must be said first, because the temptation to declare a crisis in September is how United ended up burning through Ruben Amorim in the first place. But this is not an overreaction to one bad afternoon. United sit 12th with five points, one win, zero clean sheets and a fanbase that booed the substitution of Kobbie Mainoo at Craven Cottage. On Sunday, Football Insider reported that the club are monitoring Brighton boss Fabian Hurzeler as a potential replacement. Welcome to the Carrick crossroads.

1. Five points: the worst numbers in 12 years

Let’s start with what cannot be argued with. United have taken five points from their opening five Premier League fixtures: a 2-0 defeat at Hull, a 5-2 home win over Ipswich, a 2-2 draw at Everton, a 1-0 home defeat to Manchester City and a 1-1 draw at Fulham. The last United side to record five points at this stage was van Gaal’s in 2014. Eleven years later, another former player is learning how quickly optimism evaporates at Old Trafford.

The comparisons with last season are even more uncomfortable. Ruben Amorim, for all the criticism he endured, had seven points after five games in 2025-26 — two more than Carrick has managed. The only improvement on last year is goal difference (0 versus -2). And here is the stat that should keep Carrick awake during the international break: across both opening five-game runs, United conceded exactly eight goals and kept zero clean sheets. The back line remains a building site.

Carrick arrived as interim in January with a reputation as a deep thinker and, crucially, a former midfielder who understood United’s culture. Winning 11 of his first 16 games and securing Champions League football made the permanent appointment in May feel inevitable. But as every caretaker from Solskjaer onwards has discovered, the new-manager bounce at United has a half-life — and Carrick’s has now expired.

2. Fulham exposed the effort problem

If the City defeat was painful — a 1-0 home loss to ten men — the draw at Fulham was damning. United were outrun by 6.6 kilometres at Craven Cottage and needed a deflected 89th-minute strike from Matheus Cunha to rescue a point. Gary Neville described part of the second-half display as “pathetic” on co-commentary, and Carrick’s response — praising his players’ fighting spirit and rejecting Cunha’s own suggestion that the team lacked energy — drew as much criticism as the performance itself.

Then came the Mainoo moment. With United chasing a winner, Carrick withdrew academy favourite Kobbie Mainoo and was met with audible anger from the travelling supporters. Carrick defended the change as a tactical necessity and insisted Mainoo understood the decision. But perception matters: when fans are booing the hook of a 21-year-old academy graduate in a game your team is being outrun in, you have an optics problem on top of a points problem.

In the 4-2-3-1, the double pivot of Mainoo and his partner is being asked to cover enormous ground behind an aggressive front four — and without the legs around them, the structure collapses into exactly the kind of leggy, second-to-everything display Fulham produced. Tactical tweaks mean nothing if the running numbers stay where they are.

3. The left-back black hole — and the Tyrick Mitchell race

United’s problems run deeper than effort. According to Tuttosport, via Sports Mole on 26 September, United face competition from Juventus for Crystal Palace left-back Tyrick Mitchell — a player who can be signed for free next summer, meaning Palace would likely accept a cut-price fee in January. Luke Shaw remains Carrick’s first-choice left-back when fit, but at 31 the former England international is struggling to keep pace with the league, rarely offering the overlapping energy the system demands.

Patrick Dorgu provides that energy but his defensive level has fluctuated wildly — composed around his own penalty area one week, caught out the next. Mitchell, 27, is not an exceptional defender without the ball, but he is far more reliable in his own box than Dorgu and far more durable than Shaw. The profile fits: a Premier League-proven, tactically disciplined left-back who can hold width in the 4-2-3-1 and free the left winger to stay high.

Losing that race to Juventus would be a bad look for a club already watching from 12th. And the mere fact United are shopping in January tells you what the recruitment team thinks of the current options: Shaw’s body and Dorgu’s inconsistency cannot be carried through a full season. This is a structural rebuild position, and every week it goes unaddressed, the left flank remains the opposition’s favourite route into United’s box.

4. The Solskjaer parallel is now impossible to ignore

The comparison writes itself, which is precisely why it hurts. Another beloved former player. Another interim spell that charmed the boardroom — Solskjaer won his first eight games in charge; Carrick won 11 of 16. Another permanent contract handed out in the afterglow. And now another autumn of discontent, with the fanbase asking the question that defined Solskjaer’s final months: is being a great United man the same as being the right manager for this rebuild?

The Hurzeler link adds a cruel twist. United’s supposed successor-in-waiting has Brighton third in the table with 16 goals from five games, 64.8 per cent average possession, a league-leading 22 big chances and 5.8 shots on target per game. The Seagulls beat United twice already this season — in the league and, more embarrassingly, in the Carabao Cup at Old Trafford after United surrendered a two-goal lead. United are effectively monitoring a manager who has outclassed them in every meeting so far.

Carrick retains credit in the bank — players are reportedly still behind him, and the board is not thought to consider his position under immediate threat. But the Solskjaer lesson is that credit depletes fast when results don’t follow, and the international break rarely calms a storm like this. It usually just gives everyone two weeks to talk about it.

5. What Carrick must fix when the break ends

Jamie Carragher, delivering his verdict on Carrick’s future, argued there is no need to panic yet — a view Carrick himself echoed to ESPN, calling talk of his job hanging by a thread “ridiculous” and insisting “the world doesn’t cave in” after two or three bad results. He’s right that five games is a small sample. He’s wrong if he thinks the sample isn’t telling.

The fixes are clear. First, the press has to be real or not at all: United cannot keep posting negative running differentials in a league where Brighton, Brentford and Leeds are built on intensity. Second, the left-back situation needs a decision — either Dorgu gets a sustained run to iron out the errors, or Shaw is protected with a more conservative role. Third, the double pivot needs legs: if Mainoo is going to play the No. 8 role Carrick wants, he needs a destroyer behind him, not another technician.

What comes next offers no hiding place. United’s first two games after the break are against Tottenham and Leeds — a wounded giant and an intense, high-pressing side that will happily exploit every flaw Fulham just exposed. Win those two and the Hurzeler chatter dies overnight; Carrick’s permanent era gets its second wind. Drop points in both and the Solskjaer parallel stops being lazy punditry and becomes prophecy. For Michael Carrick, the international break is not a pause button. It is the last quiet fortnight he may get.
