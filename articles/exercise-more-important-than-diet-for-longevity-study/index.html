A study found that to live a long, healthy life, people need to pay as much attention to regular exercise as to improving eating habits. Physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness showed a stronger association with lower mortality risk than a healthy diet.

On Tuesday, Japanese media outlet GigaZine reported that Glenn Gaesser (글렌 게서), a professor of exercise physiology at Arizona State University, and Robert Sallis (로버트 샐리스), a sports medicine specialist at Kaiser Permanente Medical Center, published findings in the international journal Mayo Clinic Proceedings. The study analysed the effects of diet quality, physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness on all-cause mortality risk.

The researchers used PubMed, a medical paper database of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, to conduct a comprehensive review of dozens of existing studies. Most were published within the past 10 to 20 years.

The analysis showed people who maintained healthy eating habits had a 4 to 23 percent lower all-cause mortality risk than those who did not. People who walked 11,000 to 16,000 steps a day had a 45 to 67 percent lower mortality risk than those who walked 2,000 to 5,000 steps. The group with the best cardiorespiratory fitness also had a 41 to 53 percent lower mortality risk than the lowest group.

People who consumed large amounts of ultra-processed foods, processed meat, sugars and fried foods had a 3 to 20 percent higher mortality risk. Those who exercised too little and spent most of their time sitting had a 46 to 163 percent higher risk.

The study found positive associations even with small increases in physical activity. For sedentary people, adding 2,000 steps a day was linked to a 25 to 50 percent lower mortality risk. In the group with very low physical activity, increasing daily activity by 5 to 10 minutes was associated with a 30 to 42 percent lower mortality risk.

A study published last year in the international journal The Lancet Public Health also found people who walked 7,000 steps a day had a 47 percent lower all-cause mortality risk than those who walked 2,000 steps.

However, it is difficult to confirm a direct causal relationship between exercise and longer life because many of the studies reviewed were observational. The researchers said they were not denying the importance of healthy eating, and called for balanced nutrition alongside regular physical activity while placing greater emphasis on physical activity to promote longevity and reduce the risk of death from chronic disease.
