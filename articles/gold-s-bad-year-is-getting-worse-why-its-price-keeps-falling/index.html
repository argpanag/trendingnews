Gold's bad year is getting worse. Why its price keeps falling

TheTools · 19.11.2026 13:37 · source · permalink

In this article

Precious metals investors can be forgiven if they feel like the past year has given them whiplash. 

Following a torrid run, which saw the price of gold hit new records 53 times in 2025, gold hit a historic high of $5,589.38 an ounce on Jan. 28, only to take an almost equally historic nosedive. On Wednesday, gold fell below $3,800 for the first time since September 2025, roughly a third below its peak. 

A convergence of factors pushed gold to historic highs earlier this year, according to Suki Cooper, global head of commodities research at Standard Chartered, the London-based multinational bank. Now a different convergence is bringing the price down. 

"Gold can behave as a commodity or a currency," Cooper said.

Which role it plays depends on the factors dominating the market and the psyche of investors, and right now, it's the currency argument that is bringing the precious metal down. 

Cooper said gold's climb began with strong physical demand that started in 2022 and raised the floor under prices. A second phase followed as investors rethought gold's place in their portfolios. With the cost of holding a non-yielding asset falling, they started thinking about the metal for the longer term. That, Cooper said, marked a turnaround. 

Until early 2025, Cooper said, long-term investors, especially those in gold-backed exchange-traded funds, had been cutting their exposure, which effectively added supply to the market. Last year, that reversed: ETF holdings, led by the SPDR Gold Shares (GLD), rose by roughly 800 tons and helped absorb the market's excess supply. 

"There was a lot of one-way interest, a lot of appetite to buy but not to sell," Cooper said.

With selling in the secondary market staying muted, prices kept rising. By the end of 2025, net ETF holdings had passed 4,000 tons. Globally, gold ETFs had their strongest year ever for inflows, $89 billion, led by North America, according to the World Gold Council, with assets more than doubling to $559 billion in total.

A weaker dollar, lower bond yields and worries about global debt "set the foundation for gold to hit highs in 2026," Cooper said.

Investors who had been under-exposed to the metal kept buying.

Evan Mills, a financial advisor with Scholar Financial Advising, a fee-only firm in Winston-Salem, N.C., said the lower interest rates, expectations of further rate cuts and a weakening dollar, were all reasons for investors to favor gold. 

"It was a perfect spot to be in if you were an investor in gold and commodities as a whole," Mills said. And investors did extremely well, especially with lower interest rates. "Cash doesn't do well in that environment, so a lot of people looked toward other places to hold their cash and maintain purchasing power," Mills said. 

That changed after January.

The speed of the rally made some investors uneasy, and there was growing talk that the Fed could tighten. Then, Cooper noted, the Middle East conflict broke out and gold changed roles. It stopped acting as a portfolio safe haven and became "a liquidity provider."

Cooper explained that because it was liquid and easy to sell, investors sold it to raise cash, which set off the first correction. Since then, she said, markets have been pricing in what the Fed will do.

"In 2026, the Fed has leaned more toward higher interest rates," Mills said.

This week, the Federal Reserve Board of Governors member Christopher Waller said a 50-basis point rate hike may be necessary to "shock people."

"There are higher Treasury yields and a strengthening dollar, so the opportunity cost of holding an asset that doesn't produce income is much higher," Mills said.

Mills said that in the current situation, gold investors are missing out on an environment in which cash actually does well, with money market funds that can pay 4% to 5%. 

"Cash maintains purchasing power, and you don't need to put it in an illiquid asset or one where you're relying on another person to see the value," Mills said.

From 2025 to 2026, gold didn't become any less scarce; the alternatives to owning it became more attractive.

"Stocks, bonds, the classic portfolio mix, all became a lot more attractive, so people looked at the opportunity cost and decided it wasn't worth holding gold anymore," Mills said.

This has been the case for other metals in 2026 as well, with silver plummeting from an all-time high price.

After an extraordinary rally, it's easy to start questioning gold. Mills said investors should start by remembering that expectations were extremely high. "When expectations are 20% or 30% returns in a year, then of course when returns drop, people start to panic," Mills said.

Panic shouldn't be the watchword for gold investors, but caution should remain. 

"In the short term, with real yields going up and the dollar likely strengthening with future Fed hikes, I'd be cautious about gold in the overall portfolio," Mills said. 

For investors, the key is to understand why gold is in your portfolio to begin with.  

"If you're looking for it to be a short-term purchase, then gold likely doesn't belong in your portfolio at all," Mills said. If the metal is seen as a hedge, gold can remain a valuable part of a portfolio, but it can still be a poor investment depending on the price you pay to get in. 

"If you're looking at gold to be the growth section of your portfolio, you're likely going to be disappointed," he said. "Gold is insurance, not an engine, and the mistake is buying insurance when its price has skyrocketed," he added. 

For investors who bought at January's peak as a modest, long-term hedge, a sharp downturn doesn't defeat the purpose, he said. "You have nothing to really worry about, because that's what the whole goal of gold was." 

Gold tends to do well when the dollar weakens or when markets and geopolitics turn uncertain, Mills said, and when those pressures fade, the rest of a portfolio should pick up the slack. 

"That's the whole point of insurance, and the whole point of diversification," he said. "Gold does well when the other parts of your portfolio might be slacking, and when they flip-flop, that's a good thing." 

Other commodities experts say gold remains a good long-term bet. Amy Gower, head of metals and mining strategy at Morgan Stanley, told CNBC's "Squawk Box Europe" that there are "still lots of reasons" for investors to hang on to their gold despite the correction this year.

Philip Diehl, former director of the U.S. Mint and now president of U.S. Money Reserve, an Austin, Texas-based distributor of government-issued gold, silver and platinum coins, said the long-term case for gold is still in place, but the combination of abrupt shifts in U.S. trade policy and geopolitical alliances, along with the Iran war, qualifies as a black swan event for the precious metal. 

"The fundamentals that drove that bull market remain in place. They were interrupted by the Cat 5 hurricane that was unleashed in the Persian Gulf in an unwise war," Diehl said.

He expects that to change after American voters "issued a mandate" to end that war two weeks ago. 

"I'm confident gold prices will return to their upward trajectory in 2027 as oil prices normalize and prices fall at the pump, allowing interest rates to stabilize," Diehl said. 

Central banks continue to buy gold, with the highest monthly total in September, according to the World Gold Council, though overall purchasing is 7% lower than the same period last year.

Marc Chandler, managing partner and chief market strategist at Bannockburn Capital Markets, said surveys suggest that, in aggregate, that will not change. "However, don't only think about China or U.S. rivals," Chandler said, noting that the World Gold Council has identified Poland, a NATO member, as one of the larger buyers for most of this year. 

"Retail has been burned," Chandler said. "Higher interest rates have made non-interest-bearing assets less attractive, including bitcoin." 

Chandler said that the price of gold is headed to $3,500 to $3,600. Gold hit a fresh low below $3,700 on Thursday morning.

"There is nothing on the charts that says a low is in place," Chandler said. "For the anxious bottom pickers, I urge caution and being patient to see some reversal pattern. For the most part, the decline looks fairly orderly." 

"Gold is still very expensive compared to anything but the last two years," Chandler said, noting that gold's 10-year (120-month) moving average is about $2,127 and its 20-year (240-month) moving average is about $1,666.
