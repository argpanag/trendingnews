Audio reveals exchanges between control centre and drivers before deadly Spain train crash - follow live

TheTools · 29.10.2026 09:47 · source · permalink

Summary

  • Audio recordings published in Spanish media reveal conversations between train drivers and a railway control centre in the minutes leading up to a crash that killed at least 68 people and injured dozens

  • Officials are investigating the cause of the disaster, which occurred when a train travelling from Madrid to Seville barrelled into a stationary train on Wednesday morning

  • Three audio recordings between the control centre in Madrid and two train drivers appear to show that one high-speed train was given clearance to move even though another some 25km (15 miles) up ahead had stalled

  • The release of the recordings comes as Spain begins three days of national mourning for one of the worst rail disasters in Europe's modern history

  2. Train driver told to 'resume journey' at normal speed by control centrepublished at 09:37 GMT
    09:37 GMT

    Audio recordings published by Spanish news outlets, including El Pais and RTVE, reveal the conversations that took place between the two train's drivers and the control centre in the Spanish capital ahead of the crash.

    In one recording, a control centre operator tells the Renfe driver to resume its journey at normal speed after some technical issues.

    "Continue your journey and then proceed under [Lateral Signalling Block], at normal speed with normal operation," the control centre operator says to the driver.

    The driver replies, "At normal speed?" To which the controller says: "Yes."

    The Lateral Signaling Block is a back-up or alternative train system used when the automatic signalling or data transmission system fails.

    Later, the driver of the other Iryo train says in another audio recording that communication with the train-control system has been lost and they are stationary at Urda.

    The train "is stationary in front of the entry signal at Urda", the driver tells the control centre operator. After a series of exchanges, the driver is then told to "resume the journey" normally.

    The Iryo driver then contacts the control centre again, according to a later recording, saying they cannot start the train. "The train won't allow me to control the [security] system. That's why I am still stopped in front of the entry signal," they say.

    The latter recording suggests that the controller had been notified that the Iryo train remained stationary as the Renfe train approached.

    09:30 GMT

    There's been a lot of focus on a control centre which Spain's Transport Minister Óscar Puente says typically takes charge of deciding when trains can move again in the event of system failures.

    Spanish media has obtained audio recordings obtained between train controllers working for a rail infrastructure company in Adif in Madrid and the train drivers.

    They appear to show that one high-speed train was given clearance to move at 08:36 even though another some 25km ahead had stalled.

    One train, run by state-owned operator Renfe, had 397 passengers on board. Ahead of it on the same line was a train run by private operator Iryo, with 147 passengers.

    A reported issue with the automatic signalling system - a computer failure - had forced all trains to halt on the affected stretch.

    But later the Renfe train was ordered to proceed in "error", according to Spain's transport minister.

  5. Workers confront horror of crash site as Spaniards begin asking tough questionspublished at 21:46 GMT 28 October
    21:46 GMT 28 October

    Sarah Rainsford
    Eastern and Southern Europe correspondent, reporting from the crash site

    More than 12 hours after the two trains collided here near Urda, and well after nightfall, the massive recovery operation is still in full swing.

    There are powerful lights at the scene, illuminating several train carriages tilting dangerously to one side on the tracks.

    I can make out some of the wreckage of other carriages, cascading down the embankment. Among it all are the emergency workers, confronting the horror of Europe’s worst rail disaster in many years.

    The occasional drone takes off to survey the scene, shining bright beams down below, and there’s the non-stop clunk and thud of metal as giant cranes and cutters go to work in the ruins, perhaps checking for more victims.

    At one point a funeral van passed us on the road.

    As families with missing relatives wait, and forensic teams in Toledo work to identify dozens of people who were killed here, Spaniards all over the country are asking tough questions about how this disaster was possible.

  6. Grief and anger in Spain after train collision kills at least 68published at 21:29 GMT 28 October
    21:29 GMT 28 October

    At least 68 people have been killed and dozens more injured in Spain after a high-speed train collided with another train just south of the capital Madrid.

    More than 500 passengers were travelling on the two trains at the time - this graphic shows how the crash unfolded.

    "Suitcases started flying, people started screaming," a survivor tells the BBC, saying they don't think they will ever recover from witnessing the horrific aftermath.

    Transport Minister Óscar Puente says a computer failure led to the initial halting of six trains, including the two trains that subsequently collided.

    "In the event of a system failure, the control centre takes over," Puente says, adding it was the control centre which decided to allow the trains to move again, without any speed restrictions.

    That decision, he says, was made "under inexplicable circumstances".

    Protesters have gathered outside the transport ministry, and one of the country's largest trade unions has called on Puente to resign.

    Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez says "the whole of Spain is grieving", adding: "We will get to the bottom of this, and if there has been any negligence, it will come to light."

  7. Spain crash among the worst rail disasters in Europe in modern historypublished at 20:22 GMT 28 October
    20:22 GMT 28 October

    Today's incident, which has killed at least 68 people, is the second deadly train collision on the same line in less than a year and will likely raise questions over Spain's train infrastructure.

    It is among the most deadly train, tram and subway crashes in Europe in recent years:

    Greece, 2023: A passenger train from Athens to Thessaloniki smashed head-on into a goods train on the same line in northern Greece, killing 57 people.

    Spain, 2013: A railway accident left 80 people dead and 145 injured when an eight-carriage high-speed train derailed and crashed near the north-western city of Santiago de Compostela.

    Montenegro, 2006: At least 44 were killed and more than 180 injured when a passenger train plunged into a ravine outside the capital, Podgorica.

    Austria, 2000: A cable car on a funicular train caught fire in a mountain tunnel high above the town of Kaprun, killing 155 people.

    Germany, 1998: A high-speed train derailed in Eschede in Lower Saxony, killing 101 people, in what was the country's deadliest post-war rail disaster.

  9. 'Suitcases started flying, people started screaming': Survivor recalls moment of collisionpublished at 20:04 GMT 28 October
    20:04 GMT 28 October

    Sergi Forcada Freixas
    Reporting from Urda

    Tangui Binier, 22, is among the survivors of today's crash.

    Recalling the moment the two trains collided, he says the impact was "very sudden" and "happened so fast”.

    “Suitcases started flying, people started screaming. We were lucky because our wagon actually went forward and it stopped but the wagons behind flipped over," he tells the BBC.

    Binier says he was among the first people to get out of the train, when he saw it had been "completely flipped over" and there was another train behind it.

    “There were people in danger, it was really hard to see," he says.

    "There were dead bodies, there were missing limbs. I don’t know if that is something I will ever recover from."

  10. Train disaster could influence Spanish election campaignpublished at 19:59 GMT 28 October
    19:59 GMT 28 October

    Sergi Forcada Freixas
    Reporting from Urda

    Spain’s high-speed rail network is the second largest in the world after China’s.

    But this is the country’s second major high-speed rail disaster with multiple fatalities in less than a year, following the crash in Adamuz in January, in which 46 people were killed.

    While most parties suspended campaigning today, and Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez and other political leaders travelled to the crash site, the political implications of the accident are likely to linger throughout the election campaign.

    Sánchez has called a snap election for 29 November, hoping to turn the vote into something of a referendum on housing policy.

    Many political analysts saw that as favourable terrain for the prime minister.

    But a debate about the state of Spain’s infrastructure and the safety of its rail network could prove more challenging for Sánchez, who has been in power for the past eight years.

    18:34 GMT 28 October

    Nick Beake
    Europe correspondent

    The most significant information today has come from the Spanish transport minister who said that from his perspective - looking at the information available so far - this incident was due to human error.

    He described a situation just before 09:00 local time this morning, where there had been a computer failure which had led to at least six services in the area being halted.

  18. Passengers visibly angry as questions mount over crash causepublished at 18:22 GMT 28 October
    18:22 GMT 28 October

    The train that was hit from behind was still stationary when the order was given to the other train to proceed.

    According to regulations, under such circumstances, a train driver would be required to proceed at what is known as "sight speed" - about 40km/h (25mph), a speed that allows them to stop in time if they encounter an obstacle.

    Officials will investigate what order the Renfe train got, if it was travelling at the usual speed for that section of track - "around 200km/h" according to the minister, and, more significantly, why did the operator not stop its journey when his screen would have displayed that another train on the same line was stationary.

    Affected passengers were visibly angry.

    "It isn't possible that our train was stopped for five or 10 minutes and that the other train wasn't told to stop," one of them said.

    "How can this happen with the technology we have?"

Officials will investigate what order the Renfe train got, if it was travelling at the usual speed for that section of track - "around 200km/h" according to the minister, and, more significantly, why did the operator not stop its journey when his screen would have displayed that another train on the same line was stationary.

Affected passengers were visibly angry.

"It isn't possible that our train was stopped for five or 10 minutes and that the other train wasn't told to stop," one of them said.

"How can this happen with the technology we have?"
