Lemieux Foundation, UPMC launch $5M center to study microbiome effect on cancer

TheTools · 02.10.2026 09:31 · source · permalink

The UPMC Hillman Cancer Center has launched a new effort to uncover how the trillions of microbes living in and on our bodies can shape cancer and whether harnessing the microbiome could make cancer treatments more effective. Alongside the Mario Lemieux Foundation Thursday, UPMC officials announced the launch of the $5 million Mario Lemieux Microbiome Innovation Center.

“Scientists are learning more every day about the microbiome's influence on cancer development response, inflammation, resistance to therapy, and even survivorship,” said Dr. Stanley Marks, UPMC Hillman Cancer Center chairman and chief medical officer. “The potential to harness this knowledge to improve outcomes for our patients is tremendous.”

The microbiome — the bacteria, fungi and other microbes living in and on the body — can influence whether a patient’s immune system and cancer drugs are able to do their jobs effectively. It can affect inflammation, immune response, treatment resistance, side effects, and whether patients respond to immunotherapy, according to Dr. John Byrd, UPMC Hillman Cancer Center director.

“The microbiome is changing the way we think about cancer,” Byrd said.

The center will connect research labs across Pittsburgh working to advance research about how the confluence of lifestyle, diet and biology can determine whether a certain cancer treatment will work for an individual patient.

“Sometimes the biggest impact in science comes from studying the most simple thing and making a change,” Byrd said. “And that's what this center is going to enable us to do.”

Researchers will share tools and resources, including a microbiome biobank and artificial intelligence analytics, to bring discoveries to clinical trials. Among the realms ripe for discovery is how dietary habits impact cancer biology and the effectiveness of certain treatments, Byrd said.

“If the diet [or] if the supplements that patients are taking are not aligned with that personalized therapy, [it] has less of a chance of working,” he said. “And that's what this center is going to enable us to do; to understand both of those and bring them together.”

The center will develop new therapeutic approaches based on discoveries made in the lab, with the primary goal being to deliver more personalized cancer treatment for UPMC patients.

“The goal is not discovery for discovery's sake,” Byrd said. “The goal is really to translate work that's occurring in the laboratory to clinical trials and new innovations that derive, at the end, personalized treatments that cure more patients with cancer.”

The Lemieux Foundation’s $5 million investment in the center is its latest effort to support cancer research in the region. The foundation was founded in 1993 after Pittsburgh Penguins legend Mario Lemieux was diagnosed with Hodgkin’s disease in the middle of the team’s historic 1992-93 NHL season.

Lemieux was in attendance at Thursday’s event, but he declined to speak or be interviewed by the press. His daughter, Lauren Lemieux Antoni, said the center’s potential to improve cancer therapies is at the heart of the foundation.

“For our family and for the Lemieux Foundation, this gift is personal,” she said. “To me, it represents what the foundation has always stood for: caring deeply about patients and families and helping give them more reasons to believe better days are ahead.”
