Knife crime ‘blitz courts’ to help tackle justice backlog

TheTools · 12.10.2026 07:06 · source · permalink

“Blitz courts” will be used to hear cases involving knife crime in an attempt to reduce backlogs and provide victims with swifter justice.

The courts are designed to hear cases involving a single type of offence in rapid succession and have already been tested on cases of assault on emergency workers.

More than 500 attacks on staff including police, prison, NHS and ambulance workers have been dealt with by courts holding as many as a dozen hearings in a day, halving the backlog for that type of offence.

The initiative works by grouping similar cases together in a short, intensive period, focusing judicial expertise, prosecutors’ times and court staff knowledge in one place so that hearings can be resolved more quickly and efficiently.

The success of the courts has prompted judges to support their extension to cases where suspects have been caught in possession of knives, as well as domestic abuse and drug crimes.

Sarah Sackman, the courts minister, said: “We inherited a system where victims across the country were waiting too long for their day in court.

“That is why we are making changes to do things differently, by working with the judiciary to cut delays and get our courts moving faster.

“Judges, supported by court staff across the country are already proving what can be achieved by targeting resources where they are needed most, accelerating cases, cutting the backlog and delivering the fast justice that victims deserve.”

The average time between an offence taking place and a case being disposed of in a crown court has risen to more than 600 days in the worst affected areas such as London, with the total backlog of crown court cases passing 80,000.

The concept of blitz courts has been used to speed up proceedings in exceptional circumstances, such as the aftermath of the 2011 summer riots in England, which led to large numbers of public disorder hearings.

The latest move comes as the Government pushes ahead this week with its courts and tribunals bill which will remove the right of defendants to elect jury trial, and will allow magistrates to hear cases carrying sentences of up to two years, double the current limit.

Nottingham is one of the areas where blitz courts have been operating, targeting sex offences. On one day, the cases were completed in an average time of less than an hour.

It started with a man who had sent a 13-year-old intimate images of his genitals, prompting a police investigation which found he had almost 200 indecent images of children.

Calling him a “depraved individual”, the judge at Nottingham Crown Court handed him a two-year suspended prison sentence. He also ordered him to complete 118 hours of unpaid community service, and placed him on the sex offenders’ register for 10 years.

The hearing took just 45 minutes.

The judge then heard the case of a man who had sent a 14-year-old girl intimate pictures and asked her for sex.

The defendant, who was 27 at the time, was caught by a paedophile-hunter group after arranging to meet the girl outside a supermarket. He was also given a two-year suspended sentence.

By the end of the day, the judge had completed 10 cases.

In a nod to the changes set out in the courts and tribunals bill, the judge said some of the cases he heard should not have ended up in crown court and could have been resolved by magistrates, resulting in earlier pleas, swifter justice and less pressure on the system.
