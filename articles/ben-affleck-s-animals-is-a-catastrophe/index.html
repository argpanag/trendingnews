Ben Affleck’s Animals Is a Catastrophe

TheTools · 08.10.2026 23:00 · source · permalink

I like Ben Affleck, I really do. Amid the constant and surely exhausting attention paid to his personal life over the years, the man has carved out an impressive career. As an actor, he probably deserved Oscars (or at least nominations) for The Way Back and The Last Duel. His most recent directorial effort, Air, was one of the best movies of 2023. Before that, The Town and the Best Picture–winning Argo were both solid, and Gone Baby Gone was dazzling. (The less said about Live by Night, however, the better.) And I still think that Best Screenplay Oscar he and Matt Damon won for Good Will Hunting nearly three decades ago was well deserved. I am pro–Ben Affleck. Team Ben. Etc.

So it gives me no pleasure to report that his latest, Animals, which he directed, co-wrote, and stars in (and which is now streaming on Netflix), is a catastrophe. A baffling one to boot. The film is somehow another remake of Akira Kurosawa’s masterpiece High and Low (1963), arriving a little over a year after Spike Lee’s Highest 2 Lowest. (There is no “based on” credit in Animals, however, for either High and Low or King’s Ransom, the 1959 Ed McBain novel that inspired Kurosawa’s picture.) This time, Affleck is the wealthy man at the heart of the mistaken-identity kidnapping thriller. He plays Mark Kimball, an attorney running for mayor of Los Angeles who receives a call saying his young son has been taken and will be released in exchange for $7 million. Unfortunately, Mark has just put every single cent he has into his campaign, so he’s basically broke. With the help of a mysterious, high-powered fixer, Dayle Burton (Gillian Anderson), he finds the money — in exchange, of course, for an extortionate fee. When his son suddenly turns up alive and well, Mark and his psychiatrist wife, Abigail (Kerry Washington), realize that the kidnapper accidentally took the child of Ramón (Luis Gerardo Méndez), their housekeeper’s undocumented day-laborer boyfriend. Are they still willing to pay the ransom now that the kidnapped child is not theirs but that of a poorer family? And what does Dayle (not her real name), who likes to utter aphorisms worthy of a boardroom Sun Tzu (“Leverage is the balance between the allure of incentive and the consequence of refusal”), advise them to do?

Animals wants to be a political and domestic drama as well as a kidnapping thriller, which is certainly not an impossible task when done with a modicum of artistry. The film opens with a campaign commercial for Mark in which he offers bland political bromides against footage of him chatting up ordinary Angelenos. An independent who seems to be running a few points behind in the polls, he thinks winning the Latino vote is the key to victory. His constant drive to succeed appears to have alienated Abigail, to the extent that she’s currently having an affair with Ron (Ray Fisher), a teacher who’s just left his family and expects her to do the same (on the eve of her husband’s election campaign, no less, which definitely marks Ron as a smart, un-insane guy). The script tries to weave together these elements. Mark’s ambition, entitlement, and do-gooding hypocrisy have clearly undercut his family’s needs, and now, this high-pressure situation is exposing all these simmering domestic conflicts.

But the writing is ghastly. Most of the time, Mark just flails, complains, and pouts like a giant baby, and Affleck’s twitchy, rapid-fire delivery can’t give the character any life or depth. There’s emotion in his performance, but it rarely feels like the right emotion. He looks and sounds like an angry actor trying to get through a scene instead of a man in the middle of an impossible dilemma. Maybe that’s the point — the film seeks to interrogate Mark’s narcissism — but every interaction feels like it’s been flattened into a series of predictable accusations instead of something that might provoke surprise or reflection. One climactic, teary confrontation between Mark and his wife, which perhaps could have been the film’s emotional high point, rings so false I inadvertently started laughing out loud.

And at the risk of making a “The food is terrible … and such small portions” criticism, I’ll add that nothing feels fleshed out either. Abigail’s affair is briefly touched on and ultimately abandoned. (Did poor Ray Fisher get his scenes cut again??) Anderson seems to have a good time as the domineering, white-pantsuited and English-accented Dayle, all attitude and posture, but she also seems to be in a different movie. In fact, it is entirely possible that she’s in the movie Animals should have been, an over-the-top and self-aware kidnapping thriller instead of a failed exploration of race, power, and money. The film does give Ramón, the undocumented father of the actual kidnapped child, more to do than previous versions, as he starts trying to take the law into his own hands; that might have made for an interesting story thread (and a refreshing twist on the original), but here it feels tacked on. In the meantime, as Mark’s campaign manager, Sam, Steven Yeun serves mostly to push and prod his boss into making risky decisions, but one does wonder why such a talented actor has been cast in a one-note part. What is anybody doing here? What on earth happened to this movie?

Animals begins awkwardly, proceeds chaotically, and ends ludicrously, in a way that suggests that either a more considered ending was abandoned along the way or that Affleck wanted to distinguish his variation on High and Low by doing away with any of the things that made the story’s previous iterations interesting. I’ve never read the McBain novel, but Kurosawa’s and Lee’s films are both steeped in class and economic milieus: They’re structured around forcing their protagonists to break out of their isolation and confront their privilege, with the kidnapper becoming something of a mirror image of the ostensible hero; Kurosawa’s ends with a literal mirror image. Affleck’s instead ends with one of the dumbest and most predictably action-y finales of recent years, so much so that it made me wonder if it was trying to be a parody. There are enough seemingly unintentional laughs here that … maybe it is? Perhaps the actor-director is trying to borrow a page from his bitterly funny David Fincher hit, Gone Girl (another kidnapping thriller, sort of), but Animals has none of that earlier picture’s wit or Grand Guignol extremes or gonzo structure.

Each adaptation should stand on its own, of course, and Kurosawa films have proven quite malleable over the years. (Yojimbo, itself inspired by Dashiell Hammett’s Red Harvest, became A Fistful of Dollars and Last Man Standing; Seven Samurai became The Magnificent Seven and Battle Beyond the Stars and A Bug’s Life and Rebel Moon and The Magnificent Seven again; Rashomon became The Outrage and an episode of Frasier; hell, even Ikiru became Living; oh, and The Hidden Fortress became Star Wars.) That malleability is the adapter’s friend. The stories have strong armature, so just about all other details and threads can be changed. A High and Low set in the world of a high-stakes campaign isn’t a terrible idea, potentially unraveling the entitlement, ego, and isolation of the political class. And Los Angeles, with its many simmering social conflicts and resentments, is an ideal setting for something like this. All of which makes Animals’s failure that much more heartbreaking and enraging. Oh, well. At least we’ll always have Air.
