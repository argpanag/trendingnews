[Yoo Choon-sik] IMF's advice on fiscal policy

TheTools · 23.11.2026 00:12 · source · permalink

Having grappled with catastrophic demographics, fierce competition in export markets and geopolitical tensions for years, the South Korean economy has had good reason to celebrate in recent weeks. Major institutions have repeatedly raised their growth forecasts, and South Korea has become the fourth country in the world to surpass $1 trillion in annual exports.

As recently as April, the International Monetary Fund projected South Korea’s economic growth for this year at just 1.9 percent, well below the average of 2.5 percent over the previous five years. The IMF then raised its forecast by 0.7 percentage points in July and by the same amount in October before lifting it by another 0.6 percentage points just one month later.

The IMF mission’s forecast of 3.8 percent, released last week after its annual consultations with South Korean authorities, is exactly double the 1.9 percent it projected in April. If realized, it would be the country’s strongest growth in nearly two decades, excluding periods immediately after global crises such as the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic.

The Korea Development Institute made an unusually large upward revision to its growth forecast for this year, from 2.2 percent to 3.8 percent, and substantially raised its forecast for next year.

Both institutions pointed to a fairly straightforward explanation: The global investment boom in artificial intelligence has driven a surge in demand for South Korean memory chips. Driven by the semiconductor supercycle, the country’s annual exports surpassed $1 trillion last week, making it the fourth country to reach the milestone after Germany, the US and China.

What caught my attention in the IMF statement was its advice on fiscal policy. After backing the Bank of Korea’s widely accepted inclination toward further monetary tightening, the mission said a “somewhat more contractionary fiscal stance would also help contain aggregate demand pressures, while providing targeted support to vulnerable households and firms.”

A similar note of caution appeared in KDI’s report, released around the same time. The government-run think tank recommended that “fiscal policy should take account of the economic expansion and the delay in stabilizing prices, while making spending aimed at raising growth potential more effective and preserving fiscal room over the medium term.”

KDI went on to say that the expected benefits of investments intended to raise productivity should be scrutinized when projects are selected and that performance should be assessed more rigorously after the money is spent. It also advised the government to proceed cautiously in expanding permanent spending until it is clear that the gains in tax revenue will last, so that it retains room to respond if revenues move in the other direction.

Ill-timed spending surge

While acknowledging that growth is effectively at its strongest in more than a decade and that inflationary pressures remain high, the government plans to raise spending substantially on the back of a surge in tax revenue.

Governments ordinarily expand spending when the economy is under particular strain and exercise restraint when it is experiencing strong growth. The government’s fiscal plans for this year and next run directly against that principle.

Of particular concern is the proposal to establish the Future Response Fund, which would be unprecedented in scope and scale, together with several other new funds. These arrangements appear to give the government greater latitude to pursue fiscal policy with less parliamentary scrutiny.

The IMF mission stopped short of directly criticizing these measures, but we should pay attention to its statement that “the creation of the Future Response Fund should contribute to strengthening the countercyclicality of fiscal policy.” The mission was signaling that the fund should help the government support the economy in downturns and exercise restraint in upswings.

Bank of Korea Governor Shin Hyun-song suggested some unease with the government's expansionary fiscal policy.

“If temporary fiscal measures lead to recurring spending, many items become difficult to reverse, and that creates problems when you try to unwind them later,” he said in a news conference last month. “Ultimately, the National Assembly must scrutinize them rigorously.”

His point was that spending introduced on the back of a temporary surge in tax revenue could become a serious burden if it is designed to continue even after that revenue falls. He warned that with inflationary pressures already high, an increase in public spending would inevitably add to them.

Soaring semiconductor export prices have lifted exports and headline growth, but many sectors outside the semiconductor industry already suffer from higher prices, and businesses in those sectors could also face higher borrowing costs as a result of an ill-timed government policy.

Governments in this situation often try to ease discontent quickly with cash handouts or loan subsidies. But those measures push up fiscal spending and stoke inflation again, leading the central bank to raise interest rates further. The resulting cycle can deepen the very hardship the government set out to relieve.

An overwhelming majority in the National Assembly gives the ruling party no license to abandon sound policy and leave future generations to bear the cost.
