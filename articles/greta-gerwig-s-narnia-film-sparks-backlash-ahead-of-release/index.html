Greta Gerwig’s ‘Narnia’ Film Sparks Backlash Ahead of Release

TheTools · 08.10.2026 15:59 · source · permalink

From Meryl Streep voicing Aslan to a David Bowie-inspired “rock opera,” new details emerging from Greta Gerwig’s adaptation of C.S. Lewis’ The Magician’s Nephew have fans voicing dismay over the upcoming film.

News of Gerwig’s interpretation of the classic fantasy book spread in late September following the release of excerpts from an interview given to Empire magazine. 

Online responses from fans of the Narnia series were swift and less than enthusiastic. 

“To cast Meryl Streep as Aslan in Greta Gerwig’s Narnia is a slap in the face to the genius of Lewis, but also a deliberate mockery of Christianity that is 100% intentional,” Catholic commentator Isabel Brown wrote on X. She added: “Makes me wonder what else they’ll mock about this Christian story ...” 

Narnia: The Magician’s Nephew, set for theatrical release on Feb. 12, 2027, is the fourth film to be both written and directed by Gerwig, known for her Oscar-nominated films Lady Bird, Little Women and Barbie. 

It will also be the first film adaptation of The Magician’s Nephew, the sixth book in Lewis’ seven-part series The Chronicles of Narnia, written between 1950 and 1956, but the first chronologically, telling the story of Narnia’s creation. 

Speaking on the decision to cast the multiple Oscar-winning actress in the role of Aslan the Lion — a character viewed by scholars and fans alike as the undisputed Christ figure of the series — Gerwig told Empire: “I wanted someone who was profound but not pretentious, who had gravitas but not morose self-seriousness.” She went on to describe her ideal performer as someone who could convey both wisdom and childlike wonder, while also being a great actor — and somehow, Streep was the only one who fit the bill. Explaining to Empire how she wanted the film to be “a rock opera” inspired by David Bowie, Gerwig spoke of how much the music of Bowie and his contemporaries meant to her personally, alleging that they “saved [her] life.”

“I wanted it to be big and transporting,” Gerwig said (clarifying that it wasn’t going to be a musical).

“Narnia is literally a world made out of music,” she told the magazine. “It is a world sung to life by Aslan, and rock ’n’ roll to me felt like a world made out of music.”

 

Artistic Liberty or Blasphemy?

When it comes to film adaptations of beloved works of literature, there is a balance between honoring the author’s intent and the degree to which a filmmaker is free to take artistic liberties, given that filmmaking is by its nature a collaborative undertaking. Often added to this are logistical production challenges inherent in book-to-film adaptations, as what works beautifully on the page cannot always be translated to the screen. 

Recent film versions of Homer’s Odyssey are great examples of the scope of artistic liberty when it comes to such adaptations. On the one hand, you have Christopher Nolan’s interpretation, released earlier this year to reviews ranging from praise to derision, which was aesthetically true to the source material if not linguistically. Compare this with the Coen Brothers’ O Brother, Where Art Thou?, regarded by some as one of the best adaptations of Homer’s epic poem, despite being set in the American South of the 1930s. 

Then there is J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Lord of the Rings trilogy, adapted to film in the early 2000s, which is imbued with a wealth of Catholic themes. Due to its fantasy elements and style of storytelling, bringing Middle-earth to the screen presented both practical and narrative challenges. 

Although some fans criticized some of the creative choices, director Peter Jackson has spoken openly about his intent to honor Tolkien’s vision. Most viewers can look past the elements of the book that are absent or imperfectly represented on screen, recognizing Jackson’s deep appreciation for the source material.

Why, then, does Gerwig’s Narnia hit differently? 

Although inspired by Christianity — Lewis himself was an atheist convert to Anglicanism — The Chronicles of Narnia is a fictional work, and as a filmmaker, Gerwig is well within her rights to cast whomever she chooses in her adaptation. 

And Gerwig isn’t the first filmmaker to strip a film of its Christian literary origins. Take the 2008 adaptation of Brideshead Revisited by Evelyn Waugh, or Alfonso Cuarón’s 2006 film version of Children of Men by Christian author P.D. James.

Yet, it is not inconsequential when Christianity’s heritage is whitewashed from art and history, and Lewis’ Narnia is inextricably built on a Christian worldview. Casting Streep to voice Aslan sends a message about Gerwig’s personal beliefs about the divine —  beliefs that are fundamentally at odds with the Christian ethos of Narnia. 

For Lewis, Aslan is not simply an allegory for Christ. As he writes, Aslan “is an invention giving an imaginary answer to the question, ‘What might Christ become like, if there really were a world like Narnia and He chose to be incarnate and die and rise again in that world as He actually has done in ours?’”

Moreover, Lewis’ writings frequently spoke to the complementarity of the sexes, and it’s unlikely he would find this gender swap to be innocuous. To say the “masculine imagery” that God uses to describe himself is “arbitrary and unessential,” Lewis writes in an essay on the question of women priests, “is an argument not in favour of Christian priestesses but against Christianity.”

Between Aslan voiced as a woman and a Bowie-inspired “rock opera” for a story originally set in the year 1900 (Gerwig changed it to 1955), the fear among fans is that, rather than using her artistic vision to bring the original story to the big screen, Gerwig is instead treating The Magician’s Nephew as a backdrop to tell her own story and communicate her own vision.

“I don’t think Greta Gerwig is going to be able to redeem herself after she’s made these humongous mistakes,” wrote Msgr. Eric Barr of the Diocese of Rockford, Illinois, in a Facebook post. “But what’s even worse is that a wonderful classic novel and series is going to be twisted into a parody of what it is supposed to be: a redemptive story in the midst of a struggle of good against evil.”

“What a shame and what a missed opportunity.”
