Middle class Brits in uproar at Maldon Sea Salt import 'scandal'

TheTools · 16.11.2026 15:00 · source · permalink

After an exposé revealed the use of imported salt to make its signature pyramid-shaped crystals, Maldon Sea Salt has become embroiled in what’s being called the ‘most middle class scandal of the year’.

A staple of British kitchens for more than 140 years, Maldon proudly markets its product as ‘100% sea salt flakes’, harvested from the Blackwater Estuary in Essex.

However, according to an investigation by The Observer, the company also uses rock salt mined in Germany as part of its manufacturing process.

Reporters monitoring the firm’s Longwick Farm production site reportedly spotted four lorries delivering 12-tonne bags of salt featuring branding from European mining giant K+S.

Maldon updated its website after being approached by the newspaper, explaining that it uses ‘natural, ancient sea salt’ to enrich seawater with minerals, in a technique known as ‘salt-on-salt’.

Through this traditional filtration method, the imported salt is added to seawater to increase its salinity, strengthening the brine and reducing the energy needed to evaporate the water.

In a statement shared with Metro, Maldon said its ‘products and labelling comply with all applicable food-safety and labelling legislation,’ adding that ‘artisan salt-on-salt production techniques’ are part of the brand’s 144-year heritage.

‘Rock salt is ancient sea salt, from evaporated prehistoric seas,’ its spokesperson continues.

‘The ancient sea salt minerals dissolve into the sea water at Maldon, creating a brine. The brine is heated in open salt pans, and the evaporation process creates 100% Maldon sea salt flakes.

It follows previous investigations in 2019 and 2020 from Essex Trading Standards and the Food Standards Agency (FSA), which concluded that no further action was necessary.

‘There are no specific legislative parameters to aid in establishing whether a description “sea salt” could be considered accurate or inaccurate,’ said a spokesperson from Essex County Council.

Maldon has earned a reputation as an upmarket foodie essential in recent years, after celebrity chefs including Jamie Oliver and Delia Smith championed its distinctive flakes.

Gwyneth Paltrow has also sung its praises, while actress Cameron Diaz was reportedly so fond of the stuff she carried a tin in her handbag to sprinkle on the go.

Priced at £2.85 for a 250g box, its sea salt flakers come in at £11.40 per kg — a steep jump compared to Saxa rock salt at £2.20 per 350g or £6.29 per kg, and more than five times the cost of Saxa fine salt which retails for £1.90 per 850g or £2.24 per kg.

Whether this latest revelation will leave enough of a bitter taste in shoppers’ mouths to stop them paying such a premium remains to be seen.
