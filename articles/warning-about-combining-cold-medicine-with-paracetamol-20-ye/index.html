Warning about combining cold medicine with paracetamol: 20-year-old student ends up on life support

TheTools · 21.09.2026 08:24 · source · permalink

Paracetamol is one of the most widely used over-the-counter medicines in Britain and is commonly taken for headaches, fever, colds and flu.

However, many people don't realize that some combination cold and flu medications also contain paracetamol. Taking both products at the same time can inadvertently increase the total dose to dangerous levels.

That's exactly what happened to 20-year-old student Riley Lubas, who suffered severe organ failure last year after an accidental overdose.

His condition became so serious that he ended up on life support and dialysis, while during rehabilitation he had to learn to walk and talk again.

He thought he had the "student flu"

Riley had just started studying creative media at the University of Bath when he began feeling unwell with symptoms he thought were part of the so-called "freshers' flu", a term used for infections that often spread among young students.

He went to a pharmacy and bought cold and flu tablets, as well as 500 mg paracetamol tablets.

What he didn't realize was that even cold and flu tablets contained 250 mg of paracetamol.

Over the course of four or five days, he took two tablets of each product every four hours.

This brought the total daily dose to about 9,000 mg, more than double the usual maximum limit of 4,000 mg per day for an adult.

Overdose can severely damage the liver.

When taken at the recommended dose, paracetamol is generally considered safe for most people.

The problem appears when taken in excessive amounts.

When metabolizing paracetamol, the body produces a toxic byproduct called NAPQI. Under normal conditions, the body is able to neutralize it.

But in case of overdose, the amount of NAPQI can become too large and severely damage liver cells.

In more serious cases, this can lead to acute liver failure and be life-threatening.

Early symptoms can be vague and include nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, which can make it difficult to distinguish from the actual infection for which the person is taking medication.

Abdominal pain was the first serious sign

Riley, who also worked as a bartender and is from Newport in Wales, said he didn't realise he had taken too much paracetamol.

"It was only when my stomach started to hurt and I couldn't keep down food that my flatmates told me to call 111," he said.

This decision proved decisive.

After being advised to go to the Royal United Hospital emergency room, doctors performed tests and discovered that his liver had begun to suffer serious damage.

He was immediately hospitalized.

The condition worsened within a few hours

Initially, doctors suspected hepatitis, i.e. inflammation of the liver.

His parents left the hospital after visiting hours ended, but just a few hours later they received an urgent call from the intensive care unit.

"They told us we had to come back right away," Riley's stepfather, Chris, recounted.

When they arrived at the hospital, doctors explained that the 20-year-old's liver was on the verge of failure.

His neurological condition also began to deteriorate. Riley became confused, delirious, and experienced hallucinations, which can occur with severe liver damage.

He was placed on life support and dialysis.

He was then transferred to King's College Hospital in London, a specialist centre for liver diseases.

Within a few hours, the situation became critical.

"By six o'clock in the evening he was sedated, on life support and on dialysis, with multiple organ failure," Chris said.

Doctors began investigating what medications he had used.

The family contacted a friend of Riley's at university, who found packets of paracetamol and cold and flu medicine in his room.

At that moment, the suspicion of a paracetamol overdose became very strong.

Placed at the top of the transplant list

Riley was urgently placed on the British list for a liver transplant.

However, heart damage made the transplant extremely risky for two days.

During this time, he was kept under constant monitoring.

Doctors told the family that the liver damage seemed almost irreversible.

But, unexpectedly, liver function gradually began to improve.

Doctors attributed the recovery to age and good health.

After about a week, Riley was able to come off dialysis and be removed from life support.

According to the family, even the doctors themselves were surprised by the recovery.

"There was no exact answer as to why this happened, but they thought his age and good health had helped his liver recover," Chris said.

However, the hardest part was not yet over.

He had to learn to walk and talk again.

After intensive treatment and a tracheostomy to help him breathe, Riley's body had weakened significantly.

After coming out of sedation, he could not move normally.

"Physically he couldn't move," Chris said.

“They also had to re-teach him to breathe without a tracheostomy. It felt like he was choking when he tried to breathe.”

After a few weeks, Riley was transferred to a hospital in Newport, where he continued rehabilitation.

He suffered from severe muscle weakness and swelling, the consequences of prolonged bed rest and organ failure.

A turning point came when he was able to breathe on his own. He then began intensive physiotherapy and gradually learned to walk again with the help of assistive devices.

Today it has fully recovered.

After several weeks in intensive care and then rehabilitation, Riley was allowed to return home.

Over the following year, he underwent regular heart, liver, and blood tests.

Doctors later confirmed that he had made a full recovery.

Today, about a year and a half after the incident, the 20-year-old has returned to normal life.

Family calls for more awareness

His family is now urging people, especially young people, to carefully check the ingredients of cold and flu medicines.

The main problem is that many combination products may contain paracetamol, even when this is not immediately apparent to the consumer.

"It's scary because it's so accessible, so cheap and seen as a safe drug," Chris said.

"The biggest problem is that when you're sick, whether you're young, old or a student, it can be hard to remember exactly how often you're taking your medication."

Riley's case serves as a warning to always check the label and active ingredients of medications, in order to avoid inadvertently taking paracetamol from some products. /GazetaExpress/
