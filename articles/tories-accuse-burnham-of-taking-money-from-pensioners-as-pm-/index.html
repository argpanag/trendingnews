  3. No easy options for the PM on energy supportpublished at 10:37 BST
    10:37 BST

    Kevin Peachey
    Cost of living correspondent

    The PM admits there’s another energy crisis looming. It’s only a few years since the last, but his options to support billpayers aren’t all the same.

    There’s no chance of a repeat of the energy price guarantee that capped everyone’s bills in late 2022. Bills aren’t forecast to be as high and such a policy is very expensive.

    More of a possibility is a boost to the Warm Home Discount, which currently knocks £150 off winter bills for those on low incomes. But, would it make much of a dent in the forecast £276 rise in a typical annual bill?

    The introduction of a social tariff, that offers discounted bills to the most vulnerable – similar to the water and broadband sectors, has widespread support. But it could take a while to implement and there’s the thorny issue of who funds it – other billpayers, taxpayers or business.

    And there is pressure to fund a debt relief plan for households still owing money from the last crisis.

    All in all, there are no easy options for the PM and chancellor.

    Of course, a truce in the Iran war and ships moving through the Strait of Hormuz might well provide the best solution of all by bringing international energy prices back down.

  4. New social care service 'should be free at the point of need', says care home chiefpublished at 10:23 BST
    10:23 BST

    The prime minister's proposal for a new national care service is progress, but does not go far enough to reform the social care system, according to the president of the Independent Care Group.

    Mike Padgham, who operates five care homes in North Yorkshire, tells Radio 4's Today programme he was disappointed to hear that accommodation will be not be free under the new service, which would only cover the cost of care.

    "I know it's going to be costly but we are already spending a significant amount in the NHS on people who are ready to be discharged," Padgham says, adding that it's "not necessarily more money, it's just spending it in a different way".

    Asked how you determine what is a care cost and what is a residential cost, he adds that providers would have to take into account the cost of staffing, food and accommodation, in a similar way to a hotel.

  6. No way of knowing how much pension reform will save - IFSpublished at 09:49 BST
    09:49 BST

    The Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) has warned it is not possible to predict how much money will be saved by reforming the pension triple lock.

    The government says doing so could save £15bn a year by 2040, money which Andy Burnham has committed to investing in a new national social care service.

    But Hellen Miller of the IFS tells BBC Breakfast it's uncertain, and that it's possible the changes to the triple lock could meet those savings, or save nothing at all.

    She says the new proposals means pensions will still rise and they get protection against inflation.

    The policy will avoid unsustainable increases in pension levels, and points out the triple lock was effectively an "unfunded commitment" as "we didn't have tax rises planned to pay for this ever growing bill", she adds.

    Miller continues: "This doesn't stop any politician from deciding they want to give pensioners a higher pension... but if you want to do that then much better to tell pensioners that's what you want to do rather than leave it to the vagaries of inflation."

  7. How opposition parties reacted to Burnham's speechpublished at 09:30 BST
    09:30 BST

    Prime Minister Andy Burnham's pledges in yesterday's speech drew criticism from across the political spectrum.

    Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said triple lock changes would not lead to enough savings to fund the new care system "for another two decades" and "more tax rises are inevitable".

    The Green Party's Zack Polanski criticised the government for "going after pensioners", saying it should instead "tax the rich".

    Nigel Farage, from Reform UK, accused Burnham of "launching an offensive against our elderly" and called for an early general election.

    As a reminder, Burnham said he'd like to "adjust" the triple lock so that state pensions increase every year by at least inflation or 2.5%, removing the current link with average earnings. He said this will help pay for free social care.

  8. 'Less democratic' or 'honest conversation needed': Debate continues over electoral reformpublished at 09:20 BST
    09:20 BST

    This morning, BBC Radio 4's Today programme has been hearing arguments over Burnham's proposals over electoral reform.

    Former Conservative cabinet minister Michael Gove argues while proportional representation may seem fairer "it is actually less democratic".

    He says the essence of democracy is the ability to "throw the government out when it fails and to elect a government on a clear manifesto to deliver".

    Gove says the current first-past-the-post system is best able to deliver this and also offers a clear link between the constituency and the MP.

    In response, Labour MP Cat Smith says she wants to see a commission look at the pros and cons of various systems, and wants to keep the constituency link.

    Smith said it was important that we have an "honest conversation" about whether the way we elect MPs properly reflects the way people vote.

    Smith pointed to the last election, where Labour won some 33% of vote and 63% of the seats, but Reform UK won only five seats based on about 14% of the vote.

    She described it as "unhealthy for democracy", damaging the trust between voters and our politics.

    09:07 BST

    During his conference speech yesterday, Andy Burnham set out another big idea – changing the voting system.

    He said Labour would fight the next election on a promise to change the current system - known as first-past-the-post - so that "everyone's vote counts and everyone's voice is heard".

    08:39 BST

    Burnham is next asked whether in his heart he wants the UK to rejoin the EU.

    "I have said in the book that I would like to see Britain rejoin in my lifetime so there's no point me saying otherwise, but it's not necessarily the case that that is now," he tells the Today programme.

    08:38 BST

    Burnham is now being asked why he didn't mention the "borrowing crisis" that the UK is currently experiencing.

  14. Pension changes and social care will give people 'peace of mind', says Burnhampublished at 08:29 BST
    08:29 BST

    Defending his proposal over the triple lock, Burnham says it is not right to say he's scrapped it, but instead he's "changed" it.

    Asked on BBC Radio 4's Today programme why he is hitting pensioners rather than the wealthiest, he says "this is about helping pensioners" who "currently are at risk of paying ruinous care costs".

    He says: "I believe we've got to look at that bigger picture. It cannot be right going forward that we treat dementia very differently from cancer."

    The prime minister says his proposed adjustment to the triple lock retains the link between pensions and inflation, earnings or 2.5%.

    But earnings "should be considered over a longer time frame, not every year", he adds, arguing it will make it more sustainable economically.

    He is challenged that it will save £15bn a year, which is money taken from pensions, but Burnham says it's a move to support older people to give them "peace of mind" later in life.

    He adds that the reforms will give pensioners "more ability to protect homes and savings".

  18. Triple lock, social care, water ownership: What Burnham announced in his conference speechpublished at 07:58 BST
    07:58 BST

    Yesterday, Burnham gave his first speech to a Labour Party conference since becoming prime minister. Here's a brief overview of what he announced:

    • Triple lock - The pension triple lock, which guarantees the state pension rises by whichever is highest out of inflation, wage increases or 2.5%, will be adjusted from 2030, to remove the direct link with wages but still "hold its value relative to earnings over time"
    • Social care - Savings from removing the triple lock will be put towards plans for a free at the point of use "national care service" in England which "everyone contributes" to and where "everyone is covered"
    • Electoral reform - Labour will establish a national commission on electoral reform and commit to change the electoral system in its next manifesto
    • Energy - Great British Grid will be established, a publicly owned company which Burnham says will challenge the private sector operators and drive down timings and costs for connecting businesses and homes to the grid
    • Water - The ban on public ownership of water companies will be lifted, starting a 10-year journey to a "very different water system" that will "strengthen public control"

Yesterday, Burnham gave his first speech to a Labour Party conference since becoming prime minister. Here's a brief overview of what he announced:

  • Triple lock - The pension triple lock, which guarantees the state pension rises by whichever is highest out of inflation, wage increases or 2.5%, will be adjusted from 2030, to remove the direct link with wages but still "hold its value relative to earnings over time"
  • Social care - Savings from removing the triple lock will be put towards plans for a free at the point of use "national care service" in England which "everyone contributes" to and where "everyone is covered"
  • Electoral reform - Labour will establish a national commission on electoral reform and commit to change the electoral system in its next manifesto
  • Energy - Great British Grid will be established, a publicly owned company which Burnham says will challenge the private sector operators and drive down timings and costs for connecting businesses and homes to the grid
  • Water - The ban on public ownership of water companies will be lifted, starting a 10-year journey to a "very different water system" that will "strengthen public control"
