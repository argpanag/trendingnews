John Anderson, a Country Music Hall of Fame member known for his distinct, idiosyncratic singing style, died Wednesday. He was 71. A rep for Anderson confirmed his death to Rolling Stone. A post on the singer's social media announcing his death called Anderson a "gifted country music singer whose voice and songs touched countless lives."

A native of Apopka, Florida, Anderson possessed an unmistakable country warble, one that rivaled that of even Hank Williams. He put it to good use in country Number Ones like "Wild and Blue," "Black Sheep," and his signature song, the boisterous "Swingin'." Each became a cornerstone of Eighties country music, but Anderson didn't stop there. In the Nineties, he experienced a career revival and helped shape the sound of the era with hits like "Straight Tequila Night," "Money in the Bank," and the solo composition "Seminole Wind." That song, an homage to his home state of Florida, was particularly special to Anderson, and it stands as arguably his greatest recorded vocal.

On the strength of those songs, Anderson was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 2024. "One of the main gratifications is I got to live to see it," he told Rolling Stone. "Several of my friends were inducted posthumously." On Sunday, just four days before his death, Anderson appeared with his fellow inductees at the Country Music Hall of Fame Medallion Ceremony in Nashville to induct new members Tim McGraw, Paul Overstreet, and the Stanley Brothers.

"I was talking with John Sunday night at the HOF ceremony. He was in such good spirits," McGraw wrote on social media. "We talked, we laughed, we sang his songs that I loved so much together, just goofing off. I am completely heartbroken by his passing… He was and is one of the defining voices in country music."

John David Anderson was born Dec. 13, 1954, and grew up in Apopka, Florida, near Orlando. He was drawn to rock & roll first, but after hearing the vocal performances of George Jones and Merle Haggard (the latter would later become a friend), he found himself enamored of country music. He began releasing country singles in the late Seventies and issued his self-titled debut album in 1980. While Anderson wrote some of his songs, he was an undeniable vocal stylist from day one, putting his own spin on songs like "Your Lying Blue Eyes," "She Just Started Liking Cheatin' Songs," and Billy Joe Shaver's "I'm Just an Old Chunk of Coal (But I'm Gonna Be a Diamond Someday)," which, in 1981, became his first Top Five hit.

The following year, Anderson released his breakout album Wild & Blue. Produced by Anderson with Frank Jones, the LP included the title song (ranked Number 85 on Rolling Stone's list of the 200 Greatest Country Songs of All-Time), a cover of the traditional "Long Black Veil," and the single that would become Anderson's anthem, "Swingin'," which was named Single of the Year by the CMA Awards.

"'Swingin'' was truly the big change in my career," Anderson told Rolling Stone. "We had already laid a real good foundation, with songs like 'Old Chunk of Coal' and 'She Just Started Liking Cheatin' Songs.' We were on a pretty good roll at that time, especially for a young person back in in country music. But 'Swingin'' pretty much crossed all the bridges. 'Swingin'' was a huge record." (Both LeAnn Rimes and the Mavericks went on to record their own versions.)

Anderson continued to have chart success in the Eighties after "Swingin'," with songs like "Black Sheep," "She Sure Got Away With My Heart," and "Down in Tennessee." He sputtered a bit at the end of the decade but came roaring back in 1992 with the album Seminole Wind. A bona fide comeback record, the album included radio hits like "Straight Tequila Night," "When It Comes to You," and the title track. According to Anderson, his record label at the time had concerns that "Seminole Wind" was too regional. But when Anderson debuted it live onstage in Seattle, nearly as far from Florida in the continental U.S. as one could get, it was met with a standing ovation. Anderson knew he had written a hit.

A reliable recording artist, Anderson released a few albums throughout the 2000s, but in 2020 he returned reinvigorated, teaming up with producers Dan Auerbach and David Ferguson for the rootsy LP Years. It was a celebration of Anderson's career, and positioned him as a legacy artist who transcended country. "I always loved his voice so much and he just always seemed to have a lot of songs I loved," Auerbach told RS. "Some of them seemed old, some of them seemed new, but it was just always that voice, that unmistakable thing."

A 2022 tribute album to Anderson, Something Borrowed, Something New, assembled artists like Eric Church, Tyler Childers, Sierra Ferrell, and Sturgill Simpson to interpret Anderson's catalog. Luke Combs handled "Seminole Wind."

Two years later, Anderson was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame, alongside Toby Keith and the guitarist James Burton. Anderson was heralded as an interpreter of songs, like his friends and mentors Haggard and Jones.

"Real country music and the traditional country sound does mean a whole lot to me. It's been my whole life," Anderson told RS. "I came here at a very young age and dedicated my whole life to studying and writing and doing country music the way I thought it should be. I grew up with guys like Johnny Cash, Merle Haggard, Waylon Jennings and George Jones teaching me. They'd say, 'Son, you just keep doing what you do.' In other words, to them, style is what it was about."
