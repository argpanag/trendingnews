Fenix Simulations Announces Colossal A320 V3 Update, Release Next Week - Threshold

TheTools · 17.10.2026 23:35 · source · permalink

After months of silence, Fenix Simulations has returned with a colossal development update on the Airbus A320 family, revealing the Fenix A320 Version 3, a large overhaul for Microsoft Flight Simulator 2020 and 2024.

Indeed, Fenix released a nearly two-hour-long YouTube video covering almost every aspect of the aircraft, from flight dynamics and avionics to cabin systems, sounds, and a huge changelog of smaller improvements.

The video contains several easter eggs pointing towards the Fenix Simulations A320neo family, as well as quite obvious inspiration from the Rockstar Grand Theft Auto 6 release, which you can discover yourself below.

Version 3 will be a free update for existing customers, and the release is targeting next week, provided final preparations go as planned. It will support MSFS 2020 and MSFS 2024, and apply to the existing A319, A320, and A321 family.

We got Fenix V3 before GTA 6. 😉

“Good Things Come to Those Who Wait”

Today’s announcement, in line with how Fenix tends to present major news nowadays, follows a long period of radio silence regarding A320 development, during which little was known about upcoming releases.

In November 2025, the developer announced a change in priorities, shifting some resources away from its upcoming A320neo and A321neo to address issues with the existing A320ceo family.

At the time, Fenix identified the VNAV and Electronic Centralised Aircraft Monitoring (ECAM) implementations, as well as the lack of comprehensive aircraft state saving, as areas that needed improvement before the NEOs could be released. Fenix explained that it wanted to improve these areas of the existing product line first, before shipping another paid release.

Since then, information has been limited, although the developer quietly updated its product status page earlier this month, confirming that testing of the next big update was progressing well.

Today’s announcement was accompanied by a heavy dose of Fenix-style humour, and the YouTube video opened with a parody of Rockstar Games’ Grand Theft Auto VI.

Beyond the humour, the YouTube video offers a quite exhaustive look at what has been happening behind the scenes to create this Version 3 update.

Flight Model improvements

As expected, vertical navigation (VNAV) is a major part of this update, but Version 3 also includes wide-ranging changes to flight handling.

In the announcement video, Fenix’s Aamir Thacker explains how the team revisited inertia modelling in flight and how the aircraft’s mass responds to movement around the pitch, roll, and yaw axes. In MSFS 2024, Fenix has taken advantage of a newer inertia tensor system, enabling more detailed inertia modelling than in MSFS 2020. The flight model changes have been adapted to work in MSFS 2020, but the older simulator does not, at an underlying level, support the same technology.

According to Fenix, however, much of the improvement in flight handling comes from a fundamental flight model rewrite, a flight model largely externalised and independent of MSFS, meaning both 2024 and 2020 users should experience similar changes in how the Fenix flies.

Much attention has been given to rotation and flare behaviour, as well as the Fenix’s response to control inputs, in normal flight. The ground effect simulation has also been revisited to account for the updated flight model, which we mentioned previously. All of these changes subsequently required a rework of the fly-by-wire system, including adjustments to both pitch and roll control.

To validate these improvements, Fenix says it compared the digital version with five certified full-flight simulators, and incorporated feedback from real-world A320 pilots.

Interestingly, Fenix explains that it has deliberately made the aircraft’s roll response slightly more immediate than the full-flight training simulators, based on feedback from pilots who found this more realistic.

Reworked Flight Directors and Autopilot

Fenix has, for starters, rewritten the flight directors. Previously, Fenix says the flight directors could issue abrupt or overly reactive commands, making smooth hand-flying unnecessarily difficult. The new flight directors should use more anticipatory guidance, with commands presented earlier and more gradually, in a manner that Fenix says is closer to the real A320 family.

The autopilot has been reworked to accommodate these changes, including revised pitch and roll controllers, resulting in smoother responses. The new autopilot will not follow the FD’s commands slavishly, but will instead attempt to maintain the commanded flight path and avoid unnecessary movement.

The combined improvements also received positive feedback from Fenix’s real-world A320 pilot testing team, according to the developer.

Improvements to Ground Handling

Fenix Version 3 introduces a new “hybrid” ground-handling model, in which parts of the ground model are externalised outside MSFS, to provide greater control over taxi behaviour.

This new ground handling uses custom calculations at low taxi speeds, but gradually transitions back to MSFS’s ground-handling model as you go faster.

Fenix explains that this approach addresses several shortcomings with MSFS’s ground physics, such as excessive rolling resistance on wet surfaces, braking behaviour, and the thrust required to move on slopes. Tight turns should also be improved, as well as the difference between taxing with one or two engines running.

The new ground handling model should be more consistent across different airport surfaces and operating conditions, and Fenix says it will continue to make improvements, as mass user feedback comes in.

Redesigned EFB and Interactive Docs

The Electronic Flight Bag (EFB) has once again been redesigned, now housing a redesigned settings application and a new document management system. The settings interface has gotten a search function, making it easier to find the individual settings you want to change and avoiding all the previous menu searching.

Fenix has also added optional illustrations to explain which EFB settings control what.

In addition, a new “documents” application has been added to the EFB.

This includes an interactive Quick Reference Handbook (QRH), where you can search for procedures, bookmark frequently used documents, and check off items as you clear them. Normal checklists, engine-start procedures, and abnormal situations are among the included documents, and the relevant documents will be automatically highlighted in the event of an aircraft failure.

You, as a user, can also create your own documents, such as custom checklists and specific airline procedures, which you can add as Markdown files. These documents update in real time, meaning you can edit them outside the simulator and see the changes in the aircraft immediately.

Fenix’s Thacker says this feature could be useful for those wishing to follow specific airline Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) or for virtual airlines who wish to create their own documentation.

Artwork Improvements, Vibrating Cockpit Panels

Although most new artwork development has focused on the upcoming A320neo, several improvements have also been carried over to the existing Fenix A320 products. This includes the flight deck, which has seen many smaller changes to its modelling, texturing, and animations.

Among other additions is a physics-based animation system that accounts for the mass and rigidity of individual cockpit components.

This means that you will see panels, switches, and other fixtures vibrate in response to aircraft movement, including turbulence, hard landings, and even rolling over taxiway or runway lights. Even the EFB and pilot seats can now move and vibrate slightly, and the cockpit is no longer a solid, completely rigid model.

The cabin, especially the windows, has also been redone, along with textures and reflective properties, including more realistic refraction along the edges of the window panes, based on the real A320.

Numerous other fixes and optimisations have been made, though the complete list will be provided in next week’s release changelog.

New Sounds, Active Noise Cancellation

Another substantial revision has been made to the Fenix’s sound package, based on work undertaken for the A320neo.

This includes a custom binaural audio system, which should better recreate how sounds reach your ears and interact with the surrounding environment. The new system includes differences in how sounds are positioned, reflected, or partially obstructed by other objects. This implementation should work regardless of whether MSFS’s spatial 3D audio setting is enabled.

Both the CFM56 and IAE V2500 engine sound sets have seen extensive changes, including new recordings and adjustments to the layering of individual sounds. The improvements apply to all three A319, A320, and A321 variants and all their engine configurations, which retain their unique audio profiles.

Fenix has also added active noise cancellation; this can be turned on or off by stowing/removing the pilot’s headset from its overhead compartment in the cockpit. This ANC simulation not only lowers the overall volume but also alters the tonal characteristics of individual sound layers to reproduce the effect you’d hear with an ANC headset.

Cabin Intercom System

Perhaps one of the more unexpected additions is a detailed simulation of the A320’s Cabin Intercommunication Data System (CIDS). An enhanced Flight Attendant Panel (FAP) is now also usable.

The CIDS system is responsible for numerous cabin functions, including announcements, signs, lighting, interphone communications, and monitoring certain cabin systems. Fenix says it has recreated the underlying architecture of this system, and the simulation includes two CIDS directors, with one operating as the active unit and the other in standby.

The individual decoder and encoder units are also modelled, with Type A handling all passenger-related functions, while Type B manages crew communications and some other systems. These components have their own electrical supplies and failure logic, so faults in the aircraft can affect the information and functionality available to the cabin crew on their displays.

For example, disconnecting one CIDS director can trigger an ECAM warning, while the rest of the system is operational via its remaining director.

The system’s startup sequence was also shown off, and it indeed requires some time for the directors to initialise and complete their self-tests. During a cold-and-dark startup, you may notice the cabin signs and lighting behaving differently as the system boots up.

Flight Attendant Panel and Virtual Purser

The enhanced Flight Attendant Panel is the main interface for cabin systems. Here, you manage doors and emergency slides; adjust cabin lighting; trigger announcements; and control many other cabin functions. Fenix notes its rendition is based on real equipment, including a realistic startup sequence and certain display quirks displayed by the real system.

The FAP can also be accessed and managed on an external device via the EFB.

A feature called the “Virtual Purser” has been introduced to simulate cabin crew activity throughout the flight. The virtual purser can manage announcements, adjust cabin lighting based on the flight’s state, check passenger readiness, and respond to abnormal events.

For example, the Virtual Purser will conduct safety checks before departure and upon arrival, and the time required will vary with the number of passengers on board. The “VP” can also react to failure modes such as cabin decompression, which will interrupt normal service, and trigger appropriate announcements.

New Announcements and Toilets

The existing cabin announcement system has been expanded, where previous announcement packs are now called “airline packs”. These will be all-in-one solutions, where a single airline pack can include custom EFB wallpapers, FAP screensavers, and more flexible announcements that automatically change based on aircraft type, flight conditions, or cabin configuration.

The existing Fenix announcement files are still compatible, and the developer also plans to update the documentation for creating your own announcements.

The cabin simulation now includes more detailed door and emergency slide systems, where simulated sensors and failures can affect both the FAP and ECAM displays. The Virtual Purser can operate all doors and arm/disarm the slides, and the cabin temperatures are linked to the aircraft’s environmental control systems.

The lavatories are modelled to include potable water consumption, waste accumulation, and flushing systems. These adapt to onboard PAX numbers and flight conditions and can be serviced by GSX, while also having a small effect on the aircraft’s centre of gravity.

ECAM and Flight Warning Computer

One of Version 3’s most comprehensive changes concerns the Flight Warning Computer (FWC), the basis of the A320’s ECAM system. Here, much of the architecture has been replaced, and warnings are now generated, processed, and presented to the pilot more effectively.

Fenix explained that the previous implementation did not accurately reproduce several quirks of the real hardware, such as the timing of signals between various systems. The new system attempts to account for these differences, including the fact that each component may report information at different rates.

Although the processing architecture has been made faster, deliberate delays are included in the simulation to reproduce the timing of the real equipment. This has also resulted in lower CPU usage, despite increasing the frequency at which several systems operate.

Fenix demonstrated how the autopilot disconnect warning varied in timing between the previous Fenix implementation, other simulators, and the real A320 aircraft.

The system also supports intermediate radio altitude callouts, in which the aircraft combines various pre-recorded callouts to create custom, less common altitude values, under certain conditions.

Better ECAM Behaviour

Fenix has revised the formatting and timing of ECAM messages, including the behaviour of the clear and emergency cancel buttons. The two Flight Warning Computers are now simulated as separate units, which allows for small timing differences between their respective warnings and indications.

ECAM message overflow behaviour and the management of right-hand memos have also been looked at, alongside the logic used to group alerts.

Fenix has additionally added the Takeoff Surveillance Version 1 software, reflecting the system fitted to the A320ceo family. This system cross-checks the entered takeoff speeds against the selected flap configuration and trim settings, when performing the takeoff configuration test.

Fenix says it recorded the real ECAM displays using a high-speed camera, in order to replicate some differences in the frequency with which engine parameters and flight control positions are updated. This means values like engine temperature, N1, and N2 can appear to update at slightly different intervals, which is supposedly accurate.

ECAM display interruptions during certain electrical power transitions have also been added.

State Saving and Future Features

The new ECAM architecture is designed to support comprehensive aircraft state saving, such as the persistence of system conditions and failures that carry over between simulator sessions.

A Minimum Equipment List (MEL) system is also planned, allowing you to operate aircraft with certain equipment defects in accordance with the applicable limitations it dictates.

Full state saving and MEL are not coming in V3, but Fenix notes that V3’s rebuilt infrastructure has removed several technical limitations that previously prevented their implementation.

Newer Flight Warning Computer software standards are also coming, as part of a software-standard upgrade for the older Fenix aircraft. This package will be released free of charge to those who purchase the upcoming A320neo and also own the current A320ceo aircraft.

FADEC and Engine Systems

For V3, Fenix has rewritten the digital engine controllers (FADECs), partly driven by the upcoming A320neo project, which, of course, has two entirely new engines compared to Fenix’s existing offering. The previous engine architecture was designed around the current CFM56 and IAE V2500, making it difficult to incorporate the newer A320neo engines without significant compromises.

The developers have therefore rebuilt the engine system architecture to support four different engine families in a common framework, covering about 11 engine configurations in total.

These changes incorporate more detailed engine-start behaviour, better control logic, and additional abnormal/failure scenarios.

To demonstrate, Fenix showed how the CFM56 and IAE V2500 responded differently when electrical power is removed from their control systems during startup. The CFM56 could continue spinning under certain failure conditions, because of its dedicated FADEC alternator, and the mechanical quirks of its fuel shutoffs.

Under comparable conditions, the V2500 could not complete startup, due to differences in the electrically operated fuel shutoff valve, which prevented fuel delivery and thereby starving it.

Engine Options, FLEX

The FADEC improvements also provide more configuration options for different engine standards, including improved engine-start logic and adjustable pre-start motoring times for the V2500.

Fenix has also expanded the FLEX takeoff protections, now featuring an optional system that automatically commands TOGA thrust after eight seconds if a valid FLEX temperature has not been entered. The FADEC should also handle temperature entries more realistically, with each engine simulated independently to reflect different responses and warnings under abnormal conditions.

More features, such as better derated takeoff functionality, are planned for later.

VNAV Overhaul.

Fenix says they have rewritten much of the system, including its descent path construction, speed and altitude predictions, deceleration logic, and navigation restriction handling. Under the previous implementation, older, more generalised code was used, which could produce undesirable behaviour inconsistent with the specific Airbus system Fenix chose to simulate.

With Version 3, the team has set out to recreate the logic of the Thales FMS, one of the two FMS options for the A320 (Honeywell is the other), including quirks that may appear unusual to those unfamiliar with the system.

Fenix emphasised that not every A320 behaves the same way.

Thales vs Honeywell FMS

The Thales FMS simulated by Fenix uses a decelerated approach technique, where a level segment can be inserted into the descent profile for the aircraft to slow down for its final approach – also called a drop-and-drag technique- in which the aircraft descends to a specific altitude, levels off to decelerate, and then continues towards the runway.

Some Honeywell installations instead support a Continuous Descent Approach (CDA), where a continuous, shallower descent profile is used. According to Fenix, newer Honeywell systems can also have a latched descent option, which prioritises maintaining the calculated vertical path instead of strictly maintaining a target speed.

All of these things can produce very different descent profiles, between otherwise very similar aircraft, the developer notes.

However, because managed speed mode is rarely selected for the entire arrival, the differences between FMS systems may be less noticeable in day-to-day airline ops, where you usually have to intervene manually to change your speed as ATC asks for it.

Performance, Winds and Descent Calculations

Revisions have been made to the way the FMS calculates descent profiles, taking into account aircraft weight, wind conditions, and speed and altitude restrictions. Earlier, these calculations relied on predicted rather than actual conditions, Fenix explained, whereas the real Airbus uses wind information from five different altitudes, interpolating between them to calculate its descent. Differences between the predicted and actual winds can as a result affect how an aircraft follows its planned path.

Fenix Version 3 also adds adjustable idle and performance factors, which account for differences among airframes, including airframe age and aerodynamic wear and tear. All of these settings will influence descent calculations and fuel predictions, much as the real airlines adjust them for their real-world buses.

Idle, Geometric, Deceleration Segments

The new VNAV distinguishes between idle and geometric descent segments. In an idle segment, the aircraft will attempt to descend on idle thrust. In contrast, a geometric segment will follow a calculated path between altitude restrictions and use additional thrust where necessary.

These variations can affect how the aircraft manages speed; during an idle descent, the aircraft may reduce its vertical speed to about 500 feet per minute to decelerate. In a geometric segment, however, it could maintain its descent path and reduce thrust, thereby requiring speed brakes.

The “MORE DRAG” and “TOO STEEP” messages you might get on descent have also been tuned a bit more to represent the real aircraft better.

Fenix has also improved how the 320 calculates its deceleration point, allowing the Thales FMS to introduce one of those level segments we mentioned earlier to slow down before an approach. These segments may use internally calculated altitude constraints, which can result in slightly unusual and non-rounded values. The new VNAV can also generate multilevel segments when necessary and provide more accurate flight-mode and navigation-display indications.

LNAV Improvements

LNAV has likewise been rewritten in several areas, hopefully resulting in better flight path calculations.

Previously, the FMS could struggle to generate certain turns, particularly when the aircraft was travelling too fast relative to the available distance. The new system offers “more reliable calculations” and alternative ways to intercept or rejoin the planned route. The behaviour of holding patterns, procedure reversals, and lateral offsets has also been improved, with holds now maintaining more consistent entry and exit geometry, and offsets should be better integrated with the aircraft’s VNAV calculations. The ND and autopilot now also use the same calculated path, ensuring the route shown to the pilots matches the aircraft’s actual path.

Several other smaller improvements were mentioned; these include navigation database management revisions, which allow you to select previous data cycles or use the simulator’s ILS signal when scenery and navigation data don’t align.

IAE engine oil temperatures, radio standby frequencies, GSX integration, and a negative estimated fuel on board (EFOB) issue have also been further examined. Fenix has corrected rare situations in which the aircraft could detect itself as a TCAS target, and improved the “nearest airports” page and added new cockpit door-locking sounds.

A320neo?

Version 3 focuses on the existing A320ceo family, but much of the work in this update originated from the development of the A320neo and A321neo, Fenix says.

The developers stated they want to bring the improvements to existing customers first, with all the rewritten navigation, engine control, and flight warning systems forming a common foundation for both aircraft products in the future.

Most of the Fenix team is now working on the A320neos, though there is no confirmed release date.

And as mentioned, Fenix intends to add full aircraft state saving, Minimum Equipment List (MEL) support, and newer Flight Warning Computer software standards in later updates.

Version 3 Releases Next Week

Fenix Version 3 is expected sometime next week, so anytime Oct. 19 – Oct. 25, provided final testing goes to plan. V3 will be provided as a free update for existing Fenix customers and will be available on both MSFS 2020 and 2024.

Fenix also expects some issues to emerge following the release, given the scale and scope of the changes made, but says the team will be available to address them. The developers will release another blog post on their website, as well as the complete changelog, before launch.

With BlueBird Simulations also preparing to release its Boeing 757 on Oct. 23, the same week as V3’s release, it looks like there are some busy times ahead.
