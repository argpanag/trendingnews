The chilling drama that will make you side with a female serial killer

TheTools · 03.09.2026 17:40 · source · permalink

Described as a drama for the ‘post-Epstein world’, Furious inverts true crime’s dead-girl trope, allowing women to be messy and monstrous

5/5

Contains spoilers

Contains spoilers

It is difficult to think of a TV thriller darker and more transgressive than Furious, in which we are asked to empathise with a female serial killer who injects men with fentanyl and, enraptured, stays to watch their long, painful deaths. Catherine, played by 30-year-old Irish actress Lola Petticrew, keeps syringes in a tin bedazzled with plastic rhinestones, carries a bright sequinned bag and doodles pink hearts around the photographs of her next male victim.

Catherine presents like a child, and in a way, she still is one: psychologically suspended at the age she got trafficked into a paedophile ring, where she was starved, drugged, hit and raped on camera. The problem is her “memory sucks… because I got strangled so much”, so she uses an old photograph from that time to track down all the men she thinks may have hurt her, as well as find out what happened to her best friend Isabel – who died after giving birth aged 15.

The New Yorker described Furious – the season finale of which is now streaming on Disney+ – as a crime drama for the “post-Epstein world”, and although Elizabeth Meriwether (creator of The Dropout and sitcom New Girl) loosely based the show on the 1987 film Black Widow, its portrayal of sex trafficking so closely mirrors the details of Jeffrey Epstein’s convictions that at times Furious feels adapted from the pages of a newspaper.

The trafficking operation’s ringleader Jay Easton is an old, decrepit billionaire in cahoots with the FBI and cared for by his daughter, Emma, who – like Ghislaine Maxwell – dutifully arranges the delivery of underage girls like lambs to the slaughter. Far more horrifying than any of Catherine’s murders is the brief scene in which we watch Emma gently close the door on her wheelchair-bound father sitting opposite a blank-faced girl, casually telling him to “call me when you’re done”.

Some viewers might assume a show written by a woman featuring a female killer and all-male victims could be misandrist. Furious is feminist, yes, but not in a hectoring way. It remains subversive, entertaining and even funny during its bleakest moments, with prestige acting and cliché-free dialogue that breathes new life into the tired old police procedural. Running concurrently to Catherine’s killings is the abrasive friendship between NYPD-detective-turned-new-FBI-agent Alice, played by a mesmerising Emmy Rossum, and her spunky boss, Nora (Quincy Tyler Bernstine), who has some of the best lines in the genre.

Nora, a jaded drunk, thinks newbie Alice is too soft. But they are both motivated by buried grievances: for Nora, a historic sex-trafficking case that got away from her, and Alice by her own abusive past. In one of this year’s greatest TV twists, we see Alice’s colleague and love interest Danny (Scoot McNairy) – the show’s one and only good guy – open up Jay Easton’s hard drive of child-abuse videos to find one titled “Alice”. He glimpses just a second, but it’s enough to stop him getting into bed with her even when she needs him most. “I just can’t get your face from the video out of my head,” he says, with a sadness that lands like a lump in your throat.

While Catherine’s trauma is horribly simple, Alice’s is more complicated – and Rossum masterfully delivers one of the most daring and complicated depictions of trauma and PTSD ever seen on screen. Alice’s ex-boyfriend is fellow fed Marshall (an unsettling Jake Lacy), who used to beat Alice so badly he put her in hospital. The way he pulled her out of the shower to hit her means she now avoids washing for days. But, as can sometimes happen with trauma, the brain has warped the line between abuse and desire, so Alice uses an app for sex so rough she turns up to work with a busted lip.

This is a storyline only a talent such as Meriwether – a writer who so boldly interrogated female desire in last year’s astonishing Dying for Sex, starring Michelle Williams as a terminally ill woman who leaves her husband to embark on a sexual odyssey – could pull off. It also takes a talent such as Meriwether to have given disabled actor and comedian Steve Way the unusual opportunity to “fall in love” on screen, while also giving him one of the funniest TV scenes of the year, in which he mischievously uses his motorised wheelchair to keep evading detectives.

Most transgressive of all is how Furious justifies vengeance. Alice’s own destructive impulses eventually catch up with Catherine’s, the pair forming an alliance despite the fact that one is chasing the other. Both, ultimately, want Jay Easton punished, and Catherine senses there is more than professional duty motivating Alice’s quest for justice. “Have you ever killed someone?” Catherine asks her softly in the season finale. “It’s power, watching someone die. There’s this moment watching someone go where you’re not scared at all. And I was always scared.”

Alice finally finds her power when, after following Catherine to Jay Easton’s house, she is able to take revenge against him. Again, we are asked to empathise with such actions, as Easton tells Alice that her video was one of his favourites. “You’re the movie star!” he says, his eyes shining with grotesque joy.

Back in the office, Nora – brutally unsentimental, in a way we rarely see from women on screen – commands her to watch the video. If you want this job, you have to “face it”, she says. In a scene that most viewers will find hard to forget, we see Alice sit in front of the computer and watch what was done to her, before realising the video has been shared and downloaded more than 50,000 times. Furious has been renewed for season two, and you get the sense a furious Alice may pick up from where the now-imprisoned Catherine left off.

From Making a Murderer and Gone Girls to Broadchurch, Top of the Lake and The Ted Bundy Tapes, television is littered with dead girls and women who no longer have a voice, with true-crime docs and dramas endlessly psychoanalysing the motives of men. For all its horrors, Furious offers some rare mercies: its allows women to be messy and monstrous; and, crucially, to survive.

Season one of Furious is streaming on Disney+
