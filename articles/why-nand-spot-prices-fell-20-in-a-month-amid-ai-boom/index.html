Why NAND Spot Prices Fell 20% in a Month Amid AI Boom

TheTools · 16.11.2026 21:30 · source · permalink

NAND spot prices drop 20% in one month Smartphone shipments down 14%, PCs down 22% China's YMTC closes in as commodity-chip competition intensifies AI server SSD prices rise 15% to 20%

Memory chip prices are soaring on the artificial intelligence boom, but the NAND flash market is moving in the opposite direction. Spot prices for commodity NAND used in smartphones and PCs are falling quickly, while high-value products for AI data centers remain tight and firm. The same chip now commands sharply different prices depending on where it ends up.

Bank of America said in a recent report that NAND is the product most likely to face price correction first in the current memory upcycle, industry sources said on the 16th. NAND spot prices have fallen about 20% since the second half of last month. The price of a 512-gigabit NAND wafer dropped to $14.30, down 32% from the previous quarter, while 1-terabit products fell 19% to $24.90.

Only months ago, NAND makers were raising prices, citing supply shortages. Now buyers are demanding discounts on some products. The surge in memory prices has paradoxically begun to erode demand for finished devices.

The biggest factor is weak smartphone sales. Research firm Counterpoint Research expects global smartphone shipments to fall 13.9% this year from a year earlier to about 1.08 billion units. As higher memory costs feed into retail prices, consumers are delaying upgrades, shrinking the market.

Smartphone makers' responses are also cutting NAND demand. To contain costs, manufacturers are reducing base storage capacity or charging more for higher-capacity models. Falling unit sales combined with less NAND per device leave memory suppliers facing a double squeeze.

The PC market shows a similar pattern. Global PC shipments in the third quarter fell 22.4% from a year earlier to 55.35 million units, according to research firm Gartner. Rising component costs, including memory, are being passed on to consumers, who are postponing purchases. PC makers are also focusing on drawing down existing inventory rather than placing new memory orders.

The shift is appearing first in the NAND spot market. Unlike the contract market, where chipmakers and large customers trade under long-term agreements, the spot market is where distributors buy and sell volumes on a short-term basis. As orders from device makers dry up, module suppliers holding inventory are unloading stock, accelerating the price decline. TrendForce also cited weak smartphone and PC demand, along with inventory clearance by module makers, as the main drivers of the recent drop in NAND spot prices.

Chinese competitors add to the pressure. While the DRAM market is led by three players — Samsung Electronics (005930.KS), SK hynix (000660.KS) and Micron — the NAND market includes Japan's Kioxia, U.S.-based SanDisk and China's Yangtze Memory Technologies Corp. (YMTC), making the competitive landscape far more complex. YMTC's share of NAND revenue reached 14% in the second quarter, up 5 percentage points from a year earlier, according to Counterpoint Research. That puts it rapidly closing in on Samsung Electronics at 28% and SK hynix at 19%.

YMTC is building influence in particular in the consumer NAND market, where price competitiveness matters most. As global suppliers concentrate on high-value products, Chinese makers are expanding supply of commodity chips, intensifying price competition in parts of the product range.

Production equipment is another force driving the market's split. Bank of America said older fabrication lines, which account for 20% to 30% of global NAND wafer capacity, are not suited to making the advanced chips required for high-performance enterprise solid-state drives. That means existing plants cannot be quickly converted to high-value production lines even as demand for AI data center products grows. It is why shortages in high-value products and inventory buildups in commodity chips can occur at the same time.

The NAND market as a whole is not in a slump, however. Demand for enterprise SSDs remains strong as big U.S. technology companies expand AI data center investment. Enterprise SSDs accounted for 48% of total NAND bit shipments in the second quarter, up sharply from 26% a year earlier, according to Counterpoint Research. TrendForce expects enterprise SSD contract prices to rise 15% to 20% in the fourth quarter from the previous quarter.

As AI use spreads from training to inference, SSDs that store large volumes of data and retrieve it quickly are becoming more important. Nvidia has also unveiled technology that uses SSDs to handle data generated during AI inference more efficiently.

The contrast with DRAM is even sharper. Samsung Electronics, SK hynix and Micron are concentrating capacity on high-bandwidth memory and server DRAM, tightening supply even for commodity DRAM. While expanding HBM output reduces the capacity available for commodity DRAM, in NAND the combination of Chinese output growth and the burden of older equipment is widening the supply-demand gap between product categories.

The key question is first-quarter contract prices next year. Bank of America expects NAND prices for smartphones and PCs to fall 5% to 10% from the previous quarter, while enterprise products hold flat or rise slightly. That could pull the overall NAND average selling price down modestly. It is difficult to conclude that falling spot prices have already translated into lower contract prices across the NAND market, but buyers are likely to press harder for discounts in early-year negotiations.

"Unlike DRAM, the industry broadly agrees that NAND cannot sustain the steep price gains seen so far," an official in the memory industry said. Next year's NAND conditions will ultimately depend on how much growth in high-value products for AI data centers can offset weakness in commodity chips for smartphones and PCs.
