‘Every last cent gone’: Sharron and Brett’s retirement derailed after $550,000 nightmare

TheTools · 17.11.2026 19:19 · source · permalink
  • Exclusive
  • Australia
  • VIC

November 17, 2026 - 7.19pm

Exclusive: Sharron and Brett Bosworth had been dreaming about retiring from full-time work, renting out their home and caravanning across Australia.

“Right now, it doesn’t look as promising as it did five years ago,” Sharron told nine.com.au.

“We’re just kicking the can down the road now.”

Sharron and Brett Bosworth were contacted by a “cold caller” who led them to roll their money into a self-managed super fund (SMSF). Supplied

The Ballarat couple, both aged in their late 50s, face possibly working well into their 70s after becoming entangled in the collapse of First Guardian Master Fund and losing over $550,000.

Sharron said the nightmare began with a cold call in 2019.

She and her husband had been considering switching supers with a goal of retiring within 10 years.

A “lead generator” for Melbourne financial advice firm United Global Capital (UGC), which is now in liquidation, contacted them after Brett clicked on a Facebook advertisement.

“We wanted to retire at the same time,” Sharron said.

“Technically, I probably had another 10 years left of working.

“But I’m going to probably go for another 13 now.”

By 2021, the Bosworths had rolled most of their superannuation – a total of $425,688.23 – into a self-managed super fund (SMSF).

UGC recommended that they invest 95 per cent of it into First Guardian Master Fund, a single, high-risk product.

They paid about $12,800 in fees for this to happen.

“It felt like a lot of smoke and mirrors,” Sharron explained.

“To be honest with you, we didn’t really know [about First Guardian] because there wasn’t a lot of communication. We were talking to a lot of different people.

“My spidey-sense was tingling.... but I just thought it might be the kind of industry with a high turnover of people.”

Sharron and Brett agree they should have trusted their gut instinct.

First Guardian, operated and managed by Falcon Capital Ltd, collapsed in April 2025.

This impacted roughly 6000 investors who lost an estimated $446 million – mostly in retirement funds.

“Basically, all of our money was frozen and we couldn’t take it out,” Sharron said.

“Every last cent was gone.”

UGC entered liquidation in 2024 and the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) launched an investigation into UGC, director Joel James Hewish and its related entities.

But it wasn’t until First Guardian was wound up almost a year later that the Bosworths were alerted to the damage done to their SMSF.

A determination from their complaint against UGC with the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) found that the couple’s full loss, including the estimated returns, was $551,489.95.

AFCA found that UGC’s recommendation of a single, high-risk, growth investment in First Guardian was inappropriate and that the Statement of Advice (SOA) rendered by the firm “did not demonstrate the financial firm took adequate steps to assess the suitability of the SMSF structure for the complainants”.

“Instead, it is not clear why having an SMSF was beneficial for each of the complainants on any basis other than to facilitate the investment in First Guardian,” the determination notes.

The chances of recouping even half of their combined loss are low.

UGC’s insolvency means they will be eligible for the Compensation Scheme of Last Resort, which can pay up to $150,000.

Sharron and Brett are considered as a single complainant, despite the SMSF containing both of their retirement funds.

Fellow investor and one of the founders of SOS Save Our Super Melinda Kee said there are many husband-and-wife victims of the First Guardian or similar Shield Master Fund who are regarded as singular victims.

Sharron said her retirement has been pushed back by several years as they reassess the damage done by First Guardian’s collapse. Supplied

“Once their money was combined, two individual retirement savings became assets of one SMSF,” Kee said.

“When the investment failed and those people sought redress, that legal structure could determine how their complaint and, ultimately, their compensation would be treated.

“The structure consumers were advised to establish can become the very structure that limits their redress when the advice fails.”

It has been a tough few years. But Sharron and Brett say they are coming to terms with their uncertain financial future.

Sharron “thanks God” they left some money in their industry super funds when they moved their money into an SMSF.

“I’ve never shed a tear over it though, but the knot in the stomach gets bigger and bigger,” Sharron said.

“We are pretty much starting from scratch. As of [early November] we had about $46,000 in our superannuation.

“This has set us back a few years... but worst come to worst, we’d sell the house, we’d have to downsize, and then we’d have something.

“We have contingency plans. Our retirement plan is to hop in the car and tow the caravan and go around Australia.”

ASIC has cancelled UGC’s Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) and banned director Hewish for 10 years from providing financial services.

While it is in liquidation, UGC remains an AFCA member, so investors can lodge a complaint and be eligible for the CSLR.

ASIC found UGC “lured people into investing their retirement savings in UGC-related products by having calls made to prospective clients using details including those obtained from a third-party website operator, offering them a free superannuation ‘health check’.”

After First Guardian’s collapse, the federal government and Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) has committed to cracking down on financial lead generation.

As of 2026, any financial advice licensees who “use or benefit from harmful, high-risk lead generation models risk losing their Australian Financial Services (AFS) licence”.

For First Guardian investors like the Bosworths, it is too little, too late.

“It means neither here nor there to me because the damage is done for us,” Sharron added.

“We’re trying to rally the government pay now and recover later.”
