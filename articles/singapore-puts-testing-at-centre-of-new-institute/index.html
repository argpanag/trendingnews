Singapore puts testing at centre of new institute

TheTools · 17.11.2026 01:06 · source · permalink

Singapore’s Future of Finance Institute has officially begun operations with a programme to develop practical AI risk management resources, including work on testing, assurance and third-party technology.

The Monetary Authority of Singapore announced the launch earlier this month, following the institute’s establishment announcement in June. Its initial work covered AI adoption and risk management through PathFin.ai, BuildFin.ai and SafeFin.ai.

MAS managing director Chia Der Jiun described the institute as a platform to “pool expertise and collaborate in fast-moving technology areas such as AI”.

SafeFin.ai was tasked with developing resources to help financial institutions implement MAS’ AI risk management guidelines, including for agentic AI.

Its work would extend the MindForge toolkit’s treatment of use-case assessments, testing and assurance, and third-party AI.

BuildFin.ai would develop deployment practices, tools and infrastructure, with particular attention to AI agents. PathFin.ai would support knowledge sharing and assessment of solutions’ practical usefulness.

MAS has appointed Alan Lim interim chief executive of the institute, alongside his existing role leading its Financial Infrastructure and AI Office. Further details of SafeFin.ai’s work were to follow, according to the regulator.

Building on MindForge

The initiative built on Project MindForge, launched in 2023. In March 2026, MAS announced a toolkit developed with 24 financial institutions and industry partners, covering traditional, generative and emerging agentic AI.

Its operationalisation handbook addressed governance, risk identification, lifecycle controls and the capabilities needed to sustain those arrangements. Accompanying case studies documented financial institutions’ implementation experience.

At that March launch, MAS chief fintech officer Kenneth Gay said the authority was committed to “strengthening of AI governance and risk management practices across the industry”.

For QA leaders, the significance is the attempt to connect governance expectations with resources that teams can use when evaluating systems. Agent testing can require evidence about permitted actions, escalation and recovery, alongside the quality of generated answers.

The November announcement set out a development agenda; it did not unveil a completed testing standard or a new certification requirement.

Its practical value will depend on the tools and implementation guidance that emerge, and how readily institutions can apply them to their own systems and suppliers.

Wider testing expectations

The institute’s launch followed MAS’ October AI risk management guidelines, which placed testing alongside human oversight, data governance, cybersecurity, monitoring and change management. Institutions were expected to identify their AI applications, assess their materiality and maintain controls proportionate to their risks.

“Realising these benefits sustainably requires financial institutions to understand and manage the risks that come with increasingly capable AI systems,” MAS deputy managing director Ho Hern Shin said.

The framework also addressed supplier accountability. Financial institutions remained responsible for AI used in their services, including technology supplied or operated externally. They were expected to assess its suitability and address gaps in provider assurance through appropriate safeguards.

That gave SafeFin.ai’s planned work on third-party AI a practical context: banks needed ways to assess supplier technology within their own operations.

The guidelines allowed institutions to use existing governance arrangements where these provided sufficient oversight.

Implementation was phased, with expectations in Sections 3 and 4 applying from 7 October 2027, followed by Sections 5 and 6 on 7 October 2028. MAS also planned to consult the sector in 2027 on additional guidance for agentic AI.

Cross-bank fraud detection

Alongside its governance work, MAS was testing AI’s operational value through a fraud-detection proof-of-value involving GovTech, the Singapore Police Force and five unnamed banks.

The project examined whether combining information across banks and public agencies could identify scam accounts and suspicious transactions earlier than institutions working independently. Criminal proceeds can pass through several banks, leaving each institution with only a partial view of the payment chain.

“The aim is to detect sooner, intervene faster and reduce losses,” Chia explained.

In its September update, MAS expected findings by the end of 2026. These could inform improvements to banks’ own models and a decision on whether to establish an industry-level utility. The regulator had not disclosed the participating institutions or detailed performance criteria.

For assurance teams, the trial illustrated the need to evaluate an entire service. Detection speed, false alerts and performance across different customer populations mattered alongside model accuracy.

Shared infrastructure also raised questions about access controls, delayed information and how banks would continue operating during an outage.

Those challenges gave the institute’s focus on practical implementation a wider relevance, connecting AI risk management with the reliability of financial services.
