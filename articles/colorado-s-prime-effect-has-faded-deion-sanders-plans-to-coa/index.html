Colorado’s ‘Prime Effect’ has faded. Deion Sanders plans to coach his way out of it

TheTools · 04.09.2026 03:51 · source · permalink

College

Football

LIVE

31s ago

Deion Sanders changed his approach to roster building to rebuilt what he already built at Colorado. Nick Cammett / Getty Images

The Athletic has live coverage of Colorado vs. Georgia Tech.

Colorado athletic director Fernando Lovo recently decided to go for a solo stroll around campus. He stopped inside the University Memorial Center student building and noticed a massive banner that serves as a tribute. As another college football season begins, it also marks the passage of time.

The banner lists the details of “The Prime Effect” — what Deion Sanders’ arrival as Colorado coach in December 2022 has done for the university, football program and city of Boulder. Lovo described the various statistics plastered on the banner, including the hundreds of millions of dollars of economic impact, the explosion of merchandise and season-ticket sales, the number of CU applicants surging, and the once-gaudy TV viewership numbers.

“All those things are real,” said Lovo, who replaced longtime AD Rick George in January, “and you can’t deny the correlation between those numbers and Coach Prime.”

But is the banner an indication that Sanders’ most significant impact on Colorado football has already been made?

Sanders is entering his fourth season and coming off a 3-9 record in 2025 in which the Buffs won one conference game. After another staff shuffle and roster redo, the Buffs open Thursday at Georgia Tech and the coach, unsurprisingly, is not bothered by preseason pontifications from college football experts around the country that the Buffaloes will once again struggle to compete in the Big 12.

“If my kids and my coaches and our staff don’t understand who they are, we have a problem,” Sanders said at Big 12 media days in July. “They’re not going to allow you to identify who we are. Just because our guys were snubbed off a poll that’s probably not going to be consistent with the end of the season, we don’t give a darn.”

For some alumni, success in Year 4 under Sanders won’t be measured by the afterglow of the Prime Effect, which included the high point of a 9-4 season in 2024 and a Heisman Trophy. It will be about having a say in the conference standings in October and November.

“Obviously more wins. That’s got to be the consensus, right?” said former Colorado wide receiver Mike Pritchard, who was part of the 1990 national championship team. “But the depths from which the program has come to where it is now has been remarkable, to be honest with you. Only one person could’ve done that in today’s environment.”

Coach Prime, of course.

Colorado was must-see TV in 2023 and 2024 because of Sanders’ unabashed introduction to Power 4 football, his son, Shedeur, the star quarterback, and Travis Hunter, a two-way phenom who eventually hoisted a Heisman Trophy. Celebrities flocked to Folsom Field on Saturdays.

After Shedeur Sanders and Hunter left Boulder for the NFL, the 2025 calendar year served as a reminder of how swiftly things can change. Before the start of last season, Sanders’ bladder was removed after a routine checkup revealed a cancerous growth. The Buffaloes lost seven of their final eight games, including a 53-7 loss to Utah and a 52-17 loss to Arizona.

“He hates the situation that they’re in, in terms of, he wants to correct it,” Fox Sports analyst and former Colorado quarterback Joel Klatt said.

After one of his most taxing years, Sanders decided to shift from a roster-building ethos that was untenable long-term. The Buffaloes ditched chasing transfers who were former four- and five-star high school athletes from blue-blood programs who had yet to be consistent contributors at the college level. They went searching for players who were healthy, reliable and put up numbers no matter where they played before entering the portal, even if that was at the Group of 6 level or lower.

“This offseason, he just had a willingness to switch on roster building and look at production and experience over potential,” Klatt said. “In particular, because it’s not flashy and it doesn’t generate the type of interest outside the walls of the facility that maybe they had previously.”

The Buffaloes have one of the most experienced teams in college football in 2026. The Athletic researched which Power 4 teams have the most returning snaps, starts and games played before the start of the season, and Colorado is ranked fifth in the Power 4. Colorado beat out Power 4 teams who were chasing hard after the likes of former San Jose State wide receiver Danny Scudero (1,297 yards, 10 touchdowns and 88 receptions in 2025), former Miami (Ohio) wide receiver Kam Perry (976 yards, six touchdowns and 43 receptions in 2025) and former Texas wideout DeAndre Moore Jr. (532 yards, four touchdowns and 38 receptions in 2025).

Defensively, the Buffs added former TCU linebacker Randon Fontenette (nearly 2,000 career snaps), former Texas starting linebacker Liona Lefau (68 total tackles in 2025) and former Tulane defensive lineman Santana Hopper (4.5 sacks in 2025).

The change in philosophy extends beyond recruiting almost exclusively out of the transfer portal, too. Colorado’s 2027 high school recruiting class already has 20 commitments, more than any other cycle under Sanders. By comparison, Colorado had fewer than 15 signees in each high school recruiting class in 2024 and 2025. The turnover of high school signees for the Buffaloes has been dizzying: Only 21 of 44 high school recruits from 2023 to 2025 returned for their second season in Boulder.

Sanders was recently asked about the coaching staff’s approach to recruiting this year.

“I just got more involved than I previously have been,” Sanders said. “We just took more onus on what we wanted.”

“We live in a world where the names on the back of the jerseys change a lot — that’s happened here and it’s happened all across the country,” said Lovo, who came to Colorado after one year at New Mexico. “I need to make sure I’m giving (Sanders) all the tools he needs to go evaluate and then go place the requisite value on those types of players because I think when we get that right — and I’m really confident with what I’ve seen this offseason — I do believe we can compete consistently in this conference.”

Anything is possible in the Big 12, after all. Just two years ago, Arizona State made the College Football Playoff after being picked to finish dead last in the preseason media poll. Texas Tech, an afterthought for most of its football history, has emerged as a disruptor (and contender) in the past few years.

Perhaps Sanders’ most significant offseason move was hiring Brennan Marion as offensive coordinator. The 39-year-old spent one year as head coach at Sacramento State in 2025 after leading one of the most dynamic offenses in the country during two seasons as offensive coordinator at UNLV in 2023 and 2024. Marion’s “Go-Go Offense” is a high-tempo offensive attack that prioritizes balance — something Colorado has not had since Sanders arrived.

In 2023, Colorado was the worst rushing team in college football, averaging 68.9 yards per game.

In 2024, the pass-heavy attack essentially negated any ground game as the Buffaloes again finished dead last in the country in rushing yards per game with 65.2.

In 2025, Colorado improved to average 125 rushing yards.

In his one year at Sacramento State, Marion’s offense ranked third in the FCS with 262 yards rushing per game. In his last year as OC at UNLV in 2024, the Rebels were seventh in FBS, averaging 244 yards on the ground. Several former Sacramento State offensive playmakers followed Marion to Boulder, highlighted by running back Damian Henderson II and wideout Ernest Campbell.

Klatt said Marion’s hiring should help former five-star recruit, quarterback Julian “Juju” Lewis, thrive. There are expected growing pains. The offensive line is completely turned over, including former five-star left tackle Jordan Seaton, who transferred to LSU in the offseason for a reported $4 million. As has been the case since Sanders was hired, Klatt said the Buffaloes must improve in the trenches on offense and defense, something that has yet to be accomplished in the Coach Prime era.

What has been accomplished during Sanders’ tenure has been priceless: relevancy. But life without Shedeur Sanders and Hunter (and wins) is less tantalizing to the casual TV viewer. After being a top-10-watched team in 2023 and 2024, viewership plummeted in 2025, from an average of 4.5 million in 2023 to under 2 million in 2025.

Lovo is not deterred. He said the Colorado athletic department recently set a school record for fundraising with $37.7 million donated or pledged. Sanders, Lovo said, is the main driver. The university reported a $27 million budget deficit for the athletic department, which the school believes it can whittle down through a banner fundraising year and revenue from additional ticket sales to Colorado’s seven home games in 2026. After conferring with multiple industry sources familiar with roster budgets, The Athletic estimates that Colorado’s roster cost between $18 million and $21 million.

In March 2025, George gave Sanders a five-year, $54 million contract extension, making him one of the highest-paid head coaches in the country. George, long Sanders’ biggest advocate, retired in November after 13 years as Colorado’s AD. Lovo believes Sanders can fulfill the grandiose promises the coach consistently made along the way.

“These next five years are critical in college football,” Lovo said. “To have someone like Coach Prime, who himself is just a marketing guru and genius, to help leverage that and be prepared for when success comes and really lean into that, that does give us a leg up on a lot of folks. And we’re going to use that.”

To Klatt, success means being bowl eligible at a minimum.

“What Colorado has to maintain is a level that they would be of intrigue and interest,” Klatt said. “You do that by being competitive on the field and driving interest, both regionally and nationally.

“If you want those things, you have to have a product that is desirable. I would categorize it as a must. Colorado has to get to that point. There’s not an option, or else you find yourself in a really precarious situation where your community feels the impact, your university feels the impact and you might feel the impact from a program perspective on the long-term viability of the entire department based on what seats you find yourself in.”

Meanwhile, those in Boulder are eager to see if Sanders can replicate the fervor of the first two years of his tenure, when visitors flooded the picturesque Pearl Street Mall. If he can, more gaudy numbers will be updated on the banner inside the student building.

A fall season with the Buffs in the competitive mix goes a long way, says Dave Query, who owns local restaurants like The Post and West End Tavern. He called 2023 and 2024 “enjoyable chaos.”

“It’s not the fever pitch that it was,” Query said, “but it’s still a good head cold.”

— Manny Navarro contributed to this story.
