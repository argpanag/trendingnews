Flames' Huberdeau seeks more answers as he returns to action

TheTools · 17.11.2026 13:46 · source · permalink

CALGARY — The toughest part of Jonathan Huberdeau’s hip resurfacing recovery wasn’t the pain, the gruelling rehabilitation or even the first three months he spent unable to skate.

It was the uncertainty.

Would he play again? Would the pain return? Would he ever be the same player? And, perhaps most importantly, would he be able to enjoy a normal life without constantly wondering what his hip had in store for him?

Fortunately, he had someone to turn to.

“I think it's just ups and downs,” Huberdeau said, describing the mental gymnastics of an eight-month rehabilitation following surgery.

“Are you gonna come back, or are you gonna be as good as before? Better than before? Not as good? There was some point that I was hurting a lot, and you're like, ‘OK, well, is it gonna be like this for the rest of my life?”

He pauses.

“Then I spoke with Cools, and everything was good.”

Devin Cooley, the Flames' resident philosopher, certainly has a gift for putting things in perspective. 

Whatever the secret, Huberdeau is finally ready to put the uncertainty behind him and get back to playing hockey.

The 33-year-old Flames winger will make his long-awaited return to game action Wednesday night at the Saddledome when he suits up for the Calgary Wranglers on an injury conditioning loan that represents the biggest milestone yet in his comeback from surgery last March.

Eight months after going under the knife, he's nervous, excited and understandably eager to find out what happens when the puck drops.

“There's always a nervousness,” he admitted. 

“I mean, I haven't played for a while. I think that's just a normal part. But I just gotta take it and embrace it.”

The date has been a moving target since he shut things down last February, with Huberdeau wisely resisting the temptation to put a firm timetable on a procedure so uncommon among hockey players that there isn't much of a playbook.

Patrick Kane and Yanni Gourde, two players who have navigated similar surgery and rehabilitation, were among those he contacted for advice.

Gourde returned remarkably quickly, in roughly five months. 

“There's not really a lot of data,” he explained. 

“Not a lot of guys that had done it. Everybody's different.”

Kane was particularly helpful, answering Huberdeau's questions about symptoms and what to expect. But even comparisons were difficult, with players experiencing discomfort in different areas around the hip.

The good news? Since surgery, Huberdeau says he's had no pain.

He also believes he's as strong as he's ever been, having spent months building the muscles around the resurfaced joint.

That strength will be tested in a hockey environment Wednesday, where avoiding contact is about as realistic as expecting a Calgary winter without snow.

Huberdeau has been skating with the Flames for some time and has engaged in some battles with teammates, but he hasn't had the benefit of many full practices with the big club. There's a considerable difference between controlled contact and somebody trying to separate you from the puck with game-night rigour.

He's thinking about that first real collision.

The bigger concern, though, is simply getting his game legs back.

Nine months is an eternity in the hockey world, especially for a skilled forward whose game has traditionally revolved around his hands, instincts and ability to create offence.

There's also a little unfinished business waiting for him in the warm-up.

Huberdeau has never taken a rookie lap. They weren't doing them when he made his NHL debut in 2012. After all these years, he'll get to experience the awkward privilege of being the new guy again.

He'll also be wearing No. 12, a choice that could raise eyebrows in Calgary given that Jarome Iginla's iconic number hangs in the Saddledome rafters.

Huberdeau was quick to clear that up.

“It's actually my brother's number,” he laughed. 

“No disrespect for Jarome. It’s the AHL, so it’s not the same.”

Huberdeau wants to keep things uncomplicated, get comfortable playing at game speed, regain confidence in what his hip can handle and help the Wranglers win.

“Scoring a goal would be nice, obviously, but just having fun,” he said.

“I don't think I'm gonna come back and be back at the top, and stuff like that. It's gonna take a little adjustment and a while.”

The plan is to play a couple of games in the AHL before potentially joining the Flames somewhere on their five-game eastern road trip that starts Monday in Ottawa.

But he doesn’t want to think too far ahead.

More than anything, he wants to enjoy the remaining years of his NHL career without the hip pain that had become an unwelcome companion before every game, after every game and even on days off.

“It's fun to play hockey, but you gotta think about the future and stuff,” he said.

On Wednesday, the future finally starts looking a little more like hockey.

There will be nerves, a rookie lap, a new number and, eventually, that first proper hit.

And somewhere in the building, Cools can probably take a little credit for getting him there.
