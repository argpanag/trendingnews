However, analysts at broker Yongan Futures said: "Ore prices will likely bottom out following a wave of steep falls, given that its valuation has stayed relatively low."

BHP unveils plans to double copper output in South Australia by 2040

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

BHP, the world's top copper miner, has revealed plans to double its South Australian copper production by 2040, at a cost of up to $US18.1 billion ($24.7 billion), with a final investment decision due in the first half of next year.

BHP said it had found a path to raise annual copper production from the operations to 650,000 metric tonnes, with investment of $US14.2 billion to $US18.1 billion.

That would boost copper production from the region, which BHP says is the world's second biggest for copper, and take it close to the capacity of the world's largest copper mine, Escondida in Chile.

The world's largest listed miner aims to produce 2 million tonnes of the metal, which is key to electrification, from its global operations by the middle of the next decade, and expects the Copper South Australia operations to deliver about one third of its copper growth to 2035.

When including expected by-products such as uranium, gold and silver from Copper SA, BHP said that global production will be equivalent to 2.5 million tonnes of copper per year.

The Copper SA operations include Olympic Dam and the Carrapateena and Prominent Hill mines, which it acquired in 2023, and a 2018 discovery at Ankata, which was originally named Oak Dam.

BHP said it expects consolidation, the introduction of block caving, which is a type of bulk mining, and extended processing to significantly cut operating costs.

As part of the expansion, BHP will increase the capacity of its uranium processing plant to increase production by about half, to 5,000–6,000 tonnes a year.

Australian business activity slows as new orders and output falls

S

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

The latest reading of activity in Australia's business heartland shows the pulse is weakening with the S&P Global "flash" Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) slipping deeper into contraction.

The flash, or preliminary, composite PMI reading came in at 48.7 points for November, down from 49.6 points in the previous month, where 50 is the break-even point between expanding and contracting activity.

The composite PMI combines activity in both the manufacturing and services sectors.

The contraction was driven by the sharpest decline in new order and output in five years.

S&P Global's key findings include:

  • Business activity fell at the sharpest pace in more than five years amid fresh reductions in both overall and overseas new business.
  • Spare capacity emerged and job shedding resumed.
  • Companies were also at their least optimistic in three years.
  • On the price front, the rates of both input cost and output price inflation softened in November as demand eased.

S&P Global economist Jingyi Pan said latest decline in demand reflected the impact of higher interest rates, while softening domestic and external conditions also contributed.

"Notably, inflationary pressures eased for the first time in several months as demand softened across the board, speaking to the effectiveness of the recent tightening of financial conditions," Pan said

"That said, employment also fell alongside a marked reduction in business confidence among private sector firms, pointing to more subdued output conditions in the near-term at a time when the elevated inflation has yet to be fully brought under control."

From a local macro perspective, the focus this week is on Wednesday's October inflation data from the ABS.

The market's best idea for the headline Consumer Price Index is a chunky 0.4% rise, which would lift the annual rate from 4.3% to 4.8%.

Transport, or in this case the cost of fuel, is expected to be the biggest contributor.

The RBA's preferred measure, trimmed mean CPI, which excludes volatile categories, is also expected to rise by 0.4% quarter on quarter and take the annual pace down a notch from 3.7% to 3.6%, still too high for the RBA's liking.

The credit card surcharge change may well muddy things at the margin.

We will also get the first glimpse of Q3 GDP through the release of two key partials: construction work (Wednesday) and private sector capital expenditure (Thursday).

Construction work declined in Q2, but Westpac forecasts a modest rebound driven by a pickup in residential construction and electricity-based building, including data centres, while a slowdown in the public sector may prove to be a drag.

For private sector capex, Westpac is forecasting a solid 10% jump after a decline in Q2.

An 83% surge Automated Data Processing (ADP) equipment imports in Q2 points to a lift in capex plans, according to Westpac.

Overseas, the key data point will be the US core private final consumption deflator (aka the Fed's preferred measure of inflation) for October, which is forecast to show inflation creeping ever higher, from 3.1% (yoy) in September to 3.2% last month.

We will also get a measure of developed economies business conditions via an array of Purchasing Managers' Index (PMI) numbers released early in the week.

Wall Street retreat becomes a rout, ASX to fall again

By Stephen Letts

By Stephen Letts

Clunk. Friday's capitulation on Wall Street was savage, but not entirely unexpected.

For six consecutive sessions the market was in a doleful retreat.

On the seventh day it became a disorderly flight out of not only anything related to AI, but most other sectors, markets and commodities.

  • S&P 500: -3.7%
  • Dow: -1.9%
  • Nasdaq: -5.3%

It capped Wall Street's worst week in more than six years, with the S&P 500 shedding 8.6% to be down almost 12% since its October high.

 A "correction", a 10% or more fall from a peak, is the anodyne word for it. Those forced to sell to cover losses, or see their superannuation balances shrink, would probably use something stronger.

The once high-flying US tech sector, as measured by the Nasdaq index, is now lower than it was at the start of the year.

The pain was widespread. The broad Eurostoxx600 fell 2% and the emerging markets' index dropped 3.1%.

Add in Asia's decline earlier in the day and MSCI's gauge of global stocks shed 3.3%. Its weekly decline of 8.1% was the largest since early 2020.

There's no circuit breaker in sight for the ASX. Futures trading points to 0.6% loss on the ASX 200 today, having dropped more than 5% last week, its worst week in mid 2022.

Wall Street's so-called "fear gauge", the Cboe VIX index, jumped to a seven-month high.

ANTHROPIC NOT IMMUNE IN TECH SELL-OFF

Even demand for market's new "must have" stock, Anthropic, which houses, among other things, the Claude chatbot, fizzled out to an extent.

Having leapt by 40% in its first few hours as a listed entity on Thursday, it's been on the slide ever since.

It fell another 9% on Friday, and at $US88.30/share it has subsided back towards the $US83 level of its debut.

A sizzling quarterly report earlier in the week from the scandal-tainted chip maker Nvidia failed to change sentiment about the risks associated with artificial intelligence and the massive, and often circular, investments being made in the sector.

"Nvidia reported what might have been one of the greatest quarters in corporate earnings history; they did everything right," Horizon Investment Services CEO Chuck Carlson told Reuters.

"A lot of people, myself included, were looking to the Anthropic debut to some extent as a clearing event, and it wasn't."

The Philadelphia SE Semiconductor Index, which has powered much of the broader stock market's gains in recent months, was up more than 100% in mid-June. It's now down almost 20% since the start of the year.

Among AI hyperscalers, Amazon fell 3.9%, while Meta dropped 5.9%.

The flight to safety saw the yield on the benchmark US 10-year treasury notes tumble more than 10 basis points, while the US dollar index was little changed as higher-than-expected inflation data reinforced expectations of rate hike from the Fed next month.

However, the Aussie dollar slipped again — largely due to the belief that the next move from the RBA would be a cut, rather than another hike and its relationship with commodity prices that are also falling.

COMMODITIES DUMPED

Gold fell for its fifth straight session, down 2.5% to $US3,532/ounce, its lowest level in more than a year. Bullion fell almost 10% over the week.

It was followed down by other precious metals; silver (-4.1%), platinum (-3.4%) and palladium (-2.1).

"Higher interest rates, stronger dollar, the prospects for ongoing high inflation, which prompts the Fed to continue to raise interest rates — all of those factors that we have been seeing — negatively affect gold," High Ridge Futures director of metals trading David Meger said.
