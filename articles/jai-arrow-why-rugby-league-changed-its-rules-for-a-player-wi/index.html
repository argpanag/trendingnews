Jai Arrow: Why rugby league changed its rules for a player with MND – ‘I’ll fight till the death’

TheTools · 04.09.2026 20:29 · source · permalink

An emotional Jai Arrow of the Rabbitohs thanks the crowd Darrian Traynor/Getty Images

The ovation consumed Allianz Stadium as Jai Arrow emerged from the tunnel and led out his South Sydney Rabbitohs team-mates, holding back the tears he knew were bound to come.

For game 99, it was to the soundtrack of “You’ll Never Walk Alone”.

This time for his 100th appearance for the club? “Stand By Me”.

Self-consciously holding his arms and flanked by long-time team-mate Cam Murray, Arrow took hugs from his colleagues, absorbed the spectacle and finally offered a thumbs up to all four sides that might be the most understated gesture the sport has ever seen.

The 31-year-old was marking his 100th appearance for the National Rugby League (NRL) side in Sydney against their arch rivals, Sydney Roosters.

Arrow was named Player of the Season in 2025 at a club famously supported and part-owned by actor Russell Crowe. Becoming the club’s 67th player to reach a century felt inevitable.

Yet, in May, Arrow announced his immediate retirement from the sport following a diagnosis of motor neurone disease (MND).

Three months later, club officials received special dispensation from the NRL for the second-row — sat on 98 appearances — to suit up and start their final two matches of this season, before being subbed off after the opening whistle in a pre-approved change.

Appearance 99 came in the Rabbitohs’ 42-22 win at Gold Coast Titans last weekend. On Friday night, the century arrived in front of a near sell-out 45,000 crowd.

“I wouldn’t say my relationship with rugby has changed much at all,” Arrow told The Athletic. “This game has given me so much and I am so appreciative of what it’s done for me. It’s all I’ve ever wanted to do and I got to live out my dream for 10 years.

“All that’s changed is it feels like I’m in a forever rehab and I understand that I won’t ever play again. I’ve come to terms with my diagnosis — it is what it is. I do not fear what is meant to come down the track. I’ll fight till the death.”

Arrow is a household name across Australia, winning 12 caps for Queensland in the State of Origin representative series against New South Wales that often brings the country to a standstill every year.

It helps that he has played a sport beloved by the country and, since his diagnosis, there have been a handful of events to celebrate his career and raise awareness and support for those affected by MND.

Also known as ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis), MND is a progressive neurological condition that damages the nerves in the brain and spinal cord, which have the job of telling your muscles what to do.

Difficulty with speech is among the early symptoms, and a reason why Arrow and the Rabbitohs asked The Athletic to conduct a written interview.

MND Australia states that while life expectancy following an MND diagnosis varies significantly, it averages two to three years. There is currently no cure.

Game 99 at Gold Coast provided a good rehearsal for Arrow’s big night in Sydney, but brought humour through the tears.

Rabbitohs coach Wayne Bennett, a legendary figure in the game and not known to smile often, was among the staff drafted in to help dress Arrow for the occasion — much to Arrow’s own amusement.

Then, as the No 23 walked out to rapturous applause at the Titans’ Cbus Super Stadium, tears welling in his eyes, it was team-mate Cody Walker this time escorting Arrow and making sure he lightened the mood with a word in his ear.

“I said to Cody before the game, ‘I’m gonna need you to spray me and abuse me to help’, and he did just that. It was along the lines of, ‘Don’t you f***ing cry, you dope’,” said Arrow.

“Wayne did help (with the socks) and I saw the funny side to it. I was so appreciative of what he and (club stalwart) Jason Clark did. It’s weird to think I’m a grown man and I need people to help put my socks on.

“This club and its people mean so much to me, and I’m forever in debt to them. Wayne has been unreal throughout. He’s allowed me to do what I need mentally and physically, and the club has been so understanding. They’ve allowed me to live a normal life and it’s the only place I can go and feel a sense of normality.

“It was definitely a different feeling (walking out). I was unsure how the crowd was going to be. I was unsure on how I was going to be. But it genuinely was so special, it was hard to describe. What stuck out was how loud they were. I was genuinely in shock. I just couldn’t believe the amount of support. It really will be a memory I’ll have for the rest of my life and I’m truly grateful.”

There is little hiding from rugby’s complicated relationships that have needed to weave around MND.

Arrow calls the NRL’s support “incredible” to allow him to reach 100 appearances, believing it was a joke when the idea was first presented to him by Bennett.

The support of his team-mates in seeing one of their own — and in his prime — having to deal with MND so swiftly, alongside his partner Berina and one-year-old daughter Ayla Rae, has struck him.

“It’s definitely affected everyone in different ways but, from the start, I said to my team-mates I don’t need sadness,” said Arrow. “I don’t need sympathy. I just need your support and for you to treat me no different to how it was before, and let me tell you it has not changed.

“They take the absolute living p*** out of me but I love that. Do my team-mates have moments? Probably. Do I have moments? Yes. But this disease will not stop me from enjoying every moment with them because you spend so much time together, they become your best mates.”

With his playing career celebrated in Sydney, more of the focus will shift to a life living with MND.

Arrow’s path has been trodden by others across both rugby codes. In union, from Scotland and British Lion Doddie Weir, to former Leeds Rhinos rugby league star Rob Burrow, whose diagnosis inspired former team-mate Kevin Sinfield to begin an awareness campaign that has since raised more than £11million ($14.9m) towards MND research and support.

Sinfield will tackle 7 in 7: seven ultra-marathons in seven days this month.

“I have seen what Kev has done and I’ve been lucky enough to chat to him,” said Arrow. “I’m really looking forward to seeing him when he’s in Australia. What he’s doing is incredible and not many people have the balls to do it. The MND community and myself are in awe of Kevin.

“I’ll be honest, it’s been hard with this disease chipping away at me every single day. I can feel it. But everyone has made my journey so far so much easier.

“It has been a wild ride but I would not change anything. I’ll continue to fight for everyone fighting this disease. They are not alone, I have their back as they have mine. We will find a cure, I am sure of it. This is only the start of my journey and I’m ready.

“I had no expectations of what my career was going to be (at the start) but I am proud of what I have done. I hope I’ve made my family and friends proud.”

Before the Rabbitohs beat the Roosters 50-20, Arrow certainly did that.
