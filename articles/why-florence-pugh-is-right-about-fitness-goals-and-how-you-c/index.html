From all the evidence I’ve seen, the average person’s relationship with exercise and body image in general is not in a great place right now. A statement from actor Florence Pugh this week, unfortunately, reinforces this belief.

She shares her experience of people “picking her body apart”; first fat-shaming from those in the film industry, then social media users pointing to a recent public appearance and accusing her of using Ozempic.

What upsets me most about the latter allegations is that they arrive after Pugh made changes in her life that left her feeling happier and healthier.

“Here’s the truth: I turned 30 and my New Year’s resolution was to change my relationship with alcohol and be able to do at least one pull-up in the gym,” she writes.

“It’s funny to see you all use reference images from times where I was really mentally unhappy and abusing my body, and put it next to an image from the Governors Awards the other night where I am happy and truly respecting my body.”

The impulse of many to tear others down based on their appearance will forever baffle me. What Pugh describes above is what I believe fitness should be: building a body you enjoy living in, and finding ways to feel happy and healthy within yourself. Not changing the way you look. Not becoming smaller.

“[Recently] I’ve wanted to move my body and not take it for granted,” says Pugh. “The thing that’s most important to me and my trainer is muscle. I think it’s the most important thing a woman can do: build muscle and be strong. I can now do six pull-ups and lift 110kg, which I’m sure to many of you isn’t a lot. But to me?! It’s everything.”

I love this fitness goal: becoming stronger. It’s fun, and a cascade of mental and physical health perks tends to come with it. Here's how to achieve it.

How to become stronger, whatever your fitness level

Physical strength is an adaptation from exercise. This adaptation is your body’s way of preparing to face the same physical challenge again. That’s why, if you lift weights often, your muscles become bigger and stronger; when you run regularly, your heart and lungs become more efficient.

Your body, brilliant as it is, is trying to make you better at the things you regularly do. So, to build strength and muscle, you need to challenge your muscles on a regular basis. Our feature on how to start strength training should have all the information you need to do this, starting with just two 15-minute workouts per week.

The gym is the obvious place to put this advice into action. But to newcomers, it can be a foreign and intimidating environment – I still remember stepping into my first one as an awkward, gangly teenager and immediately feeling out of place.

And finally, the body can’t do you any favours if you don’t nourish it. To build muscle, it needs nutritious food (plenty of it) and ample rest. After all, exercise triggers the adaptations you’re after, but your body only becomes stronger when it is given the opportunity to recover and rebuild.

Protein is important, providing the building blocks your body needs to forge stronger tissues such as muscles, bones, ligaments and tendons. This can come from meat, fish, eggs, dairy or plant-based sources such as nuts, beans and legumes. Nutritionists I’ve spoken to tend to agree that a daily protein target of 1.6g or more per kilogramme of lean body weight is a good figure to aim for if strength gains are your goal.

An ample, balanced diet is needed to support increasing strength levels too. If you don’t consume enough calories, all of your energy will be used up simply keeping you alive, with nothing left over for building muscle. A similar concept applies to what you eat – you need to give your body all the nutrients it needs to operate at its best if you want it to become stronger.

For this, I like fermentation expert Dr Johnny Drain’s advice: eat your CAP, or a colourful array of plant-based foods. This is likely to deliver good gut health and enough nourishment to leave you (quite literally) full of beans.

And finally, sleep is key – this is when the muscle magic happens. I understand that regular restful nights can be a pipe dream for many, myself included, but these five tips from sleep expert Dr Sophie Bostock helped me get the most out of my nightly slumber and send my energy levels skyward.

So there we have it, that’s everything you need to know to start building strength and muscle. Like an all-you-can-eat buffet, the aim here is not for you to use every insight on offer, but rather you can take what you want and leave what you don’t – I simply hope that some of it is useful.

However, perhaps ironically, my key piece of advice with exercise is not to overcomplicate it. As Pugh writes: move your body, don’t take it for granted, and enjoy yourself.
