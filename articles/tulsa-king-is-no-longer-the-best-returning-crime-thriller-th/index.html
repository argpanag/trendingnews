‘Tulsa King’ Is No Longer the Best Returning Crime Thriller Thanks to a 99% RT Sleeper Hit

TheTools · 13.11.2026 15:00 · source · permalink

Tulsa King has been excellent since it returned for Season 4, trading some of its usual breezy comedy for sharper stakes, grittier violence, and a more emotionally grounded Dwight Manfredi. The season’s mix of mob intrigue, dark humor, and unexpected heart makes it one of the series’ strongest runs yet. But if you want a richer, more unpredictable Tulsa-set crime thriller, make time for The Lowdown.

Created by Sterlin Harjo (Reservation Dogs), The Lowdown premiered on Sept. 23, 2025, and returned for a second season on Oct. 14, 2026. The show’s first season has a 98% score on Rotten Tomatoes, with the second coming in even stronger at a flawless 100%. Led by a strong cast that includes Ethan Hawke, Keith David, Peter Dinklage, and Tommy Lee Jones, the show is must-see TV, especially if you want a series that is also set in Oklahoma but offers a lot more.

Ethan Hawke Takes the Crown in 'Tulsa King' With an Excellent Performance

There is a particular pleasure in watching a show that understands its own scale. The Lowdown never pretends to be a sweeping gangster epic or a tightly wound detective-style crime thriller. Instead, Sterlin Harjo’s FX series, starring Ethan Hawke as Tulsa “truthstorian” Lee Raybon, settles happily into the messy middle distance. A used bookstore doubles as a newsroom. Other memorable elements include a broken van, family obligations that keep interrupting the investigation, and the city itself, which is both a character and an accomplice.

Hawke, in one of his most free-spirited and appealing performances, plays Lee as a man who has decided that caring is a full-time job and is willing to look ridiculous in the process. He is a hangdog without self-pity, righteous without sanctimony, and funny in the way people who have been knocked down more than once tend to be.

The neo-noir crime thriller lets him be both the investigator and the schlub, often in the same scene. When he stumbles into trouble — and he always does — the humor comes not from slapstick but from the recognition that most of us would handle a conspiracy with roughly the same combination of stubbornness and bad ideas.

The ensemble around Hawke — Keith David’s weary private investigator and the recurring players who populate the bookstore and the margins — feels less like a supporting cast and more like neighbors who have known one another long enough to skip the introductions. The mysteries (a suspicious death in Season 1, a missing prisoner and an estranged father in Season 2) provide momentum, but they never overwhelm the texture of the story.

The Lowdown is more interested in how people talk to each other while the world quietly rots than in the mechanics of the rot itself. In an era that often confuses volume with importance, it tells a quieter story. It's about how a well-observed community, a magnetic lead, and the willingness to let scenes breathe can still produce something that feels both timely and timeless. It's excellent not because it solves the big problems, but because it notices the small ones with such clarity and warmth.

'The Lowdown' Explores the Tulsa Setting With More Care and Affection

Although these two TV shows are set in the same location, they couldn't be more different. Taylor Sheridan’s Tulsa King treats Tulsa, Oklahoma, as a convenient stage for a fish-out-of-water mob story. Sylvester Stallone’s Dwight Manfredi arrives from New York, builds a crew, and imposes his old-world beliefs on a landscape that mostly reacts to him.

It's entertaining in the way a well-made popcorn movie is entertaining — propulsive, star-driven, and clear about its pleasures. But the city remains a backdrop, and the people around Dwight often function as foils or sidekicks rather than fully inhabited lives.

The Lowdown, by contrast, starts from the ground up. Sterlin Harjo is an Oklahoma native, and his series knows Tulsa the way someone knows a neighborhood they have walked around for years. It paints everything perfectly, from the location to the overlapping Indigenous, Black, and white communities who share the same streets and the same history.

Our verdict

Watch this if

  • You like rambling, darkly funny neo-noir in the vein of The Big Lebowski
  • You love Tulsa King or are invested in the state of Oklahoma

Skip this if

  • You want a tight, plot-driven mystery.
  • You have no patience for a protagonist who annoys you on purpose.

Will you watch this?

Tulsa King’s flaws are absent in The Lowdown. Where the former leans on Stallone’s undeniable charisma and a familiar genre engine, the latter trusts something that is harder to manufacture: a genuine sense of place, a willingness to let scenes meander into human messiness, and a comic tone that never undercuts the stakes. It is thought-provoking, sadder, and more specific.

One show asks what happens when a mobster claims a town. The other asks what it costs to actually live there and still care. That second question is the more interesting one, and The Lowdown answers it with a warmth, grit, and familiarity that Tulsa King rarely approaches.
