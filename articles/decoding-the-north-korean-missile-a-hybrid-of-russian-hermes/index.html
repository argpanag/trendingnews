Decoding the North Korean missile: a hybrid of Russian Hermes and US GLSDB.

TheTools · 20.10.2026 04:13 · source · permalink

North Korea modified the Russian Hermes system into a 22-tube missile system combined with the US GLSDB-style glide bomb, with a range of 100km, while Russia had not yet completed the original version. Let's explore this unique hybrid technology.

While Russia has yet to fully deploy its Hermes project after nearly three decades of development, North Korea has publicly tested and displayed a tactical missile system with many similar but significantly improved features, combining booster rockets with glide bombs in a direction closer to the US GLSDB concept.

North Korea's system appeared in public tests in May 2026 and was displayed at a defense museum in October of the same year.

It is described as a 'Tactical Launcher for Cruise Missiles' with the capability to launch guided missiles with a range of approximately 100km.

In terms of external appearance, the launcher and missile bear many similarities to the Russian Hermes project, developed by the KBP Design Bureau since the 1990s.

The original Hermes was designed as a two-stage modular guided missile system, using 130 or 207mm diameter boosters, with a maximum range of approximately 100km, a warhead of about 28kg, inertial or radio-command guidance in the mid-stage and semi-active laser or radar in the terminal stage, with a "fire and forget" capability.

The Russian launcher typically carries six launch tubes. However, the project has been largely delayed, with the last updates only appearing around 2023 and it has yet to be deployed on a large scale.

The North Korean version differs in several key aspects. The launcher has been expanded to 22 launch tubes, significantly increasing its salvo firing capability.

The missile did not retain the original Hermes configuration but switched to a hybrid design: a rocket propulsion stage combined with a glide bomb as a second stage.

After the booster separates, the glide bomb continues to fly towards the target thanks to its wings and guidance system, which extends its effective range and increases trajectory flexibility.

Analysts directly compare this concept to the American Ground-Launched Small Diameter Bomb (GLSDB), a long-range precision strike weapon jointly developed by Boeing (USA) and Saab (Sweden), which combines rocket engines from the M26 projectile with the GBU-39 guided bomb for ground launch, achieving a longer range than bombs dropped from conventional aircraft.

The statement from North Korea also mentioned the possibility of AI-assisted navigation, although the technical details have not been independently verified.

The operating principle is based on two distinct stages. In the first stage, the booster propels the projectile into orbit at high speed. After separation, the glide bomb uses a guidance system (which may combine inertial guidance, satellite, and a final seeker) to correct its flight path and attack a fixed or moving target.

The advantage of this design is that it leverages the accuracy of modern guided bombs while still launching from mobile ground launchers, reducing reliance on aircraft.

A range of approximately 100km allows for attacks on tactical targets without penetrating deep into enemy air defenses.

The fact that North Korea put this system to the test and displayed it while the original Russian Hermes was still incomplete reflects its ability to quickly adopt and improve technology.

Some analyses question whether Pyongyang received the technical documentation directly from Russia or simply copied the form and then further developed it on its own.

Although its exact origins are unconfirmed, the combination of the Hermes architecture and the US GLSDB-style glide bomb concept suggests a pragmatic approach: leveraging existing designs, increasing the number of launch tubes to enhance firepower, and adding glide capabilities to improve accuracy and survivability against defensive defenses.

This system adds to North Korea's tactical arsenal, alongside variants of the KN-23 missile and the domestically produced HIMARS-type system, forming a versatile short- to medium-range strike class.

Current information is primarily based on publicly available images from KCNA and OSINT analysis from international sources. Detailed specifications regarding the warhead, actual accuracy, or anti-jamming capabilities have not yet been fully released.

Nevertheless, the emergence of this system clearly illustrates North Korea's tendency not only to copy but also to adapt foreign technology to its liking.

(According to KCNA, united24media.com, en.defence-ua.com, fr.businessam.be, businessam.be)
