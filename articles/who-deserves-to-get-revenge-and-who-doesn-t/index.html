Who deserves to get revenge and who doesn’t?

TheTools · 08.10.2026 14:35 · source · permalink

Spoiler Warning: The Last of Us Part II; “Kill Bill: The Whole Bloody Affair.”

We’ve all heard it a million times by now: Before you embark on a journey of revenge, dig two graves. Or was it that revenge is a dish best served cold?

The consequences of pursuing revenge have been a prevalent source of drama in fiction almost as long as fiction itself has existed; it’s a facet of William Shakespeare’s works that still sparks analysis hundreds of years later. Whether or not revenge is worth the catharsis, however, is a question that has continued to yield wildly contrasting answers. Divisive debates spanning all forms of narrative media aside, audiences still come together to consume revenge stories big and small.

Kill Bill and The Last of Us Part II both revolve around revenge, starring women seeking to avenge the death of a loved one. As evidenced by “Kill Bill: The Whole Bloody Affair” (a belated re-release combining both Kill Bill films) and HBO’s “The Last of Us” (a largely faithful TV adaptation of the games), these stories’ pop cultural relevance haven’t shown any signs of waning in spite of their thematic takeaways being diametrically opposed to one another. From the way their protagonists are written to how violence is depicted on screen, one can trace how the philosophies of their creators differed.

In Kill Bill, Beatrix Kiddo (Uma Thurman) is a former assassin whose wedding rehearsal is interrupted by her scorned ex-lover Bill, who murders her fiance and everyone else in attendance. In The Last of Us Part II, Ellie seeks vengeance for the retaliatory killing of Joel, her surrogate father figure and one of the only people in the world she loved — despite their fraught relationship. Joel himself was killed in a revenge plot, which was put into motion after he killed another girl’s — Abby’s — father. Beatrix and Ellie’s reasons for beginning their quests are almost identical, but the violence they commit provokes disparate emotional responses. The hyper-stylized sword action of Kill Bill invites laughter and glee, while The Last of Us Part II features arguably some of the most horrifying violence in any screen media. The game treats players to horrifying scenes of its antagonists screaming their comrades’ names as they are executed and dismembered in spectacularly disturbing fashion with an array of weaponry including firearms, blades and improvised explosives.

The audience reactions to the most violent parts of these stories are accurate microcosms of the protagonists’ overall arcs. Beatrix’s path is one of inevitable catharsis, a debt that must be paid resulting from Bill’s heartlessness. Ellie’s, on the other hand, is one that provokes increasing levels of disgust and shame as she continues her path of destruction. The most obvious reason for this would be the difference in how Beatrix and Ellie’s foes are written: Those slain by Beatrix are cartoonish and often stupid, and the knowledge that her targets are assassins themselves seems to position them as fair game; The Last of Us Part II, however, opts to humanize all of its characters without exception, reminding the player that they all have relationships that are being torn apart by Ellie’s killing spree. Joel’s killer, Abby, is perhaps the most human character in the story. It’s even argued by some that her justification for revenge is more legitimate than Ellie’s.

However, even prior to Ellie’s most heinous acts or the player learning who Abby and her friends are, Ellie’s unhealthy thinking is what raise alarms: A once quippy and relatable character begins a highly dangerous odyssey across state lines in a post-apocalyptic America, convinced that revenge at any cost — including endangering the lives of her loved ones — is the key to unlocking her emotional peace. Abby’s reasons for what she both did become wholly irrelevant to Ellie as her singular goal consumes her entire life. At a certain point, who the victims of her quest are stops being a question, what’s more interesting is who the perpetrator has become along the way. When there’s a thriving, bustling community waiting for Ellie back in Jackson, Montana, it’s disturbing to think that crossing the country in order to find and kill someone in Seattle, Oregon is her first and only priority.

By comparison, Beatrix is a fairly static character: There’s no complex mental block stopping her from considering the consequences of her actions. From the outset, she is remarkably aware of the implications of her role in a revenge story. After Beatrix kills Vernita Green (Vivica A. Fox), the writing borders on self-referential in her address to her now-orphaned daughter: “When you grow up, if you still feel raw about it, I’ll be waiting.” Beatrix’s mission becomes messy almost as soon as it begins, but her lack of hypocrisy about what she’s done makes things feel ironically clean.

Equally crucial is the life (or lack thereof) that Beatrix is leaving behind: Unlike Ellie, who had plenty of reasons to work through her trauma in Jackson with the rest of her community, Beatrix was robbed of any sense of normalcy she was accustomed to (and any potential for rediscovering it in the future) the day of Bill’s massacre. It becomes difficult to imagine any other path for her when we feel that she’s starting from zero, leaving her with nothing to do but seek retribution (see also: John Wick). We take such details for granted as viewers eager to see where the story will go, but it’s shocking how much audience sympathy for Beatrix is generated by her blunt self-awareness and backstory as a killer.

While the concept of revenge may have no broadly agreed-upon answer, it’s clear that it remains fertile ground for exploring the nuances of human psychology. The topic of whether a target of revenge deserves what they’re getting may have become somewhat stale, but the way audiences are manipulated to identify with violent, antisocial behavior through subtle artistic choices shows that there’s still room for innovation.
