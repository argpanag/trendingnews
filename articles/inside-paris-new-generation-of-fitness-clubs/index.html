Inside Paris’ New Generation of Fitness Clubs

TheTools · 28.09.2026 15:54 · source · permalink

More than places to do Pilates, Ken Club, The Method and Rune are taking cues from hospitality to rethink fitness around the social scene.

Paris’ workout rooms are being revamped and rethought, transforming into something closer to social clubs.

Ken Club, the grande dame of the city’s fitness scene, opened in 1985 as one of Paris’ original workout spaces, when sports were still a bit of a novelty in France. It sat above the Warner Music Group offices, leading to stars such as Madonna passing through.

While its guest list led it to become more than a just an aerobics and swim spot, current owners Masada Group have spent four years and 20 million euros to rethink the club for a new generation that places sports, socializing and beautiful spaces on equal footing.

Interior architects Rodolphe Parente and Marion Mailaender updated the Ken Club’s 1980s cues, with the ambition to bring a five-star travel experience to the local-first clientele, to bring the sense of escape into everyday neighborhood life.

“We wanted to build an experience, because when we go any place in the world, there is poetry,” said Parente. “It always exists for travelers, so we wanted to try to create that for local people. It’s the best gift we can do.”

The idea that a fitness club should feel closer to hospitality than a conventional gym is also emerging at newer Paris concepts.

At The Method, a premium fitness and recovery brand with two Paris locations, the workout is only one part of the offering. Across town, Rune Sports Club, a new venue in the 11th arrondissement founded by husband-and-wife team Martin Bachelot and Manon Lorenzetti, coffee, work, drinks and socializing are built into the business model.

While the spaces all take a different approach to design, the philosophy reflects the new Paris social status. People are not just joining for access to exercise, but for somewhere they want to spend time.

At Ken Club, Mailaender approached the 1980s without trying to recreate them. “We wanted something very joyful, very playful,” she said. The team mixed materials such as steel, splashes of color and turned the logo into a clever carpet print.

“My mantra is that fun follows function,” she said.

The pair leaned on the bones of the building and its famous glass roof, she said, and used raw materials associated with the period — linoleum, bold colors, steel — then brought in the nautical references to vintage luxury liners, such as porthole windows.

The challenge was that gyms are fundamentally difficult spaces to decorate. Unlike a hotel or restaurant, they require large, mostly empty rooms.

“What is very difficult in the sports club is that we have only empty spaces,” Parente said. “You don’t have any furniture inside to make the emotion.”

Mailaender used different types of mirrors, geometric wall patterns, colored cork and linoleum, wavy steel and deliberately 1980s-style locker-room doors. The swimming pool retains its original form but has been given what she described as a “space-age” treatment, with resin textures and large windows.

The programming is contemporary even when the references are retro. Cycling, yoga, Pilates and recovery facilities sit alongside the more traditional gym spaces. “We mix low tech and high tech,” Mailaender said.

“We don’t want to make only functional stuff,” said Parente. “Life is short. We are crazy about art and we wanted to create an experience.”

The club’s collection includes a photography works from Jean-Daniel Lorieux, a sculptural armchair by Gaetano Pesce and a dog-shaped bench inspired by Keith Haring.

Also on the way is a textile fresco made from ship sail offcuts by the French design duo Jeanne Martin-Taton and Marie-Marie Vergne, better known for their work with Hermès and India Mahdavi as The Crafties. That piece will soon decorate the restaurant helmed by Michelin-starred chef Assaf Granit, which is slated to open this fall, and add another element of immersion to the space.

For Parente, however, the design is more about what happens between the members.

“We want a mix,” he said. “We want doctors. We want artists. We want people who don’t work and spend all day at the club. We want people who work [remotely] — they make it their gym and after they stay at the club to work all day long.”

The gym, he said, can be a successful social space because exercise strips away status markers.

“There is no better place than sports to make links between different people. You put two people on a bike, they’re the same. Even if one just made an IPO and the other one made a painting, they are the same. They speak the same language.”

And then there is sweat equity as the great equalizer.

“In the sauna, when they are naked after the gym, everyone is the same,” he joked.

The Method builds on the social aspect and integrates it into the daily routine. Its Trocadéro location combines two workout studios with contrast therapy and a specialty coffee shop.

It’s a “premium fitness and lifestyle destination,” said cofounder Alissa Ananieva, built around the idea that exercise and wellness should be pleasurable rather than punishing.

“The Method clubs and studios are de facto one venue, which offers high fitness and high recovery as an integrated experience,” she said. The aim is “to offer your space where you can build your own practice, so that it is not only a venue that you visit; it’s something that becomes part of your life.”

That means staying after class. At the smaller Opéra studio, opened last year, Ananieva noticed people lingering even though there was no space, and making plans to meet elsewhere.

“Some of the classes are so, so much of an emotional release, people do tend to bond after class,” she said. “We realized that a lot of people were actually hanging out after class, making plans for a coffee, but they were never doing it at the studio.”

The café at Trocadéro is designed to turn those connections into part of the experience.

“Fitness is what allows you to build frequency,” she said. “But then the café, the area where you can sit, is really what allows you to build community.”

And it’s not all kale smoothies either; Ananieva noted that the Paris locations serve croissants after workouts.

“We do talk a lot about detox and retox,” she said. “It really is what is driving us.”

The point, she said, is pleasure. “We really do believe that it is about that. It’s about finding that balance. So it’s not because you eat cheese or you go out for a glass of wine that you can’t come to a class, or vice versa.”

Art is also integrated to The Method spaces, with a piece from Tracy Emin at the Opéra location. At Trocadéro, there’s a custom mirror from Sabine Marcelis and the space will soon install a piece from Berlin-based experiential sculptor Jeppe Hein.

Recovery is also part of the offering, with the contrast therapy, with Nordic saunas and cold baths; the company plans to add IV drips and hyperbaric chambers at its upcoming New York location.

Across town, Rune Sports Club takes the club idea into a younger, more urban direction.

Founded by Bachelot, formerly an operations manager at skin care brand Horace, and Lorenzetti, who previously worked for her family’s winery, Rune occupies the former Max & Co. brand headquarters and showroom in the 11th arrondissement. The couple spent around a year and a half finding the space and almost another year renovating it as a “refuge,” with interior design direction from Das Studios.

They wanted something deliberately unlike the dark, basement-like gyms built out of necessity.

“In Paris, often we have to do sport in black, in the dark,” Lorenzetti said, simply due to space restrictions. “You don’t have a place where you want to stay after or to come and find some friends and meet people.”

At Rune, light is one of the central architectural features. A system developed by French start-up Olumi tracks the natural light outside and reproduces its changing intensity inside. During the day, the light shifts with the weather; at night, it softens to a moony glow.

“We wanted to stay in the same type of light all day long,” Bachelot said.

The central café is designed to make people pass through the social heart of the club. It is open throughout the day for coffee and food, with longer opening hours planned. In the evenings, the music gets louder on custom Matteo Garcia speakers — the same sound designer that Celine and Kenzo use for their fashion shows.

Thursdays and Fridays are after-work nights, and members can have a matcha, complete with collagen or creatine shots — or a beer or glass of wine.

“We wanted to show that we are not only a sports club; we are also a place where you can stay, work, and have a good time,” Bachelot said. It’s first afterwork happy hour saw 150 of its now 600-plus members turn up. Membership will be capped at 1000 to keep the community close knit.

The club also runs regular competitions and Hyrox training events that are open to nonmembers, giving people another reason to come through the door.

The founders say the willingness to pay a premium for this kind of place has changed along with the way Parisians think about fitness as more than functional.

“People now are OK with the fact that they will pay more to get a class to get to be in a place where they want to stay,” Bachelot said.

Good coffee, recovery facilities, saunas, ice baths and luxury beauty products available in the changing area, alongside pro-athlete instructors and more specialized programming, are all among members’ expectations.

“People want more wellness in this globality of sport,” Lorenzetti said.

Bachelot sees a generational change behind it. He points to the growing popularity of strength training, supplements and longevity-focused fitness among younger Parisians who watch popular fitness YouTubers, compared with the beer-after-rugby culture he grew up with.

“It’s a completely different way of living sport,” he said.
