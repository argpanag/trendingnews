Trump Pardons Pete Rose as Garcia Calls Biden’s Pardons a ‘Mistake’ - Newsweek

TheTools · 20.11.2026 02:11 · source · permalink

Associate News Editor

President Donald Trump has granted a pardon to the late baseball star Pete Rose, he announced on Truth Social on Thursday, the same day Representative Robert Garcia called former President Joe Biden's grants a mistake.

Rose, baseball’s career hits leader who died in 2024, pleaded guilty in 1990 to filing false tax returns and served five months in prison. Major League Baseball banned him in 1989 after an investigation found he had bet on Cincinnati Reds games from 1985 to 1987. He denied betting on baseball for years before admitting it in 2004. In May 2025, Major League Baseball Commissioner Rob Manfred removed Rose and other deceased players from the league’s permanently ineligible list, making him eligible for the Baseball Hall of Fame.

Trump first pledged the pardon in a February 2025 Truth Social post, saying he would sign it “over the next few weeks.” He renewed the promise this month at a White House briefing, saying Rose had been “very mistreated.” Trump's Thursday post said: "I have just given a Complete and Total Pardon to the Late, Great Pete Rose, a true Baseball SUPERSTAR & LEGEND."

The Rose pardon landed as Democrats preparing to take over the House signal they will put Trump’s use of pardons under scrutiny.

Garcia, the California Democrat poised to chair the House Oversight Committee when his party takes control of the lower chamber of Congress in January, said that Biden was wrong to issue his sweeping end-of-term pardons. The admission is a break with his party's last president, and it comes as Trump openly weighs preemptive clemency for the family members, aides and allies Garcia intends to investigate.

"Personally, I think that was a mistake," Garcia told anchor Kate Bolduan on CNN News Central, after she asked how Democrats would answer an expected wave of Trump pardons when Biden had already set the precedent. Presidents "should not be pardoning, first and foremost, their family members," he said. "They shouldn't be putting out these mass pardons."

Why It Matters

For weeks, Trump has defended the idea of preemptive pardons by pointing to Biden. Garcia's remark is a notable break from the former president by the Democrat who is in line to hold the House's chief investigative gavel, and it arrives as both major parties brace for a confrontation over how far the pardon power can reach.

Garcia also argued that it has limits.

"Blanket pardons can also be challenged," he said, adding that state attorneys general are running investigations of their own.

Presidential pardons cover only federal crimes, not state prosecutions, civil suits or impeachment.

What to Know

On Wednesday, Garcia released document preservation letters to 13 people in Trump's orbit. The recipients included Vice President JD Vance, White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller, Attorney General Todd Blanche, Trump's sons Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump, Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner and former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem. The letters lay the groundwork for four investigations: Trump family profiteering, corporate corruption, misconduct at the Department of Homeland Security and the administration's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files.

The White House answered with a shrug. In a letter sent Wednesday to Democratic ranking members, White House Counsel Will Scharf argued that lawmakers in the minority have no authority to conduct oversight without a delegation from a full committee, and that their requests will not carry over into the new Congress.

The demands, he wrote in a passage read on CNN, "all drip with an animus or pettiness." In a letter dated November 9, Scharf warned Garcia that efforts to investigate the administration "may cross the line into blatant criminality," a claim legal experts broadly rejected.

Garcia told CNN that the administration "cannot continue to ignore our requests." He said he is working with Representative Jamie Raskin of Maryland, the top Democrat on the House Judiciary Committee, on pardon reform.

Biden's Pardons

Biden's pardons came in three waves. On December 1, 2024, he pardoned his son, Hunter, who was awaiting sentencing on federal gun and tax charges after months of assurances that he would not. The pardon reached back to offenses from 2014. In a statement, Biden wrote that Hunter had been "singled out only because he is my son – and that is wrong."

On December 12, he commuted the sentences of roughly 1,500 people and pardoned 39 others, which the Associated Press called the largest single-day act of clemency in modern history.

Then, on his final morning in office, January 20, 2025, Biden preemptively pardoned Dr. Anthony Fauci, retired General Mark Milley, members and staff of the House January 6 committee, and police officers who testified before it.

"These are exceptional circumstances, and I cannot in good conscience do nothing," he said.

Minutes before Trump took the oath of office, the White House announced pardons for Biden's siblings and their spouses.

Garcia is not the first Democrat to object. At the time, Representative Jason Crow of Colorado called the Hunter Biden pardon "a mistake," and Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut told NOTUS that Biden had "dangerously expanded the use of pardons."

Trump has gone further. In March 2025, he declared on Truth Social that the pardons were "VOID, VACANT, AND OF NO FURTHER FORCE OR EFFECT" because they had been signed by autopen. In December 2025, he announced on Truth Social that any such document had been "fully and completely terminated." In October 2025, the Republican-led Oversight Committee deemed void Biden's actions signed by autopen without documented approval. Garcia, then the panel's ranking member, called the report a "sham investigation," saying testimony showed that Biden "authorized every executive order, pardon and use of the autopen."

Legal scholars say no mechanism exists to undo a pardon once granted.

"It's never been thought that a president has the ability to void a prior president's pardons," Mark Osler, a law professor at the University of St. Thomas, told Reuters. Garcia's criticism on Thursday was about Biden's judgment, not whether the pardons are valid.

Trump's Clemency Calculus

Trump has used the pardon power freely. On his first day back in office, he granted clemency to more than 1,500 people charged in connection with the January 6, 2021, riot at the U.S. Capitol. In April, The Wall Street Journal reported that he had repeatedly promised mass pardons to his staff, at one point telling aides that he would pardon "everyone who has come within 200 feet of the Oval."

Those private remarks became public pledges as this year's midterm elections approached. Asked on the New York Post's Pod Force One podcast whether he would pardon his staff, Trump said, "I would if I needed to, sure," adding, "I did it for J6." Pressed on Democratic talk of going after Elon Musk, he said, "It's talk, but if it is talk, we'll have to protect them." He also returned to his favorite precedent: "That's what Biden did. He gave everybody a pardon."

In October, the Washington Sun reported that White House attorneys were evaluating mass pardons for junior staffers. Musk said Wednesday that he does not need a pardon.

Democrats are already examining the pardons Trump has granted. In May, House and Senate Democrats opened an inquiry into whether some of his clemency grants reflected "pay-to-play dynamics." Lacking subpoena power at the time, they could only request cooperation, and CBS News reported that the issue would likely become a top oversight priority if Democrats won a majority in either chamber.

What Happens Next

Democrats take the majority in Congress in January. Garcia said the committee's opening investigations would see "a lot of activity" in January and February, and that action on DOGE (Department of Government Efficiency) is coming.

"These won't be our only four investigations," he said.

Trump's term runs until January 2029, which leaves him time to act on his pardon pledges whenever he chooses. Garcia argued that clemency will not end the inquiries.

"We have to have a real conversation about pardons," he said, "about pardon reform in this country and, more importantly, this idea that we're going to have these blanket pardons that can't stop our work."
