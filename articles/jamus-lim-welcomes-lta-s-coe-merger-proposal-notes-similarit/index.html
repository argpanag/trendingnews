Jamus Lim welcomes LTA’s COE merger proposal, notes similarities to his 2025 proposal rejected by Govt

TheTools · 30.10.2026 01:48 · source · permalink

WP MP Jamus Lim welcomed LTA’s proposed COE overhaul, noting its similarities to his earlier proposal for targeted discounts rejected by the Government in 2025. However, he warned that the changes would do little to address overall COE costs and unresolved issues involving Category E and private-hire vehicles.

  • Jamus Lim welcomed LTA’s proposed COE merger, noting that its rebate mechanism could indirectly achieve the targeted relief he proposed in 2025, despite the Government previously rejecting his approach.
  • Lim cautioned that the changes would do little to manage overall COE costs or resolve Category E issues, particularly regarding private-hire vehicles.
  • LTA is considering merging Categories A and B, introducing OMV-based rebates and surcharges, and reviewing Category E. Public feedback closes on 2 November 2026.

SINGAPORE: Workers’ Party (WP) Member of Parliament Associate Professor Jamus Lim has welcomed the Land Transport Authority’s (LTA) proposed overhaul of the Certificate of Entitlement (COE) system, noting that its approach bears similarities to his earlier proposal for targeted discounts, which the Government rejected in 2025.

However, the Sengkang GRC MP cautioned that the proposed changes would do little to address overall COE costs or unresolved questions surrounding Category E and private-hire vehicles.

In a Facebook post on 20 October 2026, Lim, who was admitted to hospital with pneumonia last week, reflected on LTA’s announcement two weeks earlier to review the existing distinction between Categories A and B.

The proposed framework would merge both categories and introduce rebates or surcharges based on a vehicle’s open market value (OMV), replacing the current classification based primarily on engine capacity and power output.

Lim said technological advances, particularly the widespread adoption of electric vehicles (EVs), had increasingly rendered engine capacity an outdated measure of a car’s relative value.

He noted that Category A and B COE prices had also converged over time, making the existing distinction less relevant.

"It seems like high time for a change to the system," he wrote.

Lim draws parallels with his 2025 COE 2.0 proposal

Lim highlighted similarities between LTA’s proposed framework and his earlier parliamentary proposal to introduce targeted COE discounts for households with greater transport needs.

During his 2025 adjournment motion, titled Toward Certificate of Entitlement 2.0, Lim proposed a framework offering targeted discounts to families with young children, caregivers supporting elderly parents and persons with disabilities.

He argued that equity should complement efficiency in Singapore’s car ownership system.

However, then-Acting Transport Minister Jeffrey Siow rejected the proposal, maintaining that the existing market-based COE system remained the fairest and most transparent approach, given Singapore’s land constraints, rising vehicle demand and the need to fund public transport.

Reflecting on that exchange, Lim explained that his proposal sought to extend relief to groups already recognised as requiring additional support under existing public policies.

"My thinking was that these groups—which we have already identified as in greater need of support through various public policies—will also benefit from the relief provided for by private car ownership," he wrote.

Lim says lower-OMV cars could ease costs for households needing private transport

Lim observed that LTA’s latest proposal would achieve something similar, although through a different mechanism.

He explained that his original proposal was based on what economists call third-degree price discrimination, where discounts are granted according to the characteristics of particular groups.

By contrast, the new framework adopts an approach resembling second-degree price discrimination, allowing buyers to receive discounts by selecting vehicles with lower OMVs.

Under the proposed system, buyers of more expensive vehicles would pay a premium, effectively subsidising those purchasing more affordable cars.

Households requiring private transport could therefore qualify for relief by choosing a more modest vehicle, without having to demonstrate eligibility based on their family circumstances.

Lim acknowledged that the Government had previously rejected his approach on the grounds that there was no fair way to determine which groups should receive preferential treatment.

He maintained his disagreement, arguing that existing public policies already provided ways to identify households requiring greater support.

Nevertheless, he observed that the new framework could indirectly deliver similar benefits.

"In effect, the new proposal will make cars cheaper for certain groups of needy buyers, as long as these groups self-select into the lower OMV category," he wrote.

Lim argued that while the two approaches differed in substance, the underlying spirit of the WP’s proposal remained.

Lim flags LTA’s new proposal does little to manage overall COE costs

Despite welcoming the proposed restructuring, Lim cautioned that it would do little to address the broader affordability issues he had raised in Parliament in November 2023.

During the WP’s Full Motion on the Cost of Living Crisis, he argued that fluctuations in COE prices were driven primarily by supply rather than demand.

He highlighted how vehicle quotas, tied to historical registration levels, could fluctuate by as much as 4,300 a month between the highest- and lowest-supply years, while Singapore’s car population growth rate had remained frozen since 2018.

His central recommendation was to transfer surplus quotas from high-supply years to low-supply years, ensuring a more consistent allocation across future periods.

He also proposed ending reimbursement of early-deregistered COEs at book value, prohibiting dealers from bidding directly, and requiring second and third cars to be purchased under the Open category.

At the time, Lim acknowledged that reclassifying vehicles according to market value would be reasonable and more progressive, but argued that such a change would make only "a small dent" in COE prices.

In his latest post, he reiterated that the proposed merger would not substantially address overall COE costs.

He also questioned the future treatment of Category E, particularly regarding quotas for private-hire vehicles, which he noted many believed had contributed to upward pressure on COE prices.

Lim said he intended to pursue these issues, alongside further questions about the proposed framework, during the next parliamentary sitting.

"But in the meantime, we can look forward to changes that should provide some additional relief to households that find themselves in need of a car, but who have often been shut out due to exorbitant prices," he wrote.

LTA seeks public feedback on merging Categories A and B

On 8 October 2026, LTA launched a public consultation on merging Categories A and B, with submissions closing on 2 November and recommendations expected in the first half of 2027.

The authority said advances in vehicle technology, particularly EVs, had weakened the effectiveness of existing engine-capacity and power-based classifications.

Under the proposed framework, each vehicle model would be assigned a percentile band based on its median OMV, determining whether buyers receive a rebate, face no adjustment or pay a surcharge.

Two options are being considered: a three-band structure with adjustments of up to S$15,000, or a five-band structure with S$7,500 increments. Both would produce a maximum difference of S$30,000 between the highest and lowest bands.

LTA estimated that, had the framework applied to the 52,678 cars registered in 2025, approximately half would have received either a rebate or no adjustment, while the remainder would have incurred surcharges.

The consultation also seeks feedback on Category E and other proposals, including family-based allocations, multiple-car surcharges, a separate private-hire category and pay-as-you-bid bidding.

LTA has approached these suggestions cautiously, instead proposing a transport grant for large families and measures encouraging individual bidders.

It maintained that the central challenge was supporting families while preserving a COE system that remains "market-based, equitable, and transparent", noting that allocating certificates according to household circumstances would require judgments about which needs deserve greater priority.
