Psychologist offers to adopt client, sends her 186 messages in one day

TheTools · 07.10.2026 14:31 · source · permalink

Subscribe to listen

A psychologist has been found guilty of serious misconduct after she developed a maternal relationship with a client. Photo / 123rf

A psychologist says she developed a maternal relationship with a client she was treating to fill a “big maternal gap” in her life.

She told her client she loved her and sent her 186 text messages in one day and disposed of the young woman’s methamphetamine and associated drug paraphernalia as the relationship developed.

It even got to the point where she discussed adopting her.

Now the Health Practitioners Disciplinary Tribunal has found the psychologist guilty of professional misconduct and associated malpractice and negligence that brought discredit to the profession.

Speaking through tears to the tribunal at a hearing today, the psychologist said she “felt shattered about what I did”.

“I am deeply sorry, especially to the complainant. I will never let this happen again.

“I desperately wanted to help her and I did the opposite.

“I didn’t want to lose her.”

The complainant and psychologist have interim name suppression.

The psychologist told the tribunal she found going to therapy herself had made her realise she was dealing with her own childhood trauma subconsciously through her client.

“There was a big maternal gap in my life and I didn’t realise.”

Psychologist developed ‘strong protective feelings’

According to the charge document, the woman provided psychological services to her client in a manner that distorted the therapeutic relationship while she was employed as a senior clinical psychologist between December 2022 and January 2024.

Early in the therapeutic relationship, the psychologist told her client she loved her and about March 2023 expressed strong protective feelings for her.

That same month, the psychologist discussed with her client the possibility of adopting her.

This included discussing such action with her family and sharing her family’s responses with the client.

The charging document states she eventually told her client she could not adopt her, primarily because others would not allow it.

Instead, she discussed the possibility of a personal, supportive maternal-like relationship after their therapeutic relationship ended.

Between January 2023 and January 2024, she sent up to 186 text messages a day to the client.

The messages contained expressions of emotion, affection and love, including saying she loved her and using terms of endearment such as “little one”.

She also sent about 155 photographs of her day-to-day life, including holiday places she was at, her cat and food she was eating.

In March 2023, she suggested her son and the client would be a “good match”.

On multiple occasions in therapy sessions, the psychologist gave her client hugs for five to 10 minutes and gave her gifts.

On one occasion, she disposed of methamphetamine and associated drug paraphernalia for her client.

The charge document also said she failed to take appropriate steps to assess and manage her client’s risk of harm to self, failed to disclose the nature of her relationship with her supervisor and made comments to her client that she knew could be perceived as manipulative attempts to dissuade her from making a complaint.

Conduct was ‘out of character’ – lawyer

The psychologist was suspended by the New Zealand Psychologists Board 20 months ago while her conduct was investigated.

Her lawyer Simon Waalkens told the tribunal “for over 25 years she had been a highly skilled member of the profession”.

“This conduct was out of character,” he said.

“[Her] being suspended for the past two years has been a loss to the profession.”

The Professional Conduct Committee (PCC) submitted suspension was an appropriate starting point, but Waalkens argued any more time suspended would be “quite unfair”.

“She has had the better part of two years to reflect and get the support she needs to assure this never happens again.”

He said censure and costs would be an appropriate penalty with conditions on her practising certificate.

“She deserves to be punished for her conduct but she’s been punished enough.”

Counsel for the PCC, Jo Hughson, said the client was a “vulnerable patient being harmed” and it was “serious offending”.

She said the tribunal needed to consider the “significant public safety concerns” the psychologist’s conduct posed.

On behalf of the complainant, Hughson said the client’s position, given the level of harm she had suffered, was that the psychologist should be suspended.

The psychologist sought permanent name suppression, which the complainant was strongly opposed to. The PCC also opposed permanent suppression.

Hughson said the PCC sought suspension as a starting point, but if the psychologist was fit to practise, it sought conditions on practice and censure.

The tribunal reserved its decision on penalties and name suppression and would publish its decision at a later date.
