F-16 crash details in Michigan: toxic exposure, possible bird strike

TheTools · 18.09.2026 18:17 · source · permalink

The pilot of the F-16 military fighter jet that crashed Sept. 17 south of Traverse City might have hit a bird before the dramatic descent that caused the evacuation of nearby homes and exposed people within a one-mile radius to toxic chemical fumes.

"We have a possible mayday," someone said on air traffic control audio recordings of the incident available on liveatc.net. "The wingman may have taken a bird."

Air traffic controllers then told the pilot about landing at an airport in either Frankfort or Traverse City. At the time, the jets were about 10 miles from Frankfort and about 25 miles from Traverse City, an official told him. However, the pilot reported the other jet was already on the ground and emergency services were needed. A person on the audio is later heard saying he was confident that the pilot in the downed craft had ejected but didn't see a parachute.

The F-16 Fighting Falcon was bound from the 149th Fighter Wing in Texas to the Air National Guard Combat Readiness Training Center in Alpena when it crashed in a field on the western edge of Blair Township. The Air Force is investigating the crash.

The 149th Fighter Wing, known as the Lone Star Gunfighters, is an F-16 training unit at Joint Base San Antonio in Lackland, Texas. The wing is a part of the Texas Air National Guard and Texas Military Department.

The jet was "on a routine training mission from the Air National Guard’s Combat Readiness Training Center in Alpena, when it experienced an incident earlier this afternoon," the Texas Military Department's Public Affairs Office said in a Sept. 17 statement.

Blair Township Supervisor Nicole Blonshine said the plane's pilot evacuated the aircraft via parachute before the crash. The pilot was found "conscious and alert," she said.

Officials said there were no reported injuries to civilians or damage to nearby homes.

On Sept. 18, the Texas Military Department's Public Affairs Office referred questions about the incident to the news release it issued the day before.

Concerns over nearby residents' and first responders' exposure to hydrazine, a highly volatile fuel used as an emergency power source aboard the planes, remained on Sept. 18, nearly a day after the crash. The Air Force describes hydrazine as highly toxic, flammable and corrosive.

Exposure can harm the nervous and respiratory systems and, in severe cases, can cause kidney damage or a coma, according to the federal Centers for Disease Control and Prevention..

The U.S. Air Force said an F-16 can also carry several other hazardous substances, such as jet fuel, hydraulic fluid and ethylene glycol.

First responders hospitalized for monitoring

The Grand Traverse Sheriff's Department said several first responders were evaluated because of the exposure to hazardous chemicals, and some were hospitalized overnight for monitoring. One of the emergency responders kept overnight was released the next day.

"Munson Healthcare reminds residents that individuals within the one mile radius of the incident who are experiencing symptoms such as cough or trouble breathing, should seek medical evaluation," the sheriff's department wrote in a social media post. "Those in the area who are not symptomatic should continue to monitor themselves for signs such as coughing, wheezing, or difficulty breathing, and seek care if symptoms develop."

The pilot was headed to the Alpena Combat Readiness Training Center for a joint training exercise called Operation Northern Cactus. Operation Northern Cactus takes members from the Air National Guard's 149th Fighter Wing, headquartered at Joint Base San Antonio-Lackland, Texas, "to an austere environment to participate in a simulated deployment exercise," Joint Base San Antonio said in August.

There have been incidents of hydrazine poisoning in the past.

In 2011, a military F-16's emergency power unit activated while the aircraft was inside an underground hangar in Israel, filling the area with hydrazine vapor. Four ground technicians were exposed for less than a minute.

One technician developed markedly abnormal blood tests, including reading that indicated possible muscle damage, along with elevated liver enzymes. He was hospitalized and treated with intravenous fluids. His tests eventually returned to normal and he returned to military service.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration documented a 2003 incident in which a roughly 5-gallon container of anhydrous hydrazine was released at a freight facility in Massachusetts.

Four employees were exposed to vapors and contaminated. They were taken to a hospital after decontamination. Two suffered respiratory inflammation from inhaling the vapors.

'It was a little scary,' one crash witness says

A witness, Wendy Sobeck, who was working from home in Blair Township at the time of the crash, said she was startled by the sound of a large crash and explosion. She rushed outside to see plumes of black smoke.

Her husband, Fred, went to the crash site and saw debris. Police told onlookers the plane was full of munitions and could explode, Wendy Sobeck told The Detroit News.

“The neighbor told us that he actually saw it happen ... the plane crash into the field,” said Sobeck, traffic and billing manager for the Midwestern Broadcasting Co. 

“Thankfully it landed to the side field of the home that’s there, so it didn’t hit any structures. ... It was a little scary and a little too close to home.” 

The Sobecks were told to shelter in place and close their windows and doors around 3 p.m. Sept. 17.

Soon after, residents within a mile of the crash were told to evacuate their homes because of the hazardous chemicals spilled. They sheltered at the nearby Kensington Church in Traverse City.

Residents and businesspeople were told around 9:45 p.m. Sept. 17 that it was safe to return, Michigan State Police said on the social media platform X. Authorities escorted some to their residences, according to state police.

Munson Healthcare warned people near the crash to watch for symptoms such as coughing, wheezing or trouble breathing. The health system said only people who were within a 1-mile radius of the crash and who are symptomatic should seek treatment.
