The US Navy Retired Its Fastest Carrier Fighter & Replaced It With A Slower One

TheTools · 22.10.2026 17:01 · source · permalink

Miguel Lagoa | Shutterstock | Simple Flying

The Grumman F-14 Tomcat could fly at around 1,544 mph (2,485 km/h). Its replacement, the Boeing F/A-18E/F Super Hornet, is considerably slower. So why did the US Navy retire the Tomcat in 2006 and hand its place in the carrier air wing to an aircraft that could not match its top speed? For anyone comparing the two fighter jets on paper, it still looks like a strange trade.

This article examines the missions the F-14 was designed to perform, how the end of the Cold War changed the Navy's priorities, and the maintenance costs of the Tomcat's variable-sweep wings. It will also look at the Super Hornet's multirole capabilities, the debate over alternatives such as the Super Tomcat 21, and the Navy's decision to move on from one of its most recognizable fighters.

Speed Figures That Still Make The Tomcat Look Untouchable

The numbers make the difference plain. The National Naval Aviation Museum's entry, hosted by the Naval History and Heritage Command, lists the F-14D's maximum speed at 1,544 mph (2,485 km/h), comfortably above Mach 2 at altitude. That is a remarkable figure for a carrier-based fighter. NAVAIR, by comparison, lists the Super Hornet's maximum speed as Mach 1.8 or better and identifies it as the F-14's replacement.

A comparison published by Simple Flying puts the Super Hornet's top speed at about 1,190 mph (1,915 km/h), roughly 350 mph (570 km/h) below the Tomcat's quoted figure. The National Interest lists the F-14 maximum as Mach 2.34. Figures vary with source and conditions, but the conclusion is clear: in a straight-line dash, the Tomcat was considerably faster.

The question is whether that advantage justified the cost. Top speed only tells part of the story. To understand the Navy's choice, it helps to look at the job the Tomcat was built to do, and why that job became less central after the Cold War.

The Cold War Mission That Gave Speed Its Purpose

Speed was a means of protecting the carrier. Grumman built the F-14 around its AN/AWG-9 radar and the AIM-54 Phoenix missile, and as The War Zone recounts through former Tomcat pilot Francesco "Paco" Chierici, the aircraft could carry up to six Phoenix missiles after the F-111B program intended for the fleet-defense role was abandoned. The idea was to reach incoming bombers quickly and engage them before they came within striking range of the ships. The Tomcat was built for a particular threat, and it was exceptionally well suited to that task.

Its radar could track 24 targets and support attacks on six at once, while the Phoenix could engage targets at distances exceeding 100 miles (160 km) under suitable conditions. Speed helped the F-14 quickly reach the outer edge of the carrier group's defensive zone. According to The Aviationist, the Super Hornet's external weapon pylons add drag and make it less suited to the Tomcat's high-speed interception role. For defending a fleet against long-range bomber attacks, the F-14 had a clear advantage. That did not make it the best choice for every Navy mission.

The Tomcat's strengths were tied to the threat it had been built to meet. Once the Soviet Union collapsed, the Navy had to reconsider how much it should spend on a fleet-defense specialist designed for a very different strategic landscape, so the question rises almost automatically: what happened to the Navy's need for a high-speed interceptor when the Soviet bomber fleet stopped being the main worry?

How A Vanishing Threat Opened The Door To The Hornet Family

With the Cold War over, the Soviet bomber threat that had shaped the F-14's design no longer looked the way it once had. The Aviationist notes that the aircraft's original mission had become less pressing, while the Super Hornet offered newer systems and a lighter maintenance burden. Those practical details mattered: spare parts, technician hours, and maintenance costs all affected how many aircraft the Navy could keep ready for operations. Simple Flying has also noted that the Navy no longer needed a carrier-based, high-altitude interceptor built around the same Cold War requirements. The F-14 remained capable, but its most distinctive strengths were no longer as central to the fleet's daily work.

The decision was disputed at the time. Replacing the F-14 with an all-Hornet carrier air wing drew criticism from people who believed the Navy was giving up too much air-to-air capability. In a May 1991 statement entered into the Congressional Record, Vietnam War ace and Congressman Randy “Duke” Cunningham challenged the proposed F/A-18E/F's ability to match the Tomcat.

The debate continued over the following years, with a 1993 Government Accountability Office report comparing the F/A-18E/F with the F-14D and examining the Navy’s rationale for selecting the newer fighter. Grumman offered another path: the Super Tomcat 21, a proposed upgrade with more capable engines, avionics and combat systems. The War Zone detailed in its examination of the proposed design how the company hoped to keep the design competitive, while former Navy test pilot Kevin Mason said the upgraded aircraft would have been faster and able to carry more, as Simple Flying reported.

The proposal never became a fleet aircraft. The Navy went with the Super Hornet, prioritizing a newer multirole platform and lower operating risk over extending the life of the Tomcat design. The debate was about more than dogfighting performance. The Navy also had to weigh the cost of keeping the F-14 ready, much of it tied to the aircraft's most recognizable feature.

The Maintenance Bill Hidden Inside The Swing Wings

Variable-sweep wings were the Tomcat's signature and its burden. They swept back for speed and forward for slow carrier approaches. Even the proposed Super Tomcat 21 would have retained the basic airframe and its maintenance demands. The Super Hornet's fixed wings removed that particular system from the equation, reducing the number of moving parts that maintainers had to deal with at sea.

The cost difference was substantial. Simple Flying has reported that the Super Hornet cost roughly half as much as the Tomcat and incurred about a third of its maintenance expense, while also being more reliable and easier to operate. The National Interest has likewise highlighted the Hornet family's reliability and the Super Hornet's more forgiving carrier-landing characteristics. Carrier aircraft must launch and recover from a moving deck, often at night or in bad weather. More forgiving handling can ease both risk and workload.

For budget planners, lower costs and easier maintenance made a strong case. The Navy also needed to know what the Super Hornet could do beyond fleet defense.

One Airframe Doing Fighter, Strike, And Tanker Work

The Super Hornet's biggest advantage was its ability to take on several jobs. The F-14 started life as a fleet-defense interceptor and later took on a strike role. The Super Hornet was designed for both air-to-air combat and air-to-ground missions. The Aviationist points to its 11 weapon stations and its ability to carry laser-guided bombs, JDAMs, HARM anti-radiation missiles, and standoff weapons. The Tomcat eventually gained precision-strike capability, but the Super Hornet was built around a broader mission set, supported by newer avionics and an integrated cockpit.

Logistics oriented the choice further. The Super Hornet is a larger evolution of the legacy Hornet, so the Navy could draw on familiar systems, training, and supply arrangements rather than supporting a wholly separate aircraft family. Simple Flying has noted that supply-chain considerations helped make it the lower-risk choice. As examined by Simple Flying, with external fuel, the aircraft has a range of more than 2,000 miles (3,218 km) and a service ceiling of 50,000 feet (15,240 meters). It can also carry a buddy-refueling store to refuel other aircraft in flight. One type could therefore support fighter missions, strikes, and aerial refueling, reducing the need to fit the air wing around as many specialized platforms.

The Navy gained a more flexible aircraft, but the Super Hornet has now spent decades in service. Can a design chosen for versatility keep pace with the next era of carrier aviation?

A Fond Farewell To A Legend And The Maverick Act

The change did not make the Tomcat any less memorable. Anyone who has watched one sweep its wings back and accelerate away understands why the aircraft still has such a following. Its retirement was followed by an unusually strict effort to keep parts out of foreign hands. Simple Flying reported that the Department of Defense ordered the majority of retired F-14s destroyed in Arizona, with the last aircraft destroyed in 2009. The concern was Iran, which still operates the type. As Navy Times explains, Congress barred sales of F-14 parts after foreign buyers still got hold of some.

Then Hollywood changed the mood. In Top Gun: Maverick, the veteran pilot escapes in an F-14 during the film's final act, and the Maverick Act borrows his name. The Senate passed the bill unanimously on April 28, 2026, according to Navy Times. It would transfer three surplus Tomcats from the Arizona boneyard to the US Space and Rocket Center in Huntsville, Alabama, at no cost to taxpayers, provided the aircraft are demilitarized. The War Zone's report describes the measure as a narrow exception to the policy that has kept retired Tomcats from being preserved intact. As we previously reported, the bill is a narrow exception that could put one Tomcat back in the air over the United States.

The Super Hornet gave the US Navy a fighter that could cover more missions with fewer logistical complications, but versatility does not guarantee a long service life. After decades aboard US carriers, the aircraft faces the same fundamental challenge that eventually caught up with the Tomcat: how does the Navy replace a proven fighter without losing capabilities it still needs? The Tomcat's retirement offers a useful point of comparison, particularly as its story continues well beyond its final operational flight.
