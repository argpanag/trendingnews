Army details plan to contain lead dust ahead of Wednesday detonation in Iowa | CNN

TheTools · 24.11.2026 09:00 · source · permalink

The plan to detonate explosive material at an ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa – a community in the rural southeastern corner of the state – that was delayed by days of legal and political wrangling is now scheduled to take place Wednesday morning, the day before Thanksgiving, the US Army said.

“A controlled mitigated detonation is far preferable for public health and safety than an uncontrolled detonation, which is a worst-case scenario,” Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle said at a Tuesday news conference in Middletown.

The military is moving forward with the operation after a permit from the Environmental Protection Agency was reinstated by a federal appeals court Monday and following an earlier statement from President Donald Trump saying he also wanted agreement from Iowa’s Democratic governor-elect before allowing it to continue.

Environmental groups filed suit last week to stop the detonation, saying it risks fouling air, contaminating water and harming residents’ health. The Army has argued the stockpile is too unstable to be left alone.

An emergency alert sent to cell phones in the area by the Federal Emergency Management Agency said the detonation will take place Wednesday at 9 a.m. CT. The agency said it will contact residents within 5 miles of the facility to provide evacuation or relocation assistance, and the alert encouraged people within 5 to 20 miles to stay indoors with their windows closed.

The planned explosion is meant to deal with more than 60 barrels of lead azide, a material used to detonate munitions, that is in a particularly unstable state after being stored long-term at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant. US Army officials have said they are unable to move the material due to its volatile condition.

The detonation plan, which was announced to the public only five days before its originally scheduled date, was met with criticism by the community, some elected officials and environmental groups, as it has the potential to risk lead exposure across the plains of Iowa, and especially near the plant’s home in Middletown.

How officials plan to contain the blast

Army officials detailed a wide range of measures during the detonation to lower the risk of lead spreading through the air.

“We have continuously added to the mitigation measures throughout the entire time we’ve been working on this, and we’re going to continue to add today,” Telle told CNN’s Julia Vargas Jones on Tuesday.

The concrete storage facilities are already covered in dirt, and officials said they have added sand barriers around the site along with water and gel blocks to minimize the amount of lead escaping into the air.

“We don’t want to give the public the feeling that there’s absolutely no risk,” Telle said. “The public needs to know what risk we understand and take precaution to protect themselves against any risk they perceive.”

The last time a lead azide detonation of this scale was conducted by the Army was in 1977, said Col. Aaron Teller, commander of the 71st Explosive Ordnance Disposal Group, which is in charge of the controlled explosion.

Why the Army says it can’t wait

The Army discovered in March that the lead azide, kept in a pair of bunkers the Army refers to as magazines, had been degrading, according to Telle, and the Environmental Protection Agency was notified of its existence and need for disposal that month, according to court documents. The remains of the US lead azide stockpile were moved to the Iowa facility around 2009, ATF Director Robert Cekada said, but transporting it again now could potentially cause the lead azide to accidentally explode, and officials say the only safe option is detonating the material at the plant.

“Just removing the lid from one of those containers could cause a detonation of that barrel and a sympathetic detonation of every barrel inside either one of those magazines,” Cekada said. Crystals on the barrels also keep crews from opening them to treat the lead azide with a chemical that could neutralize it, Army officials said.

Lead azide is a white powder or needle-like crystals and “may explode from shock, heat, flame or friction when dry,” according to a hazardous materials database maintained by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration.

After facing several delays and an intervention from President Donald Trump, the US Army said in a press conference on Tuesday that they are fully prepared and confident to move forward with the detonation of nearly five tons of material containing lead azide in Iowa.

Dramatic weather shifts can also make the stockpile less stable, officials said. Snow is forecast for the area late Wednesday after temperatures in the 60s over the weekend, according to Maj. Gen. Eric Shirley, commanding general of US Army Sustainment Command.

The EPA gave the Army a permit to detonate the material starting November 13, although Army officials have said cold weather could possibly cause it to accidentally explode before a planned detonation.

The Federal Aviation Administration issued a temporary flight restriction in the area surrounding the plant from Tuesday through 12:52 p.m. CT Wednesday.

Health risks and testing

Despite the mitigation plan, the detonation has the potential to spread lead in a plume that could extend many miles outside the plant. A forecast map produced by the military shows the greatest risk of lead exposure extending southwest of the plant into sparsely populated areas of Iowa and northeast Missouri.

Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds issued a disaster emergency proclamation for Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren counties, a step that will help give more resources to those areas, after the governor opened a state emergency operations center last week. The governor also said up to 100 members of the Iowa National Guard will be on the ground Wednesday to help public safety efforts, but will not help the Army with its detonation.

Missouri Gov. Mike Kehoe activated his state’s center Tuesday ahead of the detonation, according to a statement.

“If there are women who are pregnant, children under the age of six, or children in general, and individuals who may have chronic disease, you definitely want to be out of that area. I would evacuate that area. That’s signaled in the model. Outside of that area, if you’re close to that proximity and you’re uncomfortable, I think you need to do what you need to do to take care of your family,” said Sean Kaufman, principal deputy assistant secretary for preparedness and response at the Department of Health and Human Services.

FEMA said it is helping residents who choose to leave the area, beginning Tuesday.

“We’ve made it very clear to those who are within a southwesterly location, if they choose to relocate, we will offer services,” FEMA Administrator Cameron Hamilton said.

Residents who need relocation assistance are being told to call FEMA at 1-800-621-3362, according to an Army public safety advisory.

Lead is toxic to the brain and can have severe health impacts, especially in children, according to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — which is especially notable in a state that has the second-highest rate of cancer of any US state.

People who live in the affected area can have their property tested for lead contamination, an EPA official said Tuesday.

“If you want your home sampled, your property sampled, your school sampled, you will get that sample, and the EPA will assist with that sampling,” said Tom Croci, the EPA’s associate administrator for national security and operations coordination.

About 350 farms could be affected based on the expected direction of the plume, said Richard Fordyce, under secretary for farm production and conservation at the US Department of Agriculture.

The Army has shared information on how to submit a claim for property damage during the detonation, but didn’t specify what would be considered damage.

NASA will help by deploying “advanced scientific instruments and expertise to collect environmental and atmospheric data to inform the broader federal response,” it said in a post on X.

While federal officials have said they will clean up any contamination if needed, Andrew Whelton, a professor of environmental engineering at Purdue University, told CNN’s Brianna Keilar it is important for residents to get clear answers on how safe it is to be back home. He cited the 2023 toxic chemical spill after a train derailed in East Palestine, Ohio, where he said officials told residents to trust the government and some people got sick after they went home.

“Trusting is nice, but we need to verify and understand exactly what decisions they’re going to make, what information they’re going to use, and who makes that decision to lift the evacuation order,” he said.

Trump’s pause and the court fight

Trump announced Monday that he had ordered the military to halt the plan and instead wait for approval from the Court of Appeals for the 8th Circuit, as well as Iowa’s Gov.-elect Rob Sand.

“I feel the proper course is to get rid of this longterm problem but, at the same time, have ordered the Military not to do so until such time as the Eighth Circuit agrees that we can go forward, and am further making this decision fully subject to the incoming Governor Elect, Rob Sand, agreeing to the operation,” Trump wrote on Truth Social.

Trump also said he would hold Sand accountable if the plan did not proceed and there was an accidental detonation from the material’s unstable status.

In his own social media statement on Monday, Sand did not call for the operation to stop but continued to ask for transparency, saying, “The Administration should make this decision based on the information they have from the people they brought in to do the job months ago and have been on the ground in Middletown for weeks.”

The Iowa Environmental Council and Sierra Club filed an emergency motion Thursday asking for a pause in the detonation, alleging the EPA had not properly evaluated the dangers and risks of the explosion or other ways of handling it before issuing the permit. The Army has said it formed a team in January 2026 to study how to deal with the material, and that detonation is its best option, according to court filings.

While an appeals court initially granted a temporary stay, a three-judge panel lifted it on Monday, saying there was not enough evidence the environmental groups would prevail in their arguments.

After the court ruled Monday, Telle said the detonation would happen Wednesday morning “as previously scheduled, based on forecast wind conditions.”

Leaders and residents react

After initially asking the Army to pause its plans, Reynolds said in a statement Tuesday that she supports the planned detonation after being in touch with Cabinet members and Army leaders, including Secretary of War Pete Hegseth.

“I believe, based on the information I’ve been given, that the danger is immediate, and I trust the Army will do everything in its power to protect the people who could be affected,” she wrote.

Sand said Sunday that because of “decisions that have already been made over the course of months, if not years, no good options exist and the situation is more dangerous and unpredictable than it ever should have been.” He added Monday, “Everyday Iowans are entitled to know how Middletown came to this situation, and learn why the administration believes this is the best path forward.”

Likewise, Republican Sen. Joni Ernst of Iowa did not call for further delay, but said, “These materials should never have been allowed to get to this point, and Iowans are right to be frustrated.”

Local residents have expressed frustration at the sudden timeline for detonation, as well as concerns about how the spread of lead could impact people’s health, along with the health of farm animals and land.

In Missouri, where state officials expect minimal impact, the Republican governor also criticized the administration’s slow drip of information.

“I do join my fellow governors and agency officials in neighboring states in their frustration with how this has been handled,” Kehoe said Monday.

Telle said the Army needed time to make sure it was releasing accurate information and “get it right.” At the same time, he promised a renewed effort to make sure other volatile military stockpiles don’t get into this condition in the future.

“We are redoubling that effort now, and we will be examining every single storage facility across the US Army,” he said.

CNN’s Danya Gainor, Eduardo Cuevas, Julia Vargas Jones, Holly Yan, Kara Devlin and Betsy Klein contributed to this report.
