Las Vegas Grand Prix: Max Verstappen the constant amid chaos as he takes revenge on George Russell

TheTools · 24.11.2026 05:18 · source · permalink

Max Verstappen has won three of the four Las Vegas Grands Prix

  • Published
    22 November 2026

Warning: This article contains offensive language

Max Verstappen was a constant amid the chaos at a remarkable Las Vegas Grand Prix.

He was the star figure in a race that had 34 overtaking moves for the lead in 50 laps and 352 overall, in which the biggest advantage the leader ever had was 1.5 seconds, and in which there were five cars fighting nose to tail for the lead to the final lap, and for much of the race many more than that.

Even better for Verstappen, George Russell made a critical mistake 24 hours after he had revived his long-standing, on-off spat with the Dutchman.

Russell, who had been Verstappen's main threat from the start of the race, locked up his brakes and speared into the barrier trying to pass Mercedes team-mate Kimi Antonelli for second place behind Verstappen with two laps to go.

Verstappen said: "He speaks a lot next to the track, and it doesn't really bother me because he's so far below my level that it doesn't really make any sense for me anyway to try and challenge that, you know?

"And today, karma is a bitch. It was honestly fantastic when I looked in my mirror and I saw what was happening. I was, like, "Fantastic, my day is only getting better."

"And of course, when I then crossed the line in first, my day was complete."

Verstappen felt motivated to make these remarks because of what Russell had said the day before.

After an on-track altercation in practice, the pair had had an exchange of views in the stewards' room. Then, after Verstappen took pole from Russell - after fastest man Antonelli's five-place penalty was taken into account - the Briton took a pop at the Dutchman.

"I am not just going to accept somebody to screw me over like this and walk around like they are bigger and better than everyone else," Russell said. "Somebody has got to stand up to that guy."

In the paddock, a number of people felt that Verstappen was probably not the right "guy" to pick a fight with.

Even though Russell was battling with Verstappen throughout the race, the nature of their drives was different.

Russell would pass the Red Bull on the straight. Then Verstappen would reclaim the lead in more extreme fashion, on the outside of the fast kink leading on to the pit straight, or on the outside again at the long Turn Three.

Hardly anyone else even tried these moves, let alone pulled them off. Verstappen made them look routine.

After his error, Russell managed to extricate himself from the crash barrier, but he had turned a certain podium finish into a ninth place.

It was the perfect encapsulation of a season in which almost nothing has gone right.

For Verstappen, it was almost too good to be true.

'Russell is living rent free in Verstappen's head!'

'It was Indy 500 and Nascar and Formula 1 mixed'

Max Verstappen, in second place, is now 40 points ahead of George Russell in the drivers' championship

The race was unlike any grand prix anyone younger than about 70 had ever seen.

The fabled slipstreaming battles of a pre-chicane Monza in the 1960s and early 1970s - that led to the closest ever finish in F1 history at the 1971 Italian Grand Prix,, external when the first five cars crossed the line 0.6secs apart - were the closest comparison at the highest echelon of the sport.

In modern terms, it was more reminiscent of American oval racing, where the high-speed nature of the race tracks leads to long chains of cars slipstreaming and lots of overtaking, little of it meaningful until the final stages.

Mercedes F1 team boss Toto Wolff pretty much hit the nail on the head when he said: "It was Indy 500 and Nascar and Formula 1 mixed - I enjoyed it."

And it encapsulated something else about F1 2026 - the internal debate that has been raging in the sport about artificiality ever since the latest engines were introduced.

The near 50-50 split between internal combustion and electrical power has created energy-starved cars where the batteries run out before the end of long straights.

On certain tracks this has led to a form of racing known as "yo-yo", where cars constantly pass and re-pass each other depending on their respective levels of battery charge.

After a chaotic few races early in the year, this has diminished as teams have better understood energy management.

But in Las Vegas the combination of long straights and slipstreaming brought it to its apotheosis. And no-one quite knew what to think about it.

Verstappen said: "Really, really surreal and something that I've never seen before in Formula 1 myself.

"It was just a crazy amount of fighting. The slipstream was so big that you - it was just impossible to drive off, and it was just crazy how many people were just following you.

"It was just very exciting, I guess, for everyone to watch. I know, of course, it's a big show around here the whole weekend, and I hope that we delivered on track."

That was a carefully judged answer for the television cameras, the sort that sounds like one thing, but if you know the person's real views, you can read between the lines to see the nuances that hint at something else.

In the news conference, Verstappen's true feelings came out. Was it a kind of racing he enjoyed?

"When I look at it from my racer heart, no," he said. "When you look at it as a show spectacle, yes.

"I enjoyed my repassing and everything, but I prefer to look at it with my racing heart. Even my heart rate, I was not nervous at all because it's just felt kind of cruising out there, just trying to press the battery whenever it was correct or defend.

"But for the show, when you look at it from the outside, probably, yes, you like it. But, yeah, I don't know.

"I probably shouldn't say it. I probably should not. I remember winning one like this when I was playing Mario Kart.

"Sometimes you drop back and you get drafts, and there's like a catch-up, you know? Always when you're dropping back, you go faster. So that's the last time that I felt something like this."

Other drivers said similar things.

McLaren's Lando Norris, who finished second, said: "From a racing point of view, from the outside, I'm sure it probably was the best race ever in Formula 1.

"You never know what's going to happen. There were overtakes out of nowhere, and some of the overtakes weren't overtakes, you press boost and you go past someone. But the racing was still good. You're still fighting for position, and there are a lot of good things in there."

Russell said: "It was interesting. It was quite fun for maybe half the race, but then it started to get a bit tiring at one point because it was just absolutely impossible to get away."

Essentially, this is an argument between racing purists and casual fans, especially the new ones who know little of the complexities of the sport. Wolff related a story about his wife Susie's experience with corporate guests during the race.

"I guess you will have many people that will say that's not racing," Wolff said. "Personally, we need to be open minded.

"Susie was in the Paddock Club and she said that for 90 minutes people were screaming with the overtakes.

"The instant feedback I got from the friends watching, our sponsors, was that was awesome. Stefano [Domenicali, the F1 chairman] liked it very much. That's not going to happen everywhere."

Is F1 making a mistake in changing rules again?

Verstappen's Mario Kart comparison brought back memories of his criticisms of the new F1 in the first few races, when he said he hated it so much he was considering stopping.

Since then, the current rules have been tweaked, and more changes have been introduced for 2027 and 2028 to reduce this effect.

And, irony of ironies, this weekend it was announced that there will be new rules for 2030. These retain turbo hybrid engines but reduce the electrical element, with the express intention of removing energy starvation and making racing 'pure' again. The likely result will be that this kind of race could not be possible again after that.

In the context of Vegas, is this actually a mistake?

Wolff said: "We're still doing the right thing. It's what we all wanted. But I have no doubt people will complain there is not great overtaking. We go back to the 'glorious years' in the 2000s. But we're gonna get those regs right. We see what we can do. It just needs a kilometre and a half straight."

McLaren team principal Andrea Stella added: "It's very difficult to have a position as simple as saying, 'Oh, that's the race we want.' Because as soon as you say that, you will see some other perspectives which are important to consider.

"Like, I still have to talk to Lando and Oscar (Piastri) and understand, did you enjoy it or that was kind of too much and at times it felt like this is going to go very wrong from a safety point of view?

"So, I don't want to express a net position. Because, in fact, I don't have it.

"This race gave us a lot of information that we cannot ignore. In terms of the attention, the spectacle, in terms of, you know, like, wow, that's incredible racing, is this what we want?

"My main recommendation is that we remain kind of analytical. And we remain caring for the interest of the sport.

"Because we live in a world where everything is polarised, a world in which you need to have an opinion in one second. Otherwise you are a nobody. You need to have an opinion that is polarised.

"Well, I would like to have people that actually still value complexity of analysis. And caring for a common interest. That's my main message.

"When you have examples like today which offer so much information it would be a shame to process it in the space of a hot review. And say, 'Oh, let's abandon the new regulations.' That's not the case.

"But definitely there's some interesting information that we should consider from a technical point of view, from a sport regulation point of view."
