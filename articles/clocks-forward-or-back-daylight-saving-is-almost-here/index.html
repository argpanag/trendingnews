Clocks forward or back? Daylight saving is almost here

TheTools · 01.10.2026 21:40 · source · permalink

For millions of Australians, longer evenings are about to return.

4 min read

By Cameron Carr

Daylight saving begins this weekend in several parts of the country, bringing more sunlight into the afternoon and evening.

But the annual clock change also means losing an hour of sleep overnight, and it can take some people a little while to adjust.

Here's what to know.

When does daylight saving start in 2026?

Daylight saving begins on Sunday 4 October 2026.

In NSW, Victoria, Tasmania and the ACT, clocks move forward by one hour at 2am, jumping to 3am.

In South Australia, clocks also move forward by an hour, changing from 2am Australian Central Standard Time to 3am Australian Central Daylight Time.

Daylight saving will continue until Sunday 4 April 2027, when clocks move back by one hour.

So, are the clocks going forward or back?

Forward.

An easy way to remember the change is "spring forward".

At 2am on Sunday, clocks in the daylight-saving states and territories will jump straight to 3am.

That means one hour effectively disappears from the night.

The benefit comes later in the day. Sunrise and sunset will appear an hour later on the clock, giving people more daylight after work and school.

The downside is that mornings will become darker, particularly for early risers.

Where does daylight saving apply?

Daylight saving isn't observed across the whole country.

NSW, Victoria, Tasmania, South Australia and the Australian Capital Territory observe daylight saving.

Queensland, Western Australia and the Northern Territory do not.

There are also some local variations. Broken Hill in western NSW follows South Australia's time zone and observes daylight saving, while Lord Howe Island moves its clocks forward by 30 minutes rather than a full hour.

During daylight saving, Australia therefore operates across five time zones rather than its usual three.

Can changing clocks affect your sleep?

Losing an hour might not sound like much, but changing the clock can temporarily disrupt the body's internal sleep-wake cycle.

The Sleep Health Foundation says the spring transition can be harder to adapt to than the autumn change, when you gain an hour.

Most people adjust without much difficulty, although some may feel more tired or sleepy while their bodies adapt to the new schedule.

Professor Ben Bullock of Swinburne University told SBS News how the change can have "significant effects" on physical and mental health.

"There is evidence to show that if you adjust light levels and light timing, it can affect people's circadian rhythms, their underlying biological rhythms," he said.

"And if you're vulnerable to the effects of sleep, it can have quite significant effects on your physical and mental health as well."

How can you prepare for the time change?

A little preparation can make the adjustment easier.

The Sleep Health Foundation recommends gradually bringing bedtime forward by around 15 to 20 minutes for three or four nights before the clocks change.

It also suggests:

  • Getting natural light in the morning can also help reset the body's internal clock.
  • Getting enough sleep in the days leading up to the change.
  • Keeping your bedtime and wake-up time consistent.
  • Spending time outside during daylight, particularly in the morning.
  • Exercising during the day.
  • Avoiding caffeine later in the day or evening.
  • Making your bedroom dark, quiet and comfortable at night.

Don't forget about clocks and appliances that don't automatically update.

Most smartphones, computers, and internet-connected devices should adjust automatically, but watches, clocks, ovens, and other household appliances may need to be set manually.
