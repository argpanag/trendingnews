The world is hooked on anime. Japan, its creative homeland, could use the win.

In recent years, Japan’s economic prospects have offered little good news. Its population is aging and shrinking. Its manufacturers are under pressure from low-cost Chinese competitors. Headlines are dominated by energy shortages and ballooning government debt.

In New York last month, there was none of that gloom.

A decade ago, New York Comic Con — the annual four-day pop culture convention at the Javits Convention Center — was dominated by American comic books and superheroes. At this year’s gathering, anime was everywhere. This Japanese subset of animation, traditionally featuring flat graphic illustrations and characters with large, expressive eyes, had taken center stage.

Fans dressed as Nezuko, the heroine of the hit “Demon Slayer” series, clutched Hello Kitty bags and wore lanyards printed with characters from the ninja anime “Naruto.” Chris Cosentino, a New Yorker, pushed a stroller ferrying his 13-year-old Pomeranian, Haku, dressed in the straw hat and red vest of Luffy from the pirate anime “One Piece.”

The prevailing sentiment among attendees: They love anime, and they love Japan.

“Anime has become such a cultural phenomenon all over the world,” said Hiroki Totoki, the chief executive of Sony Group, the conglomerate behind recent blockbusters such as “Demon Slayer: Kimetsu no Yaiba Infinity Castle” — North America’s highest-grossing international film ever. For Japan, with its aging society, “it’s one of the bright spots,” he said.
