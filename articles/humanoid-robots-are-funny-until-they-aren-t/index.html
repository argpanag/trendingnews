Humanoid Robots Are Funny – Until They Aren’t

TheTools · 11.09.2026 13:34 · source · permalink

Tiangong Ultra completed the 100m final at the 2026 World Humanoid Robot Games in 8.64 seconds, beating Usain Bolt’s world record – before crashing into a padded wall. It was one of several robots that stumbled and toppled while competing in sports including boxing, football, track and field, and martial arts.

Why Do Humanoid Robots Keep on Falling?

But running faster and falling less does not necessarily indicate whether humanoid robots are ready for real jobs. Dennis Hong, founding director of UCLA’s Robotics and Mechanisms Laboratory (RoMeLa), says a 100m race is a relatively well-defined challenge: the course and objective are known, allowing engineers to train the behaviour millions of times in simulation.

“Running fast demonstrates impressive physical capability,” Hong says. “Dealing with the unexpected requires physical intelligence.”

Karen Liu, a Stanford University professor who leads The Movement Lab, calls the ability to respond to unfamiliar physical environments “motion intelligence”. Robots can learn eye-catching skills such as running, jumping and dancing through repeated simulations, she says, but interacting with the real world is much harder.

Liu compares it to entering an Airbnb for the first time: within minutes a person can locate the switches and refrigerator, navigate the furniture and operate an unfamiliar shower. Teaching a robot to adapt that quickly remains a major challenge.

“For a robot, perception, decision-making and physical response all have to happen accurately and fast enough to prevent a small error from becoming a fall,” Hong says. “Reproducing that seamless loop is extraordinarily difficult.”

Yet viral falls can obscure how far humanoid robots have progressed. Basic locomotion has improved dramatically in just the past two years, Liu says, and today’s falls increasingly occur as researchers push robots beyond their limits.

“We don’t see falling during walking any more,” she says. “We see falling during crazy stuff.”

Hong sees falling as necessary to that progress. “I often tell my students, ‘The more a robot falls, the better it can walk.’ If we understand why it failed and learn from it, that failure becomes the next step forward.”

From Slapstick to the Battlefield

Christoph Bartneck, a University of Canterbury professor who studies human-robot interaction, describes the spectacle as a form of schadenfreude and slapstick comedy. Robots’ humanlike bodies make their failures funny, he says, but also raise the question of what humanoid design is meant to achieve.

He examines how people respond to robots. His latest study, titled “Ethical Asymmetry in Human-Robot Interaction”, explores how people judge others based on whether they treat robots with kindness or cruelty. But Bartneck also questions what a humanoid robot running a race is really meant to prove. “If you want a machine to get quickly from A to B on a flat surface, you give it wheels,” he says. “There’s no reason to use legs.”

Humanoid robots aren’t only being developed for competitions. Beijing Institute of Technology (BIT) researchers have explicitly identified military applications for humanoid robots, including its BHR-01, which is being specialised for a broad range of applications, from riot control to fire rescue to counterterrorism operations. China’s National University of Defense Technology is already testing humanoids for military physical tasks and autonomous reconnaissance, while the US Army Research Laboratory is also exploring how autonomous robots could scout ahead of soldiers.

Their potential military use raises questions beyond technical readiness. Bartneck says ethical boundaries should be established before widespread adoption, but distinguishes military robots from autonomous weapons. The stakes change considerably, he says, if a machine can independently select and attack a human target, rather than perform supporting tasks such as carrying supplies or surveying an active warzone. “We definitely need some clear ethical guidelines of what we will consider appropriate for the robots to do and not to do,” Bartneck adds.

The technology is nevertheless advancing quickly. Hong says AI and data-driven control played a huge part in the acceleration. “What has advanced faster than I expected is dynamic locomotion,” which equips a robot with physical intelligence to perform increasingly athletic motions.

Humanoid robotics have undergone repeated hype cycles, but Liu says the progress of the past two years is different. She points to the 100m race: last year’s winning time was around 20 seconds, roughly 12 seconds slower than this year’s. Over the next five to 10 years, she expects robots to master locomotion across varied terrain, at higher speeds and while carrying objects.

But setbacks will continue as researchers test those limits. “You will start seeing falls because that’s the boundary.”

The Hardest Tasks Are the Most Ordinary

Humanoid robots’ hands remain a particularly stubborn problem. Their degrees of freedom are almost as numerous as those of the entire body, Liu says, making them incredibly difficult to replicate.

“Just grabbing a cup – it turns out to be stupidly complicated,” Bartneck says. The gap becomes clearer with far more mundane tasks like folding laundry or reliably moving an object from point A to point B.

A 2025 review coauthored by Liu found that locomotion and manipulation have both advanced rapidly, but separately. Robots still struggle to walk and handle objects reliably at the same time, particularly outside controlled environments. This reflects the “Sim2Real” gap: performance learned across thousands of simulations does not always transfer successfully to the unpredictable physical world.

For Hong, the real turning point will not be when humanoid robots stop falling – “Humans fall too” – but when they can encounter something unfamiliar, understand it and physically adapt in real time. “That’s when we’re no longer just watching a machine perform impressive behaviours,” he says. “We’re watching something demonstrate physical intelligence.”

Competitions can measure good progress, he adds, but not real-world readiness: “Real life has no rulebook, no defined course and no reset button.”
