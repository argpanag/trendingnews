Can the Warriors trade, cut, or stop paying Kristaps Porzingis? Here’s what’s on the table

TheTools · 01.11.2026 14:00 · source · permalink

The center’s indefinite absence has left Golden State thin at center and short on options.

Kristaps Porzingis’ health-related, indefinite absence threatens to put the Warriors in the place they hoped to avoid: behind the eight ball. 

Porzingis hasn’t been with the team since informing the front office of a health issue the day before training camp. The Warriors have kept details of his ailment private, though ESPN reported (opens in new tab) that the 12-year veteran is “seriously contemplating” his basketball future.

While Porzingis’ health is the team’s primary concern, his absence has already hurt Golden State. The Warriors were counting on him to space the floor and anchor their defense, provide cushion in the frontcourt next to Draymond Green, and hunt mismatches as a scoring hub while Jimmy Butler recovers. He played 15 of Golden State’s final 20 games last season, and there’s a sense of frustration within the organization that the 7-foot-2 center is absent. 

Without Porzingis, the Warriors have just Draymond Green, 36, and Al Horford, 40, at center. Charles Bassey and Graham Ike, a center on a two-way contract, have been thrust into the mix. The result is a thin center rotation that held up strong against Nikola Jokic in Denver and against the Timberwolves, but could wear down over an 82-game season.

“We got what we got,” Green said. “So, got to make it do what it do. I’m comfortable playing the five. Al’s comfortable playing the five.” 

The Warriors opened themselves up to an outcome like this when they re-signed Porzingis and crossed their fingers. There weren’t great alternative options, and there is value in maintaining his tradable salary slot, but overindexing on talent over Porzingis’ known health risks was a miscalculation. 

At the moment, the Warriors look fairly stuck. Without knowing all the minute details of the situation, here’s a look at the few levers the Warriors can — and can’t — pull.

What’s Porzingis’ current status? 

In an Oct. 14 statement (opens in new tab), the Warriors announced that Porzingis met with general manager Mike Dunleavy and head of the performance staff Rick Celebrini at Chase Center. Part of that announcement included the team’s expectation that Porzingis would be around the team. 

Porzingis did not meet that expectation. 

A week later, the Warriors released an additional statement (opens in new tab), this time specifying that “we are not anticipating Kristaps being around the team in the immediate future.” 

Dunleavy has been in touch with Porzingis’ representation. A social media post on Oct. 28 went viral showing the center getting veneers in Mallorca, Spain. 

Can the Warriors trade Porzingis?

Yes, but probably not imminently. 

Because the Warriors extended him to a contract without a raise, Porzingis has been trade-eligible since July. But finding a trade partner, and one that would work within Golden State’s parameters, is tricky. 

Porzingis has $3 million guaranteed for 2027-28, meaning he effectively has an expiring contract. Trading for such a player can provide a team with more financial flexibility, because that money comes off their books, especially if the outgoing players have long-term contracts.

Is the Warriors’ ceiling higher than everyone thought?

In any trade, the Warriors would not be able to take back more salary than they send out. They’re roughly $300,000 shy of the second apron, which they’re prohibited from exceeding. 

Teams rarely make significant trades early in the season. Most want to evaluate their rosters and let a month or two play out before determining their short-term direction. Only true tanking teams with no real aspirations for this season — a shrinking class because of the lottery reform — would consider taking on a player primarily for the cap benefits before Christmas.

The Athletic reported (opens in new tab) that the Warriors reached out to the Bucks, who fit the bill as a rebuilding team following Giannis Antetokounmpo’s departure. Milwaukee’s centers include Myles Turner, Kel’el Ware, and Jericho Sims. 

Most teams likely view Porzingis as a negative asset, even with his expiring money. The Warriors may have to attach draft capital or take back long-term salary — which they have been hesitant to do — to receive anything of value in return for him. 

The timing and peculiarity of Porzingis’ situation makes finding a deal soon challenging. More possible suitors could eventually emerge, but that won’t help Golden State find relief quickly. 

What if he retires?

It’s unclear if Porzingis will play again. Players who officially retire while under contract forfeit their salary, but their contracts still count against their team’s cap. So there’s not a ton of incentive for a player to voluntarily retire while under contract. 

There is, however, a process in which a team could pursue medical cap relief in the event of a health-induced retirement. If Porzingis walks away, an independent panel of doctors would have to determine that his condition forced him into medical retirement. Only then would his remaining salary come off Golden State’s payroll. 

This process is rare and takes time. A team must wait a full year after a player’s last game to formally petition the NBA for a career-ending medical designation. Porzingis last played April 15.

Are the Warriors paying Porzingis now?

It’s unclear if Porzingis’ absence is excused or unexcused. When someone signs a uniform player contract, they are obligated to render their services in good faith. 

A team source indicated that voiding Porzingis’ contract is not an option currently under consideration.

Situations in which a player fails to fulfill his contractual obligations are unusual. During the 2021-22 season, Ben Simmons stepped away from the 76ers amid an unmet trade demand and off-court mental health issues. Philadelphia withheld nearly $20 million of game checks. 

If the Warriors trade Porzingis, and he does not report to the acquiring team or agrees to a buyout, he would represent even more financial appeal to a hypothetical trade partner. 

What’s the deal with a buyout?

If the Warriors were to waive Porzingis, they’d still be responsible for his remaining salary. But if Porzingis and the team mutually agreed on a buyout, they’d be able to dictate new terms. 

Buyouts happen when a team and player agree to terminate a contract early for a negotiated figure so the player can become a free agent. It most often happens with veterans on expiring contracts who are out of a rotation and would like to try to latch on with a contender instead. 

There doesn’t seem to be much incentive for Porzingis to agree to a buyout. He’s owed $23 million guaranteed. 

The Warriors are $20.4 million over the luxury tax. Porzingis deciding to forfeit his salary would give them significant cash savings and duck them below the first apron, which ironically unlocks added trade flexibility. But by losing a $20 million salary slot to trade, that scenario would be robbing Peter to pay Paul. 

Could the Warriors waive-and-stretch Porzingis?

There is a mechanism for teams to waive a player and stretch their salary over a certain period of time, but that doesn’t apply to this situation. 

Teams can’t waive-and-stretch for cap purposes during the season. The deadline to do so was Aug. 31.

OK, then. What about a trade? 

Maybe Turner, who’s signed with the Bucks through 2028-29. Maybe old friend Kevon Looney, who can’t seem to crack the Lakers’ rotation. The Kings will have more bigs than they can play once Maxime Raynaud and Dylan Cardwell return. Nick Richards is on the edge of Miami’s bench.

But nobody’s really on the block at this point.

What’s the disabled player exception?

Before Jan. 15, a team can apply for a disabled player exception for someone whom an independent doctor rules out for the season. If granted, the team receives a cap credit for the lesser of the non-taxpayer midlevel exception or $10 million. 

The DPE likely wouldn’t apply to the Warriors’ situation. Even if a team is granted a DPE, it has to work within its cap parameters. Because the Warriors are hard-capped at the second apron, they effectively wouldn’t be able to use this particular cap mechanism.
