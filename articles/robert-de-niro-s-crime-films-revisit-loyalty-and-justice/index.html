Robert De Niro's Crime Films Revisit Loyalty and Justice

TheTools · 21.10.2026 00:24 · source · permalink

Robert De Niro's crime films continue to attract attention through their portrayals of criminal loyalty, law enforcement, and contested justice. Goodfellas, directed by Martin Scorsese and released in the United States on September 19, 1990, remains known for its depiction of organized crime, while Heat has returned to Netflix ahead of the planned sequel Heat 2, according to entertainment reports published October 17-20, 2026.

Across these productions, De Niro has played characters whose relationships reveal the tensions between personal allegiance and survival. The films also approach crime from different perspectives, ranging from the internal rules of organized criminal groups to the complicated relationship between a professional thief and the detective pursuing him.

In Goodfellas, De Niro portrays James Jimmy Conway, a character loosely based on James Jimmy the Gent Burke, who was widely linked to the 1978 Lufthansa heist. The film follows Henry Hill, played by Ray Liotta, as he becomes involved with organized crime and learns the importance criminals place on refusing to cooperate with authorities.

After Hill avoids identifying his associates following an arrest for selling untaxed cigarettes, Conway rewards him with cash and offers a lesson that defines the criminal code depicted in the film.

"Never rat on your friends and always keep your mouth shut."

Conway's advice reflects omertà, the traditional code of silence associated with Sicilian and southern Italian organized crime and adopted by some Italian-American criminal groups. However, the story exposes the instability of that code as greed, suspicion, and self-preservation undermine relationships within the organization.

Following the fallout from the Lufthansa heist, Hill becomes an FBI informant, testifies against former associates, and enters witness protection. His eventual decision to cooperate with authorities contrasts with the loyalty Conway previously demanded.

Goodfellas received six Academy Award nominations at the 63rd Academy Awards in 1991, with Joe Pesci winning Best Supporting Actor. The film was adapted from Nicholas Pileggi's nonfiction book Wiseguy, although the specific advice attributed to Conway does not appear in the book, according to MovieWeb.

De Niro's crime-film legacy also includes Heat, the 1995 thriller written and directed by Michael Mann. De Niro plays professional thief Neil McCauley, while Al Pacino portrays Vincent Hanna, a Los Angeles Police Department robbery-homicide lieutenant determined to capture him.

Their rivalry develops into a relationship marked by professional respect and an understanding of each other's commitment to their work. In the film's final confrontation near an airport, Hanna defeats McCauley in a shootout but remains with his dying adversary, giving the ending an emotional dimension beyond the criminal investigation.

Heat originated from Mann's 1989 television film L.A. Takedown, which was developed as a potential NBC series pilot. After the network declined to proceed with the series, Mann reworked the material into the feature film, which grossed more than $187 million worldwide against a reported $60 million budget, according to CBR.

The original film has an 84% rating on Rotten Tomatoes, according to the same report. Its return to Netflix in October 2026 has renewed attention as development of Heat 2 advances.

Mann and Meg Gardiner wrote the Heat 2 novel, published in 2022, as both a prequel and a sequel. The story explores the earlier lives of key characters before the original film and follows surviving criminal Chris Shiherlis after McCauley's death.

According to CBR's October 19 report, the film adaptation was in preparation for production, with filming expected to begin in November 2026. Leonardo DiCaprio, Christian Bale, Stephen Graham, Adam Driver, Jason Clarke, and Odessa Young were among the actors linked to or confirmed for the project in the report, although some character assignments and casting details remained uncertain.

Another De Niro crime drama, Sleepers, raised questions about the relationship between cinematic storytelling and factual claims. Directed by Barry Levinson and released in 1996, the film adapts Lorenzo Carcaterra's book about childhood abuse at a juvenile detention facility and the later revenge undertaken by former inmates.

Carcaterra's account faced scrutiny before the film's release. Sacred Heart of Jesus Church disputed claims associated with his childhood experiences, citing attendance records, while the Manhattan District Attorney's Office said it had no records of the trial described in the story.

In a 1995 interview with Time, Carcaterra defended his account and explained that he had changed identifying details to protect the people involved.

"If it happened here, you make it happen there. Because I have something to hide: the identities of these people."

Sleepers stars De Niro as Father Bobby Carillo, a priest who becomes involved in efforts to protect former inmates accused of killing a guard who abused them. The film's presentation of the events as a true story intensified debate about its historical accuracy and the ethical questions surrounding its depiction of perjury and institutional accountability.

Roger Ebert questioned the film's insistence on authenticity in his review, arguing that the claim mattered because the story depicted moral decisions that would have consequences in real life. The controversy remained a significant part of the film's reception, distinguishing Sleepers from crime dramas whose narratives are presented primarily as fiction.
