Hampshire's significant science trailblazers

TheTools · 14.09.2026 10:28 · source · permalink

 

This month, Southampton plays host to the British Science Festival, which will bring an inspiring programme of free events to the city from September 16-20. To celebrate, we take a look at some of Hampshire science trailblazers, from the engineering expertise of Southampton’s Supermarine to the brilliant mind of James Lind

Hertha Ayrton

Hertha Ayrton

(1854-1923)

Born in Portsea in 1854, Hertha Ayrton was an electrical engineer, mathematician, and suffragette.

After being educated by aunts in London, she studied mathematics at Girton College, Cambridge. However, as she was a woman, she was not awarded a degree – but that did not stop her determination. By the time she died in 1923, she had patented 26 inventions and was the first female professional electrical engineer.

One of her most significant inventions came from her studies into electric arcs which were used in lighting in the late-19th century. As a result, Hertha was the first woman elected to the Institute of Engineers in 1902 but was not admitted to the Royal Society because she was married. Her Ayrton anti-gas fan was also used to clear gases from the trenches in World War I, saving many lives.

Brunel

Isambard Kingdom Brunel

(1806-1859)

One of the greatest engineers of the Industrial Revolution, Isambard Kingdom Brunel was born on Portsea Island in 1806. At the time his father was working on engineering contracts with the Royal Navy in the area.

By the age of four, Isambard had learned the principles of Euclidean geometry. In 1834 he worked with his father Marc on the Thames Tunnel. He went on to build the Clifton Suspension Bridge and to be the first engineer of the Great Western Railway, Brunel is also known for his work on the SS Great Western and the SS Great Britain.

During the Crimean War he constructed a field hospital at Renkioi which was designed with superior hygiene standards and significantly cut mortality rates.

Jen Gupta

(Present)

Professor Jen Gupta grew up in Winchester and is an astrophysicist passionate about talking all things space.

Based at the Institute of Cosmology and Gravitation at the University of Portsmouth, her work specifically involves engagement with the wider community – and as part of her job Jen usually speaks to thousands of schoolchildren each year.

Jen’s PhD research looked at active galactic nuclei (AGN) – galaxies that emit significantly more energy than their stars, and specifically researched blazars.

She regularly appears on national TV and radio to talk about astronomy and science. Jen has also been known to use comedy when talking science to make people laugh.

James Lind

James Lind

(1716-1794)

When sailors were at sea for months on end and had a limited diet, scurvy was a disease that tormented the crew. James Lind was a naval surgeon born in Edinburgh but based at Haslar and is best known for discovering the cure for scurvy. In 1747 he conducted an experiment on HMS Salisbury where a group of 12 sailors with scurvy were given various suggested cures including cider, but some were given oranges and lemons. Within a week the group that had citrus fruit could look after the others. James also found ways of preventing outbreaks of typhus on board ships. James Lind’s paper The Treatise of the Scurvy was published in 1753 but the Admiralty did not issue an order that all men be given citrus fruits until 1795.

RJ Mitchell

(1895-1937)

Reginald Joseph Mitchell was one of Britain’s most outstanding aeronautical engineers between the two world wars. Born in Staffordshire in 1916, he developed an early interest in aviation. He was based at Supermarine, a Southampton aviation company from 1916 to 1936 where he largely worked on racing seaplanes. The final model, Supermarine S.6B, won the Schneider Trophy in 1931. Mitchell was then asked to design a different model, the Supermarine 300, which eventually became the Spitfire. During World War II, the Spitfire was critical in the Battle of Britain and helped defend Britain from the enemy. Sadly, RJ Mitchell never got to see the Spitfire in action as he died of cancer in 1937 age 42.

Eugenia Cheng

(Present)

Eugenia Cheng was born in Hampshire and is a leading mathematician, concert pianist, and educator. She is currently based in Chicago as a scientist in residence at the University of the Art Institute of Chicago.

Eugenia’s interest in mathematics includes supporting people with maths phobia and in higher matter theory. One of the inspirational ways she uses to teach non-mathematicians about maths is to combine the subject with baking as in her book, How to Bake Pi.

Other publications include set theory and the principles of logic. She makes regular television appearances including the Late Show with Stephen Colbert and BBC Radio 4. Eugenia also writes the column Everyday Math for the Wall Street Journal.

Gilbert White

Gilbert White

(1720-1793)

It was always Gilbert White’s intention to inspire people about nature and the natural world. He was a clergyman, a writer, and a naturalist. Gilbert was the first person to identify the harvest mouse, the chiffchaff, and the noctule bat as definitive species from his home in Selborne. He also set out the first principles of ecology, observing how everything is connected from soil to earthworms to humans to birds. His work on nature inspired Charles Darwin as a young man and other naturalists including Charles Lyell. Gilbert White was also a pioneer of fieldwork and its use in scientific research. His book, The Natural History of Selborne, has never been out of print since he wrote it in 1789 and his house, which is dedicated to his life, is still open to visitors today.

James Robinson

James Robinson

(1813-1862)

A blue plaque at 14 Gower Street in London remembers James Robinson’s achievements today. On December 19, 1846, he became the first person to give a general anaesthetic in Britain when he administered ether to a patient having a tooth extraction. Before that, the public suffered excruciating pain when having surgical procedures. When Hampshire-born Robinson published A Treatise on the Inhalation of Ether in 1847 it became the first textbook on anaesthetics and influenced physician John Snow.

Robinson also worked to improve standards in dentistry and founded two dental journals to spread knowledge about research. He became Prince Albert’s personal dentist and was the inaugural president of the College of Dentists in 1856. James Robinson died from a gardening injury in 1862.

Tim Peake

(Present)

Major Tim Peake is the first European Space Agency Astronaut. He is also a retired Army Air Corps officer and author. Tim Peake trained at the Army Air Corps headquarters in Middle Wallop and completed a BSc (Hons) in Flight Dynamics and Evaluation at the University of Portsmouth. In 2009, he was selected to join the European Space Agency astronaut corps and flew to the International Space Station in 2015/16. While in space, he became the first British astronaut to complete a spacewalk. He also managed to send a picture of Hampshire from the ISS. Although Tim Peake has retired from space activities, he continues to give talks and inspire people.

Walter Flight

(1841-1885)

Walter Flight was born in Winchester in 1841 and went on to study chemistry in Germany. He returned to England in 1867 to study for a doctorate at the University of London. He became an assistant in the mineralogy department at the British Museum and took a special interest in the mineral and gaseous composition of meteorites.

Studying meteorites is important because they provide a time capsule of the formation of the solar system and can indicate how water forms and potential future threats to the planet. Walter Flight wrote several papers on meteorites, informing a growing interest in the solar system. He died in 1885..
