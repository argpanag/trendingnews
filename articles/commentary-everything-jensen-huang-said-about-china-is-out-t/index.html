Commentary: Everything Jensen Huang said about China is out the window

TheTools · 11.11.2026 02:43 · source · permalink

Commentary

The Nvidia CEO’s advocacy for selling AI chips to China could be previously explained by self-interest, but things have become a lot murkier, says Catherine Thorbecke for Bloomberg Opinion.

TOKYO: No American argued harder for selling artificial intelligence chips to China than Nvidia’s chief executive, Jensen Huang. His recurring advocacy previously could be easily explained by run-of-the-mill self-interest, with billions of dollars at stake. Now, that explanation has suddenly become a lot murkier.

Whether he was wrong or right in what he believed, everything that Huang has spent nearly every public appearance pitching in recent years has gone out the window after reports that he had an affair with a woman US intelligence officials suspect of being a Chinese spy.

Nvidia’s board of directors has said that it has no reason to believe that either national security or company secrets were compromised. Huang, who has admitted only being unfaithful to his wife, pledged to fully cooperate with the board’s independent investigation. 

But whatever the results of this probe, and however long it takes, it’s unlikely to easily restore trust.

NOT SO DIFFERENT FROM THE TECH BILLIONAIRES

Across Asia, Huang’s techno-optimism that AI shouldn’t be another zero-sum game was infectious. I remember catching a glimpse of him last year in Taipei, on the island where he was born, where seemingly every surprise appearance generated a Taylor Swift-like welcome. 

Even then, I wrote that it was hard not to worry about how easily he could be set up for a fall as his role evolved from geeky video games wonk to global AI kingpin.

Yet his dramatic plunge from grace since the weekend has been so shocking partly because the honey-trap story feels so depressingly low-tech. 

Despite being the boss of the world’s most valuable chipmaker, with his own net worth topping US$150 billion, Huang had cultivated an image that separated him from the rest of the billionaire tech class. 

He seemed unusually genuine when stopping by night markets and local pubs across Asia to grab a beer or bowl of noodles, and still talked about washing dishes at Denny’s as a teen in the US. Huang, 63, spoke warmly and often about his wife of 41 years, whom he met when she was his electrical engineering lab partner. 

The story goes that he eventually came up with the idea for Nvidia while at a Denny’s, before growing it to become the more-than-US$4 trillion behemoth it is today. And now this, as if the AI-wary American public needed another reminder to never put any tech executive on a pedestal. 

For people already angry about an out-of-touch broligarchy accumulating extraordinary wealth and political influence – and who delivered a stunning verdict on it in last week’s midterm elections – one of the industry’s last charismatic ambassadors suddenly looks a lot like the rest.

CHINESE AI STILL WANTS NVIDIA

Yet the personal scandal may be beside the point. Huang has spent years warning that China could win the AI race, or is just “nano-seconds” behind, and that export controls on cutting-edge chips have backfired. 

From that, he drew a message of cooperation to keep ahead, or maybe of holding your rivals closer. 

He praised Chinese breakthrough DeepSeek as a gift to the world. His most common refrain was that if America can’t sell chips to China, then homegrown champion Huawei – perhaps Nvidia’s only real long-term competition in the Chinese market – will be empowered to fill the gap and erode US dominance.

Does this argument collapse with his credibility? Or, in the long run, will a weakened Nvidia prove him right?

Huawei still cannot fill the Nvidia-shaped void in China’s frenetic AI market. China’s biggest tech companies clearly want to get their hands on its offerings. 

ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent recently placed more than US$35 billion worth of orders for Nvidia processors that comply with US export restrictions, despite Beijing’s desire for a switch to domestic alternatives. And even Huawei has acknowledged that it can’t keep up with demand, despite recent advances.

CHINA ACCESS WILL FACE NEW SCRUTINY

Huang might have had a shot at getting some more of Nvidia’s offerings back into the China market. The demand was there, and the Trump administration appeared a receptive audience to his go-to talking points and calls for loose regulation. 

But it’s likely that the newly elected Democratic Congress will be less so. And any attempt now by Nvidia to argue for greater access to China will face obvious new scrutiny, and likely outright derision.

A further crackdown from Washington on Nvidia chips going to China will undoubtedly cause near-term pain. Yet Huawei’s leadership and Beijing have also made clear that the long-term goal is self-sufficiency, and this determination likely hasn’t changed. 

The cruellest irony may be that Huang loses his influence in Washington just before China starts proving his point.
