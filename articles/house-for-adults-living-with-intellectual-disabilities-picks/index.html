House for adults living with intellectual disabilities picks up award

TheTools · 23.09.2026 05:43 · source · permalink

RNZ / News / Community

Link: https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/eyJhbGciOiJIUzI1NiJ9._g.pKhUMkRnoOZwX4iq2fhLAqba5Rz0jb3mINKRqASmxf0/community/1512419%2Fhouse-for-adults-living-with-intellectual-disabilities-picks-up-award

A house providing round-the-clock care for adults living with intellectual disabilities in Auckland is being hailed as a turn-around success, giving residents the power to make decisions many would take for granted.

Te Anua Nua Trust's manager has picked up an industry leadership award for bringing mana to two houses in Manurewa, where 10 people live with the support of carers.

It comes amid calls for more accessible housing and support for those with disabilities.

Monique Collins has lived under the wings of Te Anua Nua Trust for 16 years, where she has a studio unit looking out onto the garden.

"I call it like a whanau home, you know the bosses the staff like a whanau home and I'm so proud I've got that," she said.

When Len Moodley came on board as the chief executive about a year ago, he introduced a new way of living that increased the choice and control residents have in their lives.

"Len is kindness, very kindness he brings joy in this house for all of us and my flatmates," Collins said.

Elderly gentleman Ben Taite has lived there for two years.

"What brought me here is a disability...the work that I do is the gardening, I'm sort of like a caretaker who takes care of the flowers."

He said those who live and work there had become his whanau.

Moodley received an Enabling Good Lives leadership award for turning around the management of the homes, giving residents more say in everyday choices such as what to eat and what activities to do.

He ensured the new staff team received training and that the systems staff use to record health and safety and finances were up to date.

"There's been a 180 degree turnaround from the time I took over. I think at the time of coming here, my priority was to make sure that we're giving the best possible service, respect and value to our clients that are being looked after at Te Anua Nua."

He said the residents now choose their activities and can also choose to stay home if they don't feel like an outing.

"We see the difference, we see what it's done to their moods. It makes them more happy, they want to go out and enjoy life like every other person would want to do. This is the exciting bit for me, when I get to see that excitement."

He travelled with the residents and staff on a recent holiday they took to Gisborne and often receives cards of appreciation from the residents.

"The expression of how they give it to you, it's just mind blowing and that's my greatest joy."

But there's a wait list - Moodley said he gets regular calls from people needing supported housing for their disabled relatives.

Te Anua Nua Trust plans to rebuild one of the homes, expanding the number of rooms.

"There's always a shortage of housing and there's a big waiting list and so we're trying to address it slowly but surely so we can do it well," he said.

Te Anua Nua Trust resident Ben Taite.

RNZ / Amy Williams

Disability advocate Nick Ruane said recent official information shows very few social houses are accessible for disabled people.

Of the near 20,000 community housing dwellings, less than two-and-a-half percent are fully modified for accessibility, while about six percent are partially modified - for example with a ramp or a grab rail.

But more than half of those homes the accessibility is unknown.

Disability Connect provides information and advice including through its housing information service, linking advisors with disabled people and their families.

Its chief executive Mike Potter said he would like to see the government act faster to ensure more accessible housing is built.

"We absolutely need more of it, New Zealand is in a housing crisis particularly for disabled people. There's just not enough of the right type of housing," he said.

"For most people and their housing, you might think about affordablity, you might think of the number of bedrooms but for disability we're also thinking about accessibility."

A spokesperson for the Ministry for Cities, Environment, Regions and Transport said the government is undertaking a review of social housing to ensure support is focused on those with the greatest and most persistent housing needs.

"The review is considering how different barriers affect people's ability to access and maintain housing, including those with disabilities," they said.

Te Anua Nua Trust service manager Angel Liu Chan and chief executive Len Moodley,

RNZ / Amy Williams

Accessible housing is delivered by a range of social housing providers, including Kāinga Ora.

The spokesperson said around 15 percent of social housing places funded through Budgets 2024 and 2025 were identified by community housing providers as accessible or accessible ready, in July.

They said the ministry is involved in the New Zealand Disability Strategy, working towards the strategy's goal for housing.

"This is that disabled people and their whānau access affordable, safe, healthy, secure and accessible homes that support their independence and wellbeing," they said.

Looking ahead, Potter said homes like Te Anua Nua Trust's are needed.

"The demand is there because people are actually living longer, and the parents of people with intellectual disability are also living longer, and they're getting to the point where they're not able to to care for their children," Potter said.

"On the way is a virtual tsunami of a number of disabled people, particularly people with intellectual disability, who will need somewhere to live."

He said if there is not enough accessible housing or supported living homes, that leaves institutional type care which is more expensive.

The day RNZ visited Te Anua Nua Trust's Manurewa home, staff were preparing a hangi for dinner and the residents were sitting down for a cup of tea and milo.

Angel Liu Chan is the service manager and said the favourite part of her days is the morning, when she is greeted with smiles and hugs.

"Arriving on the site it's fairly early and the residents are just big smiles they greet you with the warmest hugs, they lift me up in spirit," she said.

"Just watching them flourish is what delights my heart...I wouldn't even call it a job it's actually a second home for me and my staff as well they've actually become my whanau."

Like others who work there, she wants to see more care homes available that enable disabled people to live good lives.
