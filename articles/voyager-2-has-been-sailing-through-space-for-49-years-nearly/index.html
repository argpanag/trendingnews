Voyager 2 has been sailing through space for 49 years—nearly eight of them beyond the heliopause—while losing roughly four watts of power annually. In July 2026, engineers completed a risky all-at-once systems swap nicknamed the “Big Bang,” buying its three remaining instruments at least one additional year of operation.

TheTools · 01.09.2026 06:43 · source · permalink

Voyager 2 has spent almost half a century being asked to do things its designers could not reasonably have planned for. It flew past all four giant planets, made the only close visits humanity has ever made to Uranus and Neptune, crossed the heliopause, and then kept reporting from interstellar space with instruments and computers built in the 1970s.

By 2026, however, the mission’s most basic resource had become critically scarce. Voyager 2’s radioisotope generators lose about four watts of electrical output each year. That is comparable to a small night-light, but on a spacecraft operating with almost no margin left, four watts can decide whether another scientific instrument must be shut down.

In July, engineers completed an unusually ambitious power and thermal reconfiguration nicknamed the “Big Bang.” NASA’s August 2026 mission update says the maneuver should keep Voyager 2’s three remaining science instruments operating for at least one additional year.

It was not a repair in the ordinary sense. Nothing new could be installed, and no failing power source could be replaced. Instead, the team found a way to rearrange which old devices were consuming electricity and producing heat, preserving the scientific payload without allowing critical parts of the spacecraft to become dangerously cold.

Why Voyager 2 is running out of power

Solar panels become less useful as a spacecraft travels away from the Sun. Voyager 2 instead carries three radioisotope thermoelectric generators, or RTGs. They convert heat from the radioactive decay of plutonium-238 into electricity.

The generators have no moving parts and have outlived the original planetary mission many times over. But radioactive decay is also why their output gradually declines. NASA says each Voyager spacecraft now loses roughly four watts of available power per year.

The mission team has responded by shutting down nonessential equipment and, eventually, science instruments. Voyager 2 launched with instruments for 10 different investigations. Its cameras completed their work decades ago. More recently, NASA turned off the plasma science instrument in September 2024 and shut down its low-energy charged particle instrument in March 2025.

Before the Big Bang reconfiguration, the cosmic ray subsystem was expected to be the next instrument turned off. The new savings postpone that decision.

What the “Big Bang” actually changed

NASA describes the Big Bang as the simultaneous shutdown of certain powered devices and their replacement with lower-power alternatives. The word “simultaneous” is the important part.

According to Scientific American’s detailed account of the operation, engineers switched off an old tape recorder and two heaters while switching on two different heaters and a propulsion-related device. The new combination reduced the spacecraft’s power demand by nearly 10 watts while maintaining a workable distribution of heat.

The team could not simply turn one item off, wait, and then activate the replacement. The spacecraft is so cold and its thermal margin so narrow that a poorly timed transition could allow propellant lines to freeze. If Voyager 2 lost the ability to use its thrusters to keep its antenna pointed toward Earth, the mission could end even if the science instruments and radio still had electricity.

NASA outlined this problem in an April 2026 Voyager update. The mission team needed to preserve enough warmth around vulnerable hardware while reducing total consumption. Voyager 2 was chosen to attempt the procedure first because it had slightly more available power than Voyager 1 and was closer to Earth.

Engineers ran power and thermal tests in May and June, then performed the full sequence in July. The reconfiguration was complete by the middle of the month, with Voyager 2 remaining stable and its three science instruments still operating.

The three instruments that remain

Voyager 2’s surviving science payload consists of the magnetometer, the plasma wave subsystem, and the cosmic ray subsystem. Together, they measure different parts of the environment outside the heliosphere.

The magnetometer measures the strength and direction of the local magnetic field. The plasma wave subsystem detects waves in ionized gas and can help researchers estimate plasma density. The cosmic ray subsystem measures high-energy particles arriving from the Sun, the galaxy, and beyond.

These are not substitute observations that can be made from a telescope near Earth. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are the only spacecraft ever to operate beyond the heliosphere. Their instruments sample the local interstellar medium directly, from two different trajectories.

That distinction is also why the heliopause matters. Voyager 2 crossed it on November 5, 2018, according to NASA’s analysis of the instrument data. The heliopause is the boundary where the outward influence of the solar wind gives way to the interstellar medium.

Crossing it did not mean Voyager 2 had left the solar system in every possible sense. The spacecraft remains under the Sun’s gravity and is nowhere near the distant outer edge associated with the Oort Cloud. It entered interstellar space by the mission’s operational definition: it moved beyond the Sun’s protective bubble of plasma and magnetic influence.

A command sent across interstellar distance

Voyager operations are slow by necessity. Commands travel through NASA’s Deep Space Network, and the spacecraft’s responses must cross the same distance back. In 2025, NASA put Voyager 2’s one-way communication time at about 19 and a half hours. A basic command-and-confirmation cycle therefore takes roughly 39 hours, even before engineers have interpreted the telemetry and decided what to do next.

The long delay is only part of the difficulty. There is no identical flight spare that perfectly reproduces the condition of a machine exposed to space since 1977. Some temperatures that matter now were never expected to become mission-limiting and cannot be measured directly. Every command has to account for decades of component aging and for the possibility that a routine action will not behave routinely anymore.

That is why gaining roughly 10 watts required months of analysis and testing. On Earth, the saving is trivial. At Voyager 2, it represents about two and a half years of the spacecraft’s current annual power decline, although NASA’s formal estimate is more conservative: enough to keep all three instruments operating for at least one extra year.

The spacecraft was never designed for this mission

Voyager 2 launched on August 20, 1977. It reached Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in 1981, then continued to Uranus in 1986 and Neptune in 1989. No other spacecraft has visited either of those last two planets.

The planetary encounters were the mission Voyager 2 was built to perform. Its interstellar work is an extraordinary extension made possible by a favorable trajectory, long-lived hardware, and decades of engineering improvisation.

The Big Bang is the latest example of that improvisation. It does not make Voyager 2 young again, and it cannot protect the spacecraft from every possible failure. An aging computer, transmitter, thruster, or other component could still end the mission before its power plan does.

What the maneuver does is remove the next scheduled deadline. Instead of sacrificing another scientific sense to keep the spacecraft alive, the team has extracted more useful time from systems that were already aboard at launch.

One more year where no replacement exists

An extra year can sound modest beside a 49-year mission. Scientifically, it is not. There is no replacement probe approaching the heliopause, and no other mission can occupy Voyager 2’s exact place in interstellar space. Every additional measurement extends a record that cannot presently be recreated.

Voyager 2 will continue losing power. More shutdown decisions will come, and eventually the science instruments and the spacecraft itself will fall silent. The July maneuver does not change that ending.

It changes the amount of interstellar space Voyager 2 can measure before the ending arrives. For a mission working four watts at a time, that is a substantial victory.
