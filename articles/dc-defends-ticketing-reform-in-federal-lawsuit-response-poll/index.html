DC Defends Ticketing Reform In Federal Lawsuit Response - Pollstar News

TheTools · 12.11.2026 16:59 · source · permalink
  • J.R. Lind
  • 8:59 am, Thursday, 11/12/2026
  • News

The government of the District of Columbia is defending the legality and constitutionality of its new ticketing reform package in response to two federal lawsuits.

The District is being sued in federal court by a group of online resellers represented by the Ticket Policy Forum seeking to enjoin the enforcement of the RESALE Act, set to go into in effect in 2027, which caps resale at 10% above face value and caps resale fees at 10% of face value. A separate suit by another group of resellers is challenging only the resale cap.

The TPF suit says that because the act could theoretically regulate the sale of a ticket between a buyer and seller who are in different states and not in the District at all — for example, a Virginian might list for sale a ticket for a show at DC’s Capital One Arena that is ultimately bought by someone in Maryland — it violates the Commerce Clause, which leaves the regulation of interstate commerce to Congress rather than local jurisdictions. Further, it says that it can be impossible to ascertain the original face value of certain tickets, making a 10% markup (or any markup) impossible to determine. The suit also claims a First Amendment violation because it compels disclosure of the original price, which may be information the reseller has no knowledge of.

In its 60-page response, the District says the resellers are simply at odds with the policy decision and using constitutional arguments as window dressing.

“Striking out with the democratic process, Plaintiffs dressed their policy objections up in constitutional garb and turned to this Court for a judicial veto,” the filing states.

One concern raised by the resellers is that the law doesn’t cap primary pricing and places no limits on fees charged by primary ticketers, creating a competitive disadvantage. The District counters its under no marketplace obligation to create matching regulations.

When hard caps are mooted — or implemented — secondary sellers often raise the objection that it can often be impossible to determine face value without access to original transaction data and because dynamically priced or bundled tickets may well not reflect “true” face value. DC officials said the District would not hold a reseller liable as long as they were relying on a price they reasonably believed was accurate. And in any case, “if regulated parties cannot satisfy those conditions, then they simply can forgo the resale.”

The judge in the case is weighing a preliminary injunction to stop the law from coming into effect Jan. 1. The TFP is asking for a ruling by Dec. 10 to allow for an appeal before the new year, if necessary.
