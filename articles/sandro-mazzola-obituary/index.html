Sandro Mazzola obituary

TheTools · 24.09.2026 15:17 · source · permalink

Outstanding footballer who won two European Cups with Internazionale and a World Cup runners-up medal with Italy

The Italian footballer Sandro Mazzola, who has died aged 83, was a supremely gifted forward who won two European Cups and four Serie A titles during 17 years with his only club, Internazionale of Milan. With Italy’s national side he won the European Championship in 1968 and played in the 4-1 defeat by Pelé’s Brazil in the final of the 1970 World Cup.

He was six years old when his father, Valentino Mazzola, the captain of an invincible Torino team and of Italy, died aged 30, along with the rest of the team, when their chartered plane, returning in bad weather from a friendly fixture in Lisbon in May 1949, crashed into the Superga hill overlooking Turin, killing everyone on board. The demise of Il Grande Torino, as they were known, traumatized the nation.

When Sandro emerged from that tragedy to win his first European Cup in 1964, defeating the five-times champions Real Madrid 3-1 in the final, he scored two of Internazionale’s goals. In a recent interview he recalled that afterwards the veteran Argentinian forward Alfredo Di Stefano, the greatest of Madrid’s stars, gave him his shirt while telling him: “You are worthy of your father.”

A year later, during a visit by the Internazionale squad to the famous Capuchin friar Padre Pio at the monastery of San Giovanni Rotondo in south-east Italy, Mazzola stayed behind for a personal audience with the priest. He asked for an absolution, denied by his own parish priest, for the sin of having vowed during his youth that he was willing to die young, like his father, if he could also become a champion. It was promptly granted.

Born in Turin, Sandro found his early life complicated by his father’s separation from his mother, Emilia (nee Rinaldi), three years before the fatal plane crash. After the tragedy he was kidnapped by Valentino’s new partner and kept in a barn before being found and returned to his mother and his younger brother, Ferruccio.

The family moved to Milan, where a new life of comparative poverty was relieved when one of Valentino’s former teammates took the boys to Internazionale, where Angelo Moratti, the owner, made them club mascots. They sat near the dugout at every home game, wearing the club’s blue and black stripes, and were paid a match fee.

Eventually the pair joined Internazionale’s academy. Sandro was 18 when he made his first-team debut in 1961, a 9-1 defeat by Juventus after the Argentinian head coach, Helenio Herrera, had responded to perceived injustices by picking the club’s youth team en bloc. Mazzola scored Inter’s only goal, from the penalty spot, having taken school exams that morning.

It was the first of 158 goals in 565 appearances as he became a key member of a team that also included the Spanish playmaker Luis Suárez, the Brazilian winger Jair, the sweeper Armando Picchi and the two long-serving full-backs, Giacinto Facchetti and Tarcisio Burgnich, who would become his colleagues in the national team.

Using Herrera’s espousal of the catenaccio (door-bolt) defensive formation as a platform for swift counterattacks, Internazionale were soon dominating Serie A and the European Cup, in which they followed the initial victory over Real Madrid with a win against Benfica 12 months later and a losing appearance in the 1967 final against Celtic. By then the team had become known as Il Grande Inter, and Mazzola had resisted approaches from Juventus’s president, Gianni Agnelli, the boss of Fiat, who offered to double his salary and give him a Fiat dealership.

Mazzola’s name will forever be linked with that of his contemporary Gianni Rivera, who played a similar role with Inter’s local rivals, AC Milan. In the view of Italy’s manager, Ferruccio Valcareggi, the two playmakers could not be accommodated within the same national team. They had played together when Italy were eliminated in humiliating fashion by North Korea at the 1966 World Cup, when Valcareggi was Edmondo Fabbri’s assistant, and Rivera was absent with an injury two years later when the Azzurri, now with Valcareggi in control and inspired by Mazzola, beat Yugoslavia 2-0 to take the European title.

Although their functions were similar, the pair differed in every other way. Rivera was a stylishly creative presence on the pitch and a regular star on the cover of glossy magazines; Mazzola, known as Il Baffo in acknowledgement of the moustache he always wore, was a more versatile, pragmatic and consistent operator, and lived quietly with his young family.

To resolve the dilemma in time for the 1970 World Cup, Valcareggi came up with something called La staffetta – the relay. Mazzola would start the match, while Rivera would replace him at half-time. This much-discussed compromise saw Italy to the final but came unstuck against Brazil. Valcareggi delayed the switch and his team were already 3-1 down when, with only six minutes of the 90 remaining, Rivera was finally allowed to take Mazzola’s place, with no time to make an impact.

Four years later Valcareggi put them both in the team for the World Cup in Germany but appeared to prove his earlier reservations correct when the ageing Azzurri failed to make it beyond the group stage.

Mazzola won 70 international caps, scoring 22 goals, to Rivera’s figures of 60 and 14. In the circumstances, it was generally assumed that they could hardly be friends. But in an interview with the Gazzetta dello Sport a month before his death, Mazzola was emphatic in his description of “a healthy rivalry and immense respect” between the two.

He retired as a player in 1977, having captained Internazionale for seven seasons, and became an executive at the club, responsible for signing stars such as the Brazilian striker Ronaldo and the Argentinian defender Javier Zanetti. From 2000 to 2003 he served as sporting director at Torino. He also worked as a broadcaster for RAI, the national TV network, commentating on Italy’s World Cup wins in 1982 and 2006.

He is survived by his wife, Graziella (nee Galante), and their children, Ilaria, Valentina, Sandro and Paolo.
