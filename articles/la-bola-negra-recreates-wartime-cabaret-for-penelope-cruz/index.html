La Bola Negra Recreates Wartime Cabaret for Penelope Cruz

TheTools · 28.11.2026 00:04 · source · permalink

A ruined cinema in Spain provides the setting for one of Penelope Cruz's standout performances in La Bola Negra, where she portrays cabaret singer Nené Romero entertaining Nationalist troops during the Spanish Civil War in 1937. The sequence combines historical research, theatrical design and elaborate costumes to recreate a moment of entertainment amid wartime violence.

Screen Daily explored the production process in an article published November 25, 2026, speaking with production designer Roger Bellés, costume designer Ana Lopez Cobos and makeup designer Marilo Osuna. The three department heads have previously collaborated with directors Javier Calvo and Javier Ambrossi, collectively known as Los Javis.

The film moves between three periods: 1932, 1937 and 2017. Inspired by the life and work of queer poet and playwright Federico Garcia Lorca, the production also draws on his unfinished play, which shares the film's title.

Bellés explained that the filmmakers wanted the performance venue to contrast with the surrounding violence.

“a theatre in ruins, with the idea her character brings light and beauty in the context of war” was how Bellés described the setting.

The production team examined approximately 40 potential locations before selecting an abandoned cinema in Sigüenza, approximately 130 kilometers northeast of Madrid. The building had closed during the 1950s and offered the deteriorated appearance required for the scene.

Bellés also acknowledged the practical challenges of working with a prominent international performer while operating within the film's overall budget.

“Choosing the right setting was key, but we were also aware Penelope has worked in individual sets that cost what our entire film cost. We knew we couldn’t give her a Pirates Of The Caribbean soundstage, but we could offer something special,” Bellés said.

His professional relationship with Los Javis began while the directors were developing their first film.

“I met Los Javis when they were preparing their first film Holy Camp! [in 2017],” Bellés explained. “I was drawn by their creativity and their boundless energy. I felt like a kid running away with a travelling circus company.”

Lopez Cobos had known the directors even longer, recalling their early days working in Madrid's Lara theatre.

“performing in the hall of the Lara theatre in Madrid, and Ambrossi was doing shifts as a waiter” was how Lopez Cobos described that period.

She said their continuing creative partnership requires a fresh approach to every production.

“I have worked with them ever since, but every project is different. They’re always exploring the new so you’re never in the comfort zone, in the best possible sense,” Lopez Cobos said.

Preparing the cinema required industrial cleaning and structural reinforcement. The team also recreated a painted backdrop based on an authentic 1940s theatre.

“We restored it to adapt it to our needs,” Bellés said.

Cruz embraced the location despite its neglected condition, according to the production designer.

“And Penelope was totally game, she loved the atmosphere and the details. I was worried we would have missed a pigeon dropping here or there, but she never complained,” Bellés said with a laugh.

Bellés also explained how the production's visual identity changes across its three historical periods.

“In the three timelines that intertwine in the film, the mood and the filmmaking style change, so the references we used were different too,” he said.

Although the wartime storyline relied heavily on archival photographs, Cruz's cabaret sequence required a different visual approach.

“So here, people will see echoes of paintings by Egon Schiele, Toulouse-Lautrec, Bob Fosse’s Cabaret, Fassbinder,” Bellés said.

The production combined those artistic influences with historical research into performances organized for Nationalist troops during the Spanish Civil War.

“But Los Javis don’t give references for you to replicate anything. It’s just to convey the mood they’re looking for. They are very precise but they don’t micromanage,” Bellés explained.

A second performance features Cruz singing on top of an authentic military vehicle rather than a studio replica.

“on a real Panzer 1 we got from a Spanish collector,” Bellés said, describing the tank used in the sequence.

The vehicle came from a shipment that Nazi Germany had sent to the right-wing army during the Spanish Civil War. A Spanish collector personally drove the tank during filming.

Bellés emphasized the production's effort to combine authentic historical objects with cinematic storytelling.

“This was part of the shipment Hitler’s Germany sent to the right-wing army during the Spanish Civil War. The collector drove it himself. We tried to get a sense of the real throughout the film, and this is a real tank, a real theatre but a magical moment too, truth amid the great lie that is cinema,” Bellés said.

Costume designer Lopez Cobos conducted additional research into cuplé performers, female entertainers associated with a Spanish musical tradition popular during the late 19th and 20th centuries. Their performances combined singing, comedy and provocative theatrical elements.

Makeup designer Osuna described the historical significance of the women whose artistic legacy inspired the character.

“[They were] women who were courageous, provocative and strong, who advocated for women’s rights and took to the stage at a moment where everything was controlled by men,” Osuna explained.

Lopez Cobos based Cruz's costume on historical French fashion influences and the structured silhouettes associated with earlier theatrical performers.

“The costumes worn by these singers were often inspired by 19th-century French fashion – more structured looks,” Lopez Cobos said.

The design incorporated a corset, a heart-shaped neckline, a mermaid-style silhouette and a pearly bolero jacket. Cruz also expressed a preference for the corset-based design.

“And Penelope was keen to use a corset so we went for a French-style outline. The initial inspiration was a photo of a Folies Bergère artist, but we added the heart-shape décolleté, the siren [fish] tail, and a pearly bolero jacket in a Goya-esque touch,” Lopez Cobos explained.

The costume department also considered a darker alternative inspired by Hollywood fashion from the 1920s.

“a black gauzy dress inspired by 1920s Hollywood” was another option considered during the design process.

However, Cruz preferred the white outfit, which cinematographer Gris Jordana also appreciated because of its visual contrast against the dark interior of the abandoned cinema.

Lopez Cobos connected the costume's color to the scene's symbolic meaning.

“a fantasy glimpse in the context of the horrors of war,” Lopez Cobos explained.

A white feather boa completed the ensemble after being introduced during the final stages of costume preparation.

“It was a last-minute addition, but it looked so great that we went for it,” Lopez Cobos revealed.

La Bola Negra premiered in the Cannes Competition and was subsequently acquired by Netflix for the US market and Altitude for the UK market.
