Explore the Night Sky at Duke’s Teaching Observatory | Duke Today

TheTools · 10.11.2026 00:06 · source · permalink

Get an up-close look at the moon, planets and distant galaxies through the observatory’s telescopes

In the darkness of a grassy clearing in Duke Forest, the sky looks different. Speckled dots of bright light stretch in every direction, some faint and others sharp. Every so often, a streak of light crosses overhead.

On a spring evening, Nate Nussbaum and his wife, Jackie Cohen, stood in line for a turn peering through one of three telescopes pointed above. Their three young kids had been learning about astronomy in school, and earlier that same night, NASA’s Artemis II splashed down from its voyage around the moon.

“And then to come here and be able to look at the stars is pretty cool,” Nussbaum said.

Each semester, the Duke Teaching Observatory offers free open houses for anyone interested in seeing and learning about stars, planets, the moon or astronomy. The final open houses of 2026 are Nov. 14 and Dec. 5.

It began in 2002 as a teaching tool for students and has grown into a draw for families, hobbyists and curious first-timers eager to learn.

The “observatory” is an open field off Cornwallis Road in Duke Forest. A gravel lane on the southwest side of the road is fenced off with a wire if you arrive too early, a small sign on a wooden post near the entrance reads, “John Couch Farm.”

Everyone wonders if they’re in the right place the first time they visit. If there’s a large shed to your immediate right after you turn into the gravel lane, you are.

Down the hill, another shed houses five Meade LX200 GPS Schmidt-Cassegrain telescopes used for each event.

Yuriy Bomze, a Lab Administrator in the Department of Physics who helps manage the telescopes along with volunteers from the student-run Stargazing Devils Astronomy Club, said the crowd of maybe 100 to 150 people on April 10, the day Artemis II returned to Earth, was larger than average.

Telescopes were aimed at Jupiter, a bright, white circle, where three of its four moons were also visible through the lens; Uranus; and at the Orion nebula, a cluster of stars inside a cloud of gas and dust. Depending on the time of year, meteor showers, galaxies and details of the surface of the moon also can be viewed – though never a full moon. Open houses are scheduled when there is a little moonlight, otherwise light pollution would prevent viewing.

“The moon is very beautiful, though we’re kind of used to seeing it almost every day,” Bomze said. “But when you look through a telescope and you see the line between day and night, you see shadows from mountains and craters. It’s three-dimensional – it’s so much more.”
