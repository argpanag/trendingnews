Russia builds giant radar on Nato’s eastern flank

TheTools · 20.10.2026 14:54 · source · permalink

Satellite images provide evidence that Kaliningrad, the Kremlin’s semi-exclave on the Baltic Sea, is being built up into a hub for electronic warfare systems

Russia is building a gigantic radar system in the heart of Nato’s eastern flank at Kaliningrad, its semi-exclave on the Baltic Sea, according to satellite images.

The complex at Gvardeysk, about 20 miles from the Polish border and 40 miles from Lithuania, consists of three radiating arms of antenna masts, each roughly 500 metres long.

The structures appear to be part of an over-the-horizon (OTH) radar that could theoretically detect missiles, warships and aircraft at distances of between 550 and 2,000 miles, depending on its specifications — comfortably enough to cover Britain and the rest of Europe’s northern and western periphery.

The photographs were taken by Iceye, a Finnish–Polish earth observation satellite company, and first published by the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung newspaper.

The images are the latest evidence that Kaliningrad is being built up into a hub for sophisticated military sensors and electronic warfare arrays.

The territory, which is more or less due north of Warsaw, was annexed to Russia by Stalin at the end of the Second World War and retained by Moscow as a foothold in Europe after the break-up of the Soviet Union. It is estimated to host enough storage facilities and delivery systems for up to a hundred nuclear warheads. High-ranking sources in Poland and Lithuania believe Russia has already begun deploying warheads to Kaliningrad.

Over the past four years the area has become a source of continuous GPS interference that has played havoc with navigation for shipping and aircraft across a significant swathe of northeastern Europe. Lithuania estimates that Russia has built at least 36 “spoofing” antennae in the region.

An OTH radar on the scale seen at Gvardeysk could generate wavelengths of more than 10 metres, tens of times longer than standard long-range air surveillance radar. These are bounced off the upper atmosphere at high frequency to detect objects beyond line-of-sight, including stealth aircraft. 

The European Nato allies maintain a handful of systems with similar capabilities, including the British Pluto I and II OTH radars at RAF Akrotiri on Cyprus and the French comparably sized Nostradamus facility near Dreux, 50 miles west of Paris.
