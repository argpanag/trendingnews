8 Best Thriller Shows for Harlan Coben Fans (That Aren't Based on His Books)

Harlan Coben has made an entire career out of proving why nobody should answer an unexpected phone call, visit their hometown, or dig through old family secrets. He has turned Netflix into his own personal thriller factory, giving us shows that have a normal-looking family, a life-changing event, and a small town that is harboring a decade's worth of tea. Between Fool Me Once, Stay Close, The Innocent, and Missing You, I've come to learn the formula by heart.

Once you've burned through the entire Coben-verse, though, the well runs dry quickly. So I searched for TV shows that scratch the same itch without his name on the poster. Think missing people, suburban families with skeletons in their closets, and a plot that makes it impossible to stop at three episodes. You just have to binge the entire series in one sitting. While the eight TV shows I've chosen for this list aren't based on Harlan Coben's books, they all share the same ingredients that make his thrillers so addictive.

Why You Should Trust Me: I write about crime dramas, mystery series, and binge-worthy thrillers for MovieWeb on a regular basis, and I've watched the entire Coben Netflix catalog more than once, including the international versions. I picked these eight shows because they structurally overlap with his work, not just because "this show has a twist." And if I'm being honest, at this point, every show has a twist.

Honorable Mentions

The Sinner (2017 – 2021): Bill Pullman plays a detective drawn to a crime that makes no sense at all, as a seemingly ordinary woman commits a shocking act of violence with no memory why. Each season digs into a single suspect's buried past instead of a whodunit. The exploration of "look how much damage one hidden trauma can do" is pure Coben.

All Her Fault (2025): Sarah Snook stars in this eight-episode mystery series about a mother whose young son disappears after a playdate, setting off a nightmare of suspicion and buried secrets. If the combination of domestic normalcy and sudden catastrophe is your favorite part of Fool Me Once or Missing You, this one gets very close.

And Then There Were None (2015): In this series, 10 strangers arrive on an isolated island and are killed one by one. This storyline is admittedly a little less "someone from my past has returned with a terrible secret" and more "absolutely nobody should have accepted this invitation." Still, the paranoia, shifting alliances, and desperate search for answers make it an easy recommendation for anyone who has read Coben's books and/or seen the Netflix adaptations.

'Pretty Little Liars' (2010 – 2017)

Proof the Coben Formula Works For Teenagers Too

On paper, Pretty Little Liars and a Harlan Coben thriller don't have a lot in common. One is a seven-season teen mystery involving four friends, a missing girl, and an anonymous tormentor known as "A." The other usually begins with an adult discovering that their spouse, sibling, or childhood best friend has apparently been lying to them for the last 20 years. In practice? They're close cousins.

After Alison DiLaurentis disappears, her friends Aria, Spencer, Hanna, and Emily start receiving messages from someone who knows every secret they thought disappeared with her. Then Alison's body is found, the messages continue, and Pretty Little Liars spends seven seasons proving that everyone in Rosewood should have been investigated at some point. Coben's shows are obsessed with secrets, too. Every character is hiding something, every new answer raises another question, and the mystery keeps expanding beyond what seemed possible.

'Behind Her Eyes' (2021)

Everyone in This Show Is Lying to Someone

If there's one thing Harlan Coben followers understand, it's that a thriller should never reveal its hand too early. Behind Her Eyes understands this, and over six episodes, it makes you question whether you understood anything at all. The seires is about sngle mother Louise (Simona Brown) who starts an affair with her new boss, David (Tom Bateman). She befriends his wife, Adele (Eve Hewson), without either of them knowing about the other.

All three characters keep secrets from each other, and the finale reframes the entire premise. The show has the same compulsive structure that makes Coben adaptations so easy to binge in a weekend. It gives you multiple characters with questionable motives, constantly shifts your understanding of who deserves sympathy, and reveals just enough information to make you think you've figured everything out. Trust me, you haven't.

'Defending Jacob' (2020)

When the Suspect Is Your Own Child

In Defending Jacob, Chris Evans does something Steve Rogers never had to worry about: wonder whether his teenage son might be a murderer. Based on William Landay's bestselling novel, the Apple TV miniseries follows Andy Barber (Evans), an assistant district attorney whose life unravals after a teenage boy is murdered in his Massachusetts town. When the evidence starts pointing toward Andy's son, Jacob (Jaeden Martell), the case stops being a professional responsibility and starts terrifying him.

The central question is simple and asks whether Jacob committed the crime. Naturally, though, the series doesn't make the answer simple at all. Defending Jacob is probably the closest match to Coben's storytelling when it comes to emotional stakes. Coben regularly takes an ordinary family and gives them something devastating to tackle. This one also lets the uncertainty around Jacob's innocence drive the narrative. For those who like domestic thrillers, this is an essential watch.

'The Undoing' (2020)

The Perfect Life Is Usually the First Thing to Go

A wealthy Manhattan therapist, a devoted husband, a beautiful home, a private-school family, and no reason to suspect anything could go wrong. Of course, since The Undoing is on this list, everything does go wrong. The series follows Grace Fraser (Nicole Kidman), whose carefully constructed life collapses after a brutal murder rocks her social circle and her husband, Jonathan (Hugh Grant), suddenly disappears. What follows is a very Coben-esque exercise in asking how much Grace knew about her own marriage.

Based on Jean Hanff Korelitz's novel You Should Have Known, the HBO miniseries thrives on the same kind of domestic paranoia that fuels many of Coben's best stories. Grace is forced to reconsider her marriage, Jonathan's past, and every explanation she has accepted without question. The more she learns, the less stable her version of reality becomes. Hugh Grant also plays against type here, and it works because you're so used to his charm that you refuse to see that he's covering a much darker truth.

'Big Little Lies' (2017 – 2019)

Wealthy Suburbs, Beautiful Houses, Terrible Secrets

The women in Monterey have beautiful homes, successful families, and enough buried resentment to power the entire California coastline. Told mostly in flashback from the night of a murder at a school fundraiser, Big Little Lies follows these women as their friendships, marriages, and parenting choices are revealed as tangled up in the case.

The ensemble cast is unfairly great, including Reese Witherspoon, Nicole Kidman, and Shailene Woodley. However, the show's biggest strength is how efficiently it turns everyday interactions into potential clues. A disagreement at school, an uncomfortable conversation between spouses, and an old grudge suddenly takes on much larger significance. Like many of Coben's thrillers, this one also understands that a mystery becomes much more compelling when everyone involved has something to hide. The murder is what starts this story, but the secrets keep it moving.

'His & Hers' (2026)

A Marriage Investigating Its Own Murder Case

If you've already watched every recent Harlan Coben adaptation, His & Hers offers something familiar yet fresh. Based on Alice Feeney's novel, the Netflix limited series follows Anna Andrews (Tessa Thompson), a reporter who took a career break after a personal collapse, and is pulled back into public life when a murder happens in her Georgia hometown. Her estranged husband, Detective Jack Harper (Jon Bernthal), is running the official investigation.

The story unfolds through their separate viewpoints, allowing us to see the same case from two different and increasingly suspicious angles. Those familiar with Coben's work know that perspective is never neutral in a thriller. The person telling you the story may be mistaken, may withhold something, or may outright lie to you. His & Hers mines that idea throughout.

'Sharp Objects' (2018)

Going Home Can Be the Worst Possible Idea

Harlan Coben characters have a terrible habit of returning to places they should have left alone. Sharp Objects turns that idea into an eight-episode nightmare. Amy Adams stars as Camille Preaker, a Chicago journalist sent back to her small Missouri hometown to cover the murder of two young girls.

It should be a straightforward assignment. Unfortunately, Camille hasn't made peace with Wind Gap, or with her complicated mother, Adora (Patricia Clarkson), and the memories she's spent years trying to outrun. The murder investigations are gripping, but Sharp Objects is ultimately interested in what happens when the place that made you who you are is also the place that damaged you.

'Broadchurch' (2013 – 2017)

A Murder Mystery Where Everyone Knows Everyone

Broadchurch is a must-watch and focuses on what happens when an 11-year-old boy is found dead on a beach in the small English town of Broadchurch. Since the entire community becomes part of the investigation, that alone should make it an easy sell for those who love Coben's work. David Tennant and Olivia Colman lead the series as detectives Alec Hardy and Ellie Miller, who investigate the murder while navigating a town where everyone knew the victim. More importantly, it's also a place where everyone has a reason to lie.

Broadchurch shares one of Coben's favorite storytelling tricks. It takes an unassuming community and slowly reveals everything happening beneath the surface. As the investigation expands, marriages come under scrutiny, friendships fracture, old resentments resurface, and people who initially appear unrelated to the crime suddenly become important. If you love Coben because his thrillers combine plot twists with personal consequences, watch this one.
