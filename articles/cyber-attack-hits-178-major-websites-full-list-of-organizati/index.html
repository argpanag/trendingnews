Cyber attack hits 178 major websites: Full list of organizations

TheTools · 18.11.2026 03:27 · source · permalink

Hackers have planted malicious code on some of the world's most popular websites, potentially exposing visitors to the theft of their personal and financial information, cybersecurity experts have warned.

Security researcher Scott Helme, founder of Report URI, told Newsweek in an email Tuesday that researchers found at least 188 infected Optimizely projects across 178 organizations, including the BBC, CNN, Zoom, HSBC, Salesforce and Equifax.

Helme estimated that the malicious code may have been served during roughly 34 million page visits, with a broad possible range of 6 million to 150 million. He added: “That counts visits that loaded the code, not people who lost money or data. The actual harm is far smaller.”

According to Helme, the code could steal payment-card details, swap cryptocurrency wallet addresses, capture cookies and screenshots and redirect older iPhones and iPads to an exploit kit. Researchers could see about $7,000 in cryptocurrency reach wallets linked to the attacker, he said, although they could not determine how many card numbers may have been stolen.

“This is a supply-chain attack: Compromise one trusted vendor, and its code runs on every customer's website at once,” Helme said, adding that researchers had seen “no evidence of AI involvement.”

The attack may have affected websites including Zoom, CNN, HSBC, the BBC, IKEA, Salesforce, Equifax, Fox News, Alaska Airlines and GitLab, according to researchers at cybersecurity firm Report URI who identified the attack on Monday. A full list is provided below.

Hackers injected malicious JavaScript, a common computer language, into scripts hosted by Optimizely, a company that provides software allowing businesses to test different versions of websites, the researchers said.

Optimizely said in a status update on its website on Tuesday that "an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability" in its Experimentation platform. "We've fixed the vulnerability and removed the injected code from affected projects," it said.

"The most important take-away is not that malicious code appeared on websites associated with 178 major organizations. It is that a vulnerability in one shared technology provider can potentially expose people across otherwise unrelated businesses and industries to consequences they never expected or were prepared for," John Walsh, Field CTO at IGEL told Newsweek in an email Tuesday.

Walsh said the "potential exposure could extend into the millions and [tens] of millions given the combined reach of the organizations identified. But we must distinguish potential exposure, execution of malicious code, and confirmed compromise."

"The 178 organizations represent potentially affected websites, not 178 confirmed corporate breaches or a known number of victims," he said. "A defensible victim estimate requires traffic during the exposure window, the specific pages and scripts involved, and forensic evidence of successful theft."

Peter Lowe, threat research lead at ZeroFox, explained the attack as "a third-party supply chain attack."

"The sites affected weren’t breached in the usual sense at all—their own code and infrastructure were fine, they were loading JavaScript from a provider they trusted, and that trust is what got exploited," Lowe told Newsweek on Tuesday. "There's been a lot of discussion lately about offensive AI frameworks and I wouldn't be surprised to find out that this is an example attack."

Consumer credit reporting company Equifax told Newsweek that it was aware of the reports and had "immediately investigated this issue and ceased our use of Optimizely."

"We have not identified evidence of any compromise to our systems or impact to our data," it added.

Furniture retailer IKEA told Newsweek that it was aware of the reports and investigating "to understand any potential impact on our digital services and customers."

Among the information the malicious code was designed to steal were browser session cookies, small pieces of data websites use to remember that a user has already logged in.

If an attacker obtains a valid session cookie, they may be able to hijack that active session and access an account as the victim without entering the victim’s password again.

The risk depends on the website’s security controls and the type of cookie stolen. The discovery of cookie-stealing code does not by itself establish that every visitor’s session was compromised.

Lowe said the measurable financial impact appears limited so far, with only a few thousand dollars traced to cryptocurrency wallets linked to the attack. But that does not capture other potential losses, including stolen payment-card details, browser cookies and attacks targeting unpatched iPhones.

“Money is only the part we can count,” Lowe said. “Non-HttpOnly cookies, card details typed into sites’ own forms rather than a hosted payment frame and the iOS exploit chains served to anyone on an unpatched iPhone are all impacts nobody can measure from the outside.”

Salesforce Disables Optimizely Scripts as Investigation Continues

Salesforce said it is investigating unauthorized modifications to Optimizely scripts used on certain company websites and has disabled the third-party scripts as a precaution.

“Salesforce is investigating unauthorized modifications to scripts provided by Optimizely and used on certain Salesforce websites,” the company said in a security advisory. “The modified scripts could load malicious code in visitors' browsers.”

Salesforce said Optimizely scripts had been disabled across its preview and production instances while the investigation continued.

The company said it would notify affected customers “without undue delay” if it identifies unauthorized access to their data.

The incident involved third-party Optimizely code loaded by certain Salesforce websites. Optimizely has said an unauthorized party exploited a vulnerability in its Experimentation platform and used it to add malicious JavaScript to some customer projects.

Optimizely said it blocked the vulnerable path, deployed a fix and removed the injected code from affected projects. The company also said it ended active sessions, revoked tokens and reset access for affected user accounts.

Security researchers at Report URI said they examined 1,611 Optimizely scripts and found 188 infected scripts associated with 178 organizations. Researchers said the malicious code was designed to swap cryptocurrency wallet addresses, skim payment-card information, steal cookies and screenshots and direct some iPhone and iPad users toward iOS exploit infrastructure.

"We have built extraordinary convenience by connecting systems, applications, suppliers, and services," Walsh said. "But those connections create dependencies in which the compromise of one component can now affect hundreds of organizations simultaneously. The question is whether our digital infrastructure is designed to contain an attack when something inevitably goes wrong."

How Did the Cyber Attack Happen?

Rather than hacking into 178 organizations separately, the attackers exploited a vulnerability in Optimizely's website testing service, which is used across numerous major websites, Report URI said.

By tampering with the software, the hackers were able to place malicious code on websites belonging to organizations that relied on it, meaning visitors could encounter the software simply by opening an affected website.

Researchers found that the malicious code was designed to carry out several types of theft. One method involved changing cryptocurrency wallet addresses copied by users, potentially sending payments to the hackers instead of intended recipients.

Helme said that this isn't a new attack. He said: “The same crypto wallet has been receiving and cashing out funds since March 2026, so this is an established operator, not a newcomer.”

“The same infrastructure was injected through at least three other third-party script vendors in the days before Optimizely," he added.

The software could also attempt to steal credit card details, access information stored in browsers and capture screenshots.

Some of the code targeted iPhones and iPads, attempting to exploit weaknesses in older versions of Apple's operating systems, Report URI said.

Report URI identified 188 affected website scripts across 178 organizations.

The researchers also traced approximately $7,040 in cryptocurrency payments to wallets linked to the attack on November 16, although the full financial impact is unknown.

What Should I Do Now?

Anyone who visited an affected website on November 16 should be aware of the potential risks, particularly if they entered payment details or made cryptocurrency transfers.

“Anyone logged in to an affected site: Log out of all sessions and change your password," Helme urged.

Walsh said that consumers should avoid unfamiliar links but noted that it "is insufficient when malicious code arrives through a trusted website’s technology supply chain."

"Visiting a familiar, legitimate website does not guarantee that every piece of software executing in a browser is trustworthy," Walsh said. "Researchers reported malicious JavaScript designed to target payment information, cryptocurrency transactions, and certain mobile devices. Consumers could potentially be exposed without clicking a suspicious email or visiting an obviously fraudulent website.

"Consumers should monitor financial accounts, enable transaction alerts, employ multifactor authentication, keep browsers and mobile operating systems updated, and scrutinize unexpected payment changes. Anyone who submitted sensitive payment information during the exposure window on an affected site should follow that organization’s guidance."

However, simply opening a website does not mean information was stolen.

What you can do is:

  • Update your devices: Make sure your iPhone, iPad and other devices are running the latest available security updates.
  • Check your bank accounts: Look for payments you do not recognize, particularly if you entered card details on an affected website.
  • Review cryptocurrency transfers: If you sent cryptocurrency, check that it went to the correct wallet address.
  • Watch for suspicious account activity: Be alert to unexpected login notifications or changes to your online accounts.
  • Contact your bank if necessary: If you notice an unauthorized payment, report it immediately.

Optimizely said it had addressed the vulnerability and removed the malicious code.

However, investigators have not established how many people may have been affected.

Experts Say Organizational Change Needed

Manoj Nair, CTO at Snyk, a cybersecurity company for Fortune 500s and partner of OpenAI and Anthropic, told Newsweek Tuesday that the responsibility lies with the companies and not with the individual.

"Organizations need to integrate security into the agentic development process," Nair explained. "The goal has to be eliminating vulnerabilities before they reach production, not scrambling after an attacker finds them."

He continued: "But agentic development is still driving vulnerability backlogs up fast, and triaging by severity is a relic of a slower era. AI can chain together lower-level vulnerabilities that were never worth prioritizing and turn them into a working exploit."

Ilkka Turunen, Field CTO at Sonatype, a software company that helps organizations manage and secure their software supply chains echoed those sentiments.

“Modern online services are built on a web of third-party code, and attackers are increasingly looking to exploit it," Turunen said.

"The Optimizely attack shows just how easily a single compromise can cause widespread disruption when so many organisations rely on the same external components and services.

"A dangerous confluence of developments is taking shape: attacks are becoming increasingly autonomous and exploiting third-party components, while businesses’ growing reliance on external software distribution creates yet another avenue for threat actors to exploit," he warned.

Turunen continued: "At this point, there’s no excuse for overlooking software supply chains. Organisations need to understand exactly what is being shipped, lest they unknowingly open the door to attackers themselves.”

Lowe said the attack also highlighted the risk posed by third-party code running on major websites.

“Your attack surface includes everyone you trust,” Lowe said. “If a party you trust is compromised then effectively you are.”

“The question isn’t whether you trust your third parties, it’s whether you’re monitoring them,” Lowe added. “For a lot of organisations the honest answer is no, and that’s the gap worth closing.”

Walsh agreed that the organizations must take accountability for this type of security, saying, "we cannot put the burden entirely on consumers: they cannot inspect the JavaScript behind every legitimate website. Businesses must validate third-party code, restrict its privileges, monitor unusual behavior, and contain supplier compromise.

"Cybersecurity must be built around prevention, containment, and recovery—not detection alone."
