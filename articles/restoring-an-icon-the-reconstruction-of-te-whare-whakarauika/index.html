Restoring an icon: The reconstruction of Te Whare Whakarauika Town Hall - News and information - Wellington City Council

TheTools · 20.10.2026 19:06 · source · permalink

The re-opening of Te Whare Whakarauika Wellington Town Hall in February has been made possible by some world-leading earthquake-strengthening, construction and heritage restoration practices.

Earthquake strengthening

The earthquake-strengthening and restoration of the Town Hall is one of the most complex construction projects undertaken in New Zealand.

The first challenge was making a building resting on its original early 1900s unreinforced concrete pilers on reclaimed land earthquake-resilient. Retrofitting base isolators (invented in Wellington in the 1970s), sheet piling and a rattle space ensure the building is strong and safe for the next 100 years.

The building has been re-piled in phases by propping it up with steel and installing new foundations. Rubber lead bearing base isolators have been installed throughout the building. The base isolators allow the building to move back-and-forth and side-to-side in an earthquake. This limits the movement inside the building, helping to preserve the many heritage elements.

Sheet piling is a construction technique that creates a support structure of interlocking steel sheets driven vertically down into the ground to form a wall. These metal sheets fit tightly together creating a barrier that can withstand the pressure of water and soil.

A rattle space is an area around the building that allows it to move in an earthquake without hitting anything.

Having a building which matches the strength of Pōneke’s newest buildings – including Tākina Wellington Convention and Exhibition Centre – allowed for the detailed heritage restoration and improvement work to take place.

Heritage restoration

Malmsbury Bluestone from Australia was very fashionable in New Zealand at the turn of the 20th century, and it can be seen on the lower exterior of the Town Hall. The bluestone had to be removed so steel reinforced concrete could be installed under the brick walls. Each piece of bluestone was labeled, barcoded, stored, and returned to its exact spot from which it was removed.

When the Town Hall was originally built over 120 years ago, pressed metal sheets were manufactured as an elegant yet economical alternative to regular plastered tile. Pressed metal was very popular in the late 19th century, but a lot of it was confiscated to melt down for the WWII war effort. Through the restoration of the Town Hall ceiling, two new pressed metal designs previously lost have been recovered and added to the archive.

Redevelopment Project Manager Gervis Lawrie says: “The pressed metal ceiling of the auditorium is 70% of the original sheets. We were able to use the original die to punch the remaining sheets. The auditorium floor timber is also 70% original. The choir stall timber is completely original, and has been taken away, stored, itemised and re-installed in its original space.”

As the main performance space, the Adam Auditorium’s high-quality acoustics have been preserved. An extra set of windows has been installed for acoustic isolation, while sound insulation has been added to the corridors surrounding the auditorium.

The Town Hall Organ

The longstanding jewel of the Town Hall is the concert organ. Crafted to have the power of a full symphony orchestra, the organ has been a cornerstone of concerts and community events. It is currently being rebuilt piece-by-piece, including some 12-metre long pipes that weigh 900kgs. It is one of the few remaining Edwardian-era concert pipe organs still in its original state. None of the organ’s core mechanism has been altered, meaning it will sound as wonderful as it did in 1906.
