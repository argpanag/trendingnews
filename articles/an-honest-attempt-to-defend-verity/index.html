An honest attempt to defend ‘Verity’

TheTools · 01.11.2026 22:17 · source · permalink

Nobody is walking into “Verity,” the latest installment of Colleen Hoover adaptations, expecting the next film festival favorite. After all, Hoover is infamous for her romance writing that gave her BookTok virality, along with dedicated fans and die-hard haters. As a stranger to her writing and unfamiliar with the contention behind “It Ends with Us,” I was, at the least, morbidly curious to see how this psychosexual thriller would translate into cinema. 

“Verity” follows undiscovered author Lowen Ashleigh (Dakota Johnson) as she becomes the ghostwriter for Verity Crawford (Anne Hathaway), a bestselling author left catatonic and paralyzed after a car crash. While staying at the Crawford family home to work in her office, Lowen stumbles across a hard drive with Verity’s tell-all confessional, detailing an alarming possessiveness over her husband Jeremy (Josh Hartnett) which resulted in the jealousy-driven death of her daughter. Lowen lives in Verity’s shadow, writing her stories and falling for her husband. The story is interspersed with memories from Verity’s perspective, and both Lowen and the audience must decide what truths to believe and figure out if there’s more to the bedridden author than what meets the eye.

Regardless of its intriguing premise, the film is a bland, coagulated blob of thriller plot points and sex scenes. In other words, it is bad. But, of course it’s bad — it doesn’t want to be “good.” It even seems gratuitous to write a review for such a film. However, films like “Verity” are also one of the most divisive topics I see in online arts criticism: Is the growing popularity of BookTok-esque media a sign of heightening anti-intellectualism, or is shitting on them just overly pretentious? After all, regardless of which side you’re on, we all have to draw some sort of line to define what we consider successful art. 

In “Verity,” there were plenty of plot points the writers could have expanded on and connected to real-world challenges, such as the competitive effects of internalized misogyny, the reality of postpartum struggles and the burnout of being a caregiver. They chose not to. Nevertheless, I don’t think that should count against this film, or any of its kind. Chick-flicks, romantasy books, reality TV and melodramas — out of all the relatively superficial media circulating in the world, our gender-biased brains tend to excessively shame the kind targeted toward women’s interests. They’re labeled trashy or lowbrow, and the people who enjoy them are often belittled for it. Yet, I think there’s value in the guilty-pleasure entertainment that’s so easily made fun of. 

The plots are simple, tropey and indulgent. For instance, “Verity” starts with a morbid twist on the classic meet-cute: A car crash leaves Lowen’s blood-splattered shirt unsuitable for a business meeting, and Jeremy swoops in to save the day with the donation of his very own crisp button-up, a charming smile and that instant romantic spark. It’s not at all believable. But, by being simple, tropey and indulgent, these stories reflect the desires of the audience they’re marketed toward. What problems do we want to watch get solved with unreasonable ease? What protagonists do we glorify and aspire to be? When we want to shut our brains off from the stress of our real world, where do we jump to? 

These types of films aren’t made to help solve the world’s problems — they’re meant to be a place we can run from them. It’s entertainment that’s made just to entertain, and there’s nothing wrong with that. But regrettably, that brings me to the most offensive crime that “Verity” commits: Despite setting low expectations through a raunchy and unrealistic plotline, it still manages to be incredibly, impressively boring. It is more likely to leave you eager to return to reality than it is to help you escape from it. 

For most of the film, it feels like nothing really happens and there is no reason behind any of the things that do happen. Verity immediately declares she is dark and disturbed at the start of her monologue, which means that all the following scenes revealing that she is dark and disturbed are rendered superfluous rather than shocking. The emptiness of the film is further emphasized by a complete dissonance between the film’s score and its actual scenes, building crescendos and repetitive scare chords that are paired with monotonous shots of Verity failing to be ominous as she moves her head a few degrees to the left or stands in corridors. The film attempts to redeem itself by tacking on a plot twist with a whisper of foreshadowing, but it’s too little and too late to compensate for the hour and a half you’ll spend waiting for something interesting to happen before it. 

Although the film is marketed as a thriller with spicy romance, the characters have zero personality and zero chemistry with each other. Lowen’s character whispers every sentence sensually, sticking various objects in her mouth while thinking, showering and vaping (a lot). Her chemistry with Jeremy spawns out of nowhere, which is wholly uninteresting to watch. Although Jeremy and Verity’s chemistry is somewhat redeemable, it is nearly impossible to become invested in the love triangle because Verity is so cartoonishly villainous that you can’t root for her, and Lowen is so unbelievably dry that you don’t want to root for her.

If you are one of Colleen Hoover’s die-hard mega fans, I am sorry to say that despite my best attempts, I cannot defend this one. I won’t speak on the novel, but I will on the film adaptation. “Verity” is too slow-paced to be entertaining and too cliche to warrant being slow-paced. Its flagrant lack of narrative coherence distracts from what could have been a simple but enjoyable story. If you choose to see this one in theaters, both Anne Hathaway and I warn you: “Only darkness ahead.”
