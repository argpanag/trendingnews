‘The Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping’ review: Underwhelming prequel to hit YA franchise suffers from a lack of compelling characters

TheTools · 19.11.2026 20:19 · source · permalink

Joseph Zada, Jesse Plemons, Ralph Fiennes and Kieran Culkin star in Lionsgate’s sixth instalment

Source: Lionsgate

‘The Hunger Games: Sunrise On The Reaping’

Dir: Francis Lawrence. US. 2026. 158mins

The sixth Hunger Games film serves as an origin story for a memorable side character who finally gets his moment at the centre of the action. But although Sunrise On The Reaping is appropriately dark and disturbing, returning us to the tyrannical dystopian world made popular in Suzanne Collins’ novels, familiarity proves even more deadly than this prequel’s titular do-or-die competition. Joseph Zada plays Haymitch Abernathy (portrayed by Woody Harrelson in the original series as an older, more disillusioned individual) but the new film badly needs a protagonist as compelling as Katniss Everdeen — or a screen presence as singular as Jennifer Lawrence.

A drab afterthought rather than a crucial new chapter

Arriving in UK and US theatres on November 20, Sunrise On The Reaping hopes to build on the success of the 2023 prequel The Ballad Of Songbirds & Snakes, which collected $364 million despite including none of the original stars. Set approximately 25 years before the events of 2012’s The Hunger Games, the new picture returns the franchise to its central hook; a group of youngsters battling to the death for the world’s entertainment. Action-packed and featuring new actors playing iconic characters, Sunrise On The Reaping will benefit from the star power of Jesse Plemons, Ralph Fiennes and others. Expect sturdy grosses for the latest instalment in a series that has far outlived its sci-fi YA rivals.

Through convoluted circumstances, Haymitch (Zada) is selected to participate in the 50th edition of The Hunger Games, where he must fight to stay alive against 99 competitors, including the spirited Maysilee (Mckenna Grace) who, like him, hails from impoverished District 12. Determined to overthrow the evil President Snow (Fiennes), the young man is helped by Plutarch (Plemons), who is part of Snow’s inner circle while covertly assisting the budding rebellion.

This is the fifth film in the series directed by Francis Lawrence, who managed to make The Ballad Of Songbirds & Snakes an unexpectedly thoughtful drama about the rise of a young Snow, largely shifting the focus away from action set pieces to deliver a solid character study. Sunrise On The Reaping seeks to combine the two impulses, giving longtime fans another harrowing Hunger Games adventure while also showing how Haymitch eventually became the embittered mentor to Katniss.

Sunrise On The Reaping struggles in its opening reels as we get to know the young Haymitch, who is not an especially arresting figure. Part of the problem is that Zada, recently seen in Netflix’s East Of Eden series, fails to captivate, his performance lacking the gravitas or inner fire to suggest a revolutionary-in-training. But Billy Ray and Michael Lesslie’s screenplay also shoulders its share of the blame, keeping Haymitch a superficially brooding hero. The other new characters are equally underwhelming, with Grace’s Maysilee barely making much of an impression beyond the fact that she is lethal with a blowgun.

The storytelling ramps up once Haymitch and the other tributes are tossed into The Hunger Games, which allows Lawrence to craft a few gripping set pieces. In Sunrise On The Reaping, the natural world is filled with terrible dangers set to destroy the contestants, including ravenous forest creatures, carnivorous berries and bloodthirsty birds. Each of these sequences emphasises the brutality of the Games even before the tributes square off in ferocious hand-to-hand combat. By now, viewers will be well-acquainted with the horrible deaths and mournful monologues about destiny that accompany the Games, but Lawrence does his best to inject a grim urgency to the predictable plot machinations.

In truth, Sunrise On The Reaping’s most striking quality is Uli Hanisch’s production design, which turns the ominous Capitol into a dreary monochrome – a stark contrast to the lavish interiors that are bathed in sleek lighting and decorated with futuristic furniture. The result is a totalitarian nightmare in which the privileged cruelly lord their power over the weak. The film’s look proves a more potent illustration of political oppression than anything expressed in the tortured, on-the-nose dialogue.

Kieran Culkin plays the egotistical master of ceremonies Caesar Flickerman (played by Stanley Tucci portrayed in the earlier films), and although the Oscar-winner avoids copying his predecessor’s mannerisms, the effect is nonetheless something of a dull echo. Fiennes’ Snow (formerly played by Donald Sutherland) brings a fair share of haughty malice, but is similarly muted.

Plemons acquits himself better in a role originally brought to life by the late Philip Seymour Hoffman, emerging as an unlikely father figure to Haymitch. But because Zada hints at neither Harrelson’s sarcastic wit nor zen-like calm, Sunrise On The Reaping is an origin story without a sense of grandeur or stakes. Rather than setting up the epic Katniss narrative that transfixed millions of moviegoers, this prequel feels oddly incidental — a drab afterthought rather than a crucial new chapter.
