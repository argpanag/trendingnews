Lindsay Clancy case declared a mistrial after jurors fail to reach an agreement

TheTools · 05.09.2026 00:51 · source · permalink

Summary

  • The judge in the trial of Lindsay Clancy - the US mother accused of murdering her three children - has officially declared a mistrial

  • That means the trial has collapsed without a verdict - court will reconvene at the end of the month to decide what happens next

  • The decision follows a last-ditch appeal from the defence after the jury said earlier that "with a heavy heart" they were "unable to come to a unanimous decision"

  • Clancy, 36, is charged with three counts of first-degree murder in the deaths of her three children - Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and eight-month-old Callan - at their Massachusetts home in 2023

  • She does not deny killing them, but her lawyer argues she was suffering from postpartum psychosis, and could not distinguish right from wrong

The judge in the trial of Lindsay Clancy - the US mother accused of murdering her three children - has officially declared a mistrial

That means the trial has collapsed without a verdict - court will reconvene at the end of the month to decide what happens next

The decision follows a last-ditch appeal from the defence after the jury said earlier that "with a heavy heart" they were "unable to come to a unanimous decision"

Clancy, 36, is charged with three counts of first-degree murder in the deaths of her three children - Cora, 5, Dawson, 3, and eight-month-old Callan - at their Massachusetts home in 2023

She does not deny killing them, but her lawyer argues she was suffering from postpartum psychosis, and could not distinguish right from wrong

  3. How charges against Clancy might have differed in other countriespublished at 22:39 BST 4 September
    22:39 BST 4 September

    Asma Khalid
    Co-host, The Global Story Podcast

    In another country, the charges against Lindsay Clancy might have looked somewhat different - at least regarding youngest child, who was eight months old when he was killed.

    For his death, Clancy may have faced a charge of infanticide rather than murder.

    Several other Western countries - including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Ireland - have a specific law around infanticide.

    The definitions vary, but broadly they follow the British precedent set about 100 years ago, which offers leniency to a mother for killing a baby in the first year of their life. (Though, in some countries, the age limit is higher).

    The crime often carries a lighter prison sentence than murder. In Canada, for example, the maximum sentence is five years.

    The rationale for an infanticide distinction "is based around the idea that the balance of the mother's mind was disturbed as a consequence of her not having fully recovered from the effects of giving birth", says Dr Karen Brennan, infanticide researcher at the University of Essex.

    To be clear, the US has no infanticide law. But in 2018, Illinois, external became the first state in the nation to explicitly recognise postpartum mental health as a factor in sentencing.

    All this being said, only one of Clancy’s children was an infant. Her older children were three and five years old.

  4. 'History buff' judge evokes Churchill as he thanks jurypublished at 22:20 BST 4 September
    22:20 BST 4 September

    Kwasi Gyamfi Asiedu
    Live reporter

    Following the announcement of the mistrial, Judge Sullivan thanked the 12 jurors who, after several days of deliberating, could not come a unanimous decision.

    Sullivan, a self-described "history buff" evoked Britain's World War Two leader Winston Churchill while expressing his gratitude to the nine women and three men on the jury.

    Paraphrasing Churchill in his famous "Finest Hour" speech in 1940, Sullivan told the jurors: "Let us brace for our duties."

    "I always liked that phrase," Sullivan said.

    "Each one of you knew how long the case was going to go, you knew how difficult the case was going to be to listen to facts wise, but still you braced for that duty."

  7. How the Clancy trial has been distorted on social mediapublished at 21:19 BST 4 September
    21:19 BST 4 September

    Marianna Spring
    Disinformation and social media correspondent

    Social media feeds right now are flooded with theories, analysis and conspiracy content about the Lindsay Clancy trial. This is not the first trial by TikTok - but it's definitely captured the internet's attention.

    In many of the videos, women share their legitimate concerns about a lack of support for postpartum psychosis in the US, which is central to Clancy's defence.

    But some sleuths have taken it further, unpicking every moment from the court livestream and shedding doubt on whether Clancy killed her children in the first place - even though that's something to which she has already admitted.

    Clancy's lawyer Kevin Reddington is the subject of fan edits and montages, while other relatives - like her ex-husband Patrick Clancy - are targeted with abuse and, in some cases, defamatory claims that go beyond evidenced criticism. There was even a viral Bratz doll re-enactment of the crime scene questioning whether she killed her children.

    On a recent episode of the BBC's Top Comment podcast, fellow investigative journalist Matt Shea and I analysed how the facts of this trial are being distorted on social media - and spoke to some sleuths about why they're posting.

  8. New jury would hear all evidence from the beginningpublished at 20:58 BST 4 September
    20:58 BST 4 September

    Sakshi Venkatraman
    US reporter

    Though we don't yet know what will happen next, Massachusetts criminal defence lawyer Joe Serpa says he thinks the prosecution is likely to retry this case.

    "Given the number and seriousness of the charges, the public attention, and the resources already committed, it would be unusual for the Commonwealth simply to abandon the prosecution after one deadlocked jury," he says.

    "If there is a second trial, a completely new jury will hear the evidence from the beginning," he adds.

    "The lawyers, however, will not be starting from zero. Both sides have now seen the opposing case in full and can revise their expert examinations, cross-examinations, themes, and presentation," Serpa explains.

    "The central question for a retrial may therefore be whether the Commonwealth can present essentially the same evidence with greater restraint and sensitivity, while the defense attempts to reproduce what it believes was a jury substantially inclined toward acquittal."
