Samara Nangala Fernandez Brown: why the nation should bear witness to her cousin Kumanjayi Walker’s death

TheTools · 09.11.2026 02:07 · source · permalink

‘The world needs to see what happened to Kumanjayi.’

Warning: This story contains images of and references to Indigenous Australians who have died

Samara Nangala Fernandez Brown, a Warlpiri woman, can’t forget the moment she might have saved her cousin from his death at the hands of Northern Territory police.

In November 2019, 19-year-old Kumanjayi Walker was fatally shot by the now-former Northern Territory police officer Zachary Rolfe. In SBS and NITV’s new true crime documentary series, 2.6 Seconds: Death in the Outback, his cousin, Fernandez Brown, who led the nationwide Justice for Walker campaign, offers her testimony of the events that led to Walker’s death.

It’s a testimony accompanied by previously unseen police body-worn footage of Walker’s final hours, and Fernandez Brown’s personal recordings of the injustices she says were dealt to her community in the aftermath.

“In our culture, seeing images of someone who has died is culturally inappropriate, so it wasn’t an easy decision to ask my aunty if she would allow footage of the last moments of her son’s life to be shown to Australia,” Fernandez Brown says. “I felt so much grief even having to ask her and other family members for permission.”

But first-hand footage is a vital element of 2.6 Seconds’ truth-telling approach. “There was this resounding feeling from everybody that it just needed to be seen. If we’re talking about truth-telling, then the world needs to see what happened to Kumanjayi.”

Fernandez Brown is acutely aware of how such intimate footage of her cousin’s last days will stay with people, but she and her family say they need people to see it. “Oftentimes with deaths in custody in Australia, and also globally, there seems to be an onus on the individual,” she says. “There seems to be this blame on the individual. So, it was important to show how he was treated, and the reality of his last moments. It was so difficult and gut-wrenching to watch, and I know it’s going to impact so many people to see.”

On the night of Walker’s death, Fernandez Brown and Walker were in their home community of Yuendumu for their grandfather’s funeral. But the sorry business of those days has never come to an end for Fernandez Brown, her family and her people. Seven years after Rolfe fired those bullets, and four years after he was acquitted on all charges relating to Walker’s death, Fernandez Brown hopes 2.6 Seconds: Death in the Outback will bring a long overdue reckoning.

“Until after Australia and the world see it, I don’t feel like I can properly take care of myself,” she says. “I’m still waiting for that. It feels really surreal that we’re at the end now.”

In one of the documentary’s many impactful testimonials, Fernandez Brown reflects on the last time she saw Walker. He was walking on the road while she was driving from a sorry business ceremony for their grandfather to his gravesite at the cemetery. “It’s a moment that I often think about and reflect on because it just would have changed everything if he did come to the cemetery with us. As everybody knows, it happened while we were at the cemetery, and he was at the house.”

The series outlines a chain of tragic moments and systemic failures without which things could have turned out differently. “People watching the documentary will identify those sliding doors moments that would have changed the outcome of Kumanjayi still being with us,” Fernandez Brown says. “It is deeply painful to think about one thing that could have changed the rest of our lives, essentially, our family and communities, because we wouldn’t be grieving the loss of him.”

A national reckoning with a global injustice

For Fernandez Brown, 2.6 Seconds: Death in the Outback is also about connecting Walker’s death to the larger national and international conversations around deaths of people of colour at the hands of police.

Since the royal commission into Aboriginal deaths in custody handed down its final report in 1991, more than 600 Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people have died or been killed while in police custody in Australia.

“We tend to have an understanding of it globally, particularly in North America, but a lot of people don’t know about deaths in custody in Australia and that deeply horrific history and present we have,” Fernandez Brown says.

The series “invites people to care about Kumanjayi, about my family, about Warlpiri, but even beyond that, about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, and other families that might have experienced death in custody”, Fernandez Brown says.

“I hope it makes them feel seen, feel heard, and feel some sense of healing through us. I hope it invites broader Australia to no longer sit on the fence about deaths in custody, to no longer tiptoe around this epidemic that’s happening in Australia.

“I hope it invites global solidarity with other Indigenous people and people of colour around the world. I hope it brings change in Australia. I hope people are able to reckon with the failed systems that exist in Australia and the systems that failed Kumanjayi and are continuing to fail other Kumanjayis.”

Beyond 2.6 Seconds: Death in the Outback

Despite the injustice and grief, Fernandez Brown is working to create momentum for hope and change in the wake of her cousin’s death. A former board member and Stop Black Deaths in Custody campaigner with the Dhadjowa Foundation, she is now establishing a not-for-profit organisation, 4 Yapa Ltd. Its work will include advocating for the implementation of recommendations from her cousin’s coronial inquest, developing media and legal literacy resources to help Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people understand their rights, and investing in the Yuendumu community.

“I’m hopeful that we can move towards a conversation that doesn’t end at deaths in custody,” Fernandez Brown says. “How do we switch to prevention? How do we talk about investing in our communities? How do we talk about the barriers that exist and what we have control over? That’s what we’re hoping to do, to support our communities.

“I’m interested in doing two things at once: looking at how we got there and how we stop that, but also, how do we get from stopping deaths in custody to keeping our people safe and happy? How do we look towards future successes and really try to push that narrative where we have control and we’re not just fighting against this system?”

In Australia, the crisis support service Lifeline is 13 11 14. Indigenous Australians can call 13YARN on 13 92 76 for information and crisis support. Other international helplines can be found at befrienders.org.
