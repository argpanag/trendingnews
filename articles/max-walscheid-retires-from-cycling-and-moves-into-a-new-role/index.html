Max Walscheid retires from cycling – and moves into a new role at CRIVIT Performance Center | Trek Race Shop

TheTools · 25.09.2026 12:45 · source · permalink

After more than a decade as a professional cyclist, Max Walscheid has decided to retire from racing at the end of this season. His decision was made easier as Lidl-Trek and Schwarz Group offered the perfect transition.

At the end of the 2026 season, Max Walscheid will stop racing professionally but remain with Lidl-Trek, moving into a new role at the CRIVIT Performance Center in Bad Wimpfen alongside the Health & Performance Team. The 33-year-old, who will continue his medical studies, will draw on his experience in high-performance sport and medicine to further strengthen the team.

Throughout his career as a professional cyclist, Walscheid rode eight Grand Tours and 14 Monuments, whilst collecting 15 victories – including becoming World Champion in the Mixed Relay Team Time Trial in 2021. But over the years he also developed himself into a great support rider for top sprinters like Jonathan Milan and especially for the northern classics in Flanders and Roubaix, where he finished 8th in 2023. 

His last race will be the Sparkassen Münsterland Giro on October, 3rd – a race Walscheid won already back in 2018.

While Walscheid still has more studying to do in order to become a fully-qualified doctor, he will now take a role within the team that includes equipment and performance testing and diagnosis, alongside projects at the CRIVIT Performance Center where he can begin applying the knowledge accumulated through more than a decade at the highest level of cycling, as well as using his educational background.

“I believe sport has formed me and my character and I will never leave this part behind,” he says. “So working in a high-performance environment with opportunities to test, to optimise – that’s something I find extremely interesting.”

Photo by Dario Belingheri/Getty Images

“Max only spent one year racing with Lidl-Trek, but he certainly made it count. He rode the best season of his career, and he was a great addition to the sprint train of Jonathan Milan, as well as supporting Mads on the cobbles,” says Andy Schleck, Lidl-Trek CEO.

“What makes him right for this new role is the combination of his racing experience and medical studies. He knows from the inside what a rider goes through, from a Grand Tour to Roubaix, and his medical training gives him the tools to understand why. Very few people have both, and we’re pleased to keep that perspective within the team.”

When you love something like I love cycling and you dedicate such big part of your life for such long time to it, it is never easy to say goodbye.

It hurts, yet it is the perfect moment and I am very lucky to choose this moment after a great last year with my team and whilst being on personal peak condition.

When signing last year with the team, I communicated honestly that I can see the end of my chapter as a rider already on the horizon. Yet I was extremely motivated to make the absolutely most of it, as I‘ve always been.

The story: Max Walscheid - two careers in one

There was a time when Max Walscheid thought becoming a professional sportsperson belonged to a different universe. During his formative years in Germany, the idea of it never really entered his psyche. None of his family members had a history in elite sport and there were few professional athletes in his immediate surroundings. He recalls visiting the track and field facilities near home and experimenting with what his body was capable of, but professional sport was something that happened to other people.

“I never really thought of it as an opportunity for myself,” he says. “The professional sports world seemed to be in a completely different dimension.”

Studying medicine, though, was different: “I always wanted to study medicine when I was still going to school. I just thought it was a pretty cool job.”

So, a young Max Walschied had two ambitions that existed almost independently in his mind; one seemed realistic, and the other almost unimaginable. Sometimes, though, life has a way of surprising you. Walscheid discovered bike racing, and found he had a talent for it. 

While he was a relative latecomer to the sport, he advanced quickly through the under-23 ranks and was spotted in 2015 by a WorldTour team. From then, his journey in professional cycling began. The years that followed were filled with a relentless education on elite performance: learning how to train, race, eat, recover, travel and, ultimately, survive at WorldTour level. Medicine, on the other hand, was put on hold.

“Once I was a pro, I paused studying for four years completely. I definitely had to catch up on some training as my base was not very big. I had a rather thin under-23 programme and definitely needed to raise my physical abilities to keep up in the WorldTour,” he explains.

“I was completely overwhelmed and busy with being professional. Nutrition, tactics, travelling – there was a lot to learn, which I didn’t know back then.”

The seasons rolled on and university disappeared into the background. In 2020, however, the world came to a sudden and abrupt halt. The Covid-19 pandemic struck and, like everything, professional cycling was brought to a standstill – Walscheid found himself with time to think again.

“Racing paused, nobody knew how the season would proceed and suddenly I had time and thought: what I could do with that?”

It didn’t take long for the 33-year-old to work it out: “I restarted university and went back to my studies,” he says.

Walscheid stresses that he didn’t have a grand plan to complete a medical degree while simultaneously maintaining a WorldTour career when racing resumed. He just restarted his studies and kept going, fitting in studying and lectures when he could alongside a demanding training and racing schedule. The progress was often slow, but it was progress nonetheless. There were no shortcuts for the German, he attended a regular schedule, completed theory and practical elements, and was present like any other student.

“I realised that it is possible. In the end, progress added up,” he states.

Last winter, when he made his move to Lidl-Trek, he took on his first full-time semester alongside training camps, testing and travel. His schedule, as he admits, was “on the limit”.

“Doing that was really demanding, but eventually I rode the strongest season of my career,” Walscheid says. While he had finally seemed to strike the balance of pursuing his medical education with the demands of being a professional cyclist, Walscheid still struggled to see how the two worlds could ever meet.

“People kept telling me that it was such a nice sweet spot to have the theoretical background, education towards being a doctor and then credibility in sports, being professional myself. That’s always nice to hear, but honestly I didn’t see many job opportunities where this would either really count or which sparked a lot of interest,” he says.

“Of course. I could become a regular doctor, maybe including some sports medicine into my daily work, but then more than a decade as a WorldTour professional would have been gone, and there wouldn’t be many cross-links anymore.”

At the end of 2026, though, after eleven years in the professional ranks, Walscheid has found his solution. While he will hang up his race wheels by the end of this season, concluding his career at Sparkassen Münsterland Giro, he won’t be waving goodbye to the sport he has dedicated his life to. 

His next chapter will take him to the CRIVIT Performance Center in Bad Wimpfen now. So this is by far not the end of Walscheid’s story in cycling, but a logical continuation of his dual-careers so far. He once put medicine aside to pursue cycling, then spent years fitting university around an elite sporting career. Lidl-Trek will help him bring those two identities together.
