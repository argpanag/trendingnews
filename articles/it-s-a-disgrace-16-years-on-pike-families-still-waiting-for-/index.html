'It's a disgrace': 16 years on, Pike families still waiting for decision on criminal charges

TheTools · 22.11.2026 10:57 · source · permalink
PHOTO

Pike River families say it is a disgrace they are still waiting for a decision on whether criminal charges will be laid following an explosion at a mine in which 26 men died.

Two miners survived and managed to walk out.

In February, police said they were nearing the final stages of their criminal investigation into the disaster at the West Coast mine on November 19 in 2010.

Eight months on, police assistant commissioner Corrie Parnell reiterated that point.

"Operational security around the next phase of the investigation is critical, and therefore it is not appropriate to comment further at this time."

The investigation was reopened in 2018 and figures showed since then it had cost more than $35 million, with about half  spent on consultants, specialist fees and overseas travel.

In September 2022, police announced they were reopening the borehole drilling operation as part of the investigation and 10 boreholes were drilled, imaged and resealed.

Human remains were found in the mine in 2023. In November 2024, police said they were working with the Crown Solicitor and considering whether to lay charges over the disaster. It was hoped a decision would be reached in the first half of last year.

In February, police said they had been working with the Wellington Crown Solicitor on the "legally complex" case for more than 18 months.

Crown Law referred questions to police.

Some family members of those who died said it was not good enough.

Sonya Rockhouse, whose son Ben was 21 when he was killed, said it was "bloody frustrating" and "absolutely ridiculous" to still be waiting for a decision.

Police had been in touch with her last week but they did not have any new information.

"I said to them, 'You know, people are starting to die, it's been so long', and we just don't understand the hold up really - it just beggars belief."

Lawyer Nigel Hampton, KC, who represented the miners' union at a royal commission of inquiry and the victims' families, was gravely ill, as were others connected to the disaster.

"Anna's [Osborne, who lost her husband] living on borrowed time, her five years was up last year and thankfully she's showing no signs of anything happening," Rockhouse said.

"But you just don't know. I'm getting older, I'm 68 next birthday, I could have a heart attack and drop dead tomorrow."

She said those deciding whether to prosecute "need to get their flippin' act together and make the right decision".

"We've warned them, we've told them that if we don't get the result that we want, then they're going to hear a hell of a lot more from us.

"If we've got to go back and protest and do what we did before, then we will - I'll retire from work and we'll go for it."

Meeting with minister 'waste of time'

On the 16th anniversary, Rockhouse said she and Osborne would be in Auckland for the 2026 New Zealand Screen Awards, where the feature film Pike River about the mining disaster had received 15 nominations.

"It's better than last year on the 15th anniversary, we were meeting with Brooke van Velden and that was a flipping waste of time."

She said they had not heard anything since meeting with the former minister for workplace relations and safety, but still wanted to see a corporate manslaughter charge introduced in New Zealand.

Bernie Monk, whose 23-year-old son Michael died in the mine, said it had been a 16-year wait for redress.

He said some of the families were told three months ago that a decision on charges was weeks away, but then nothing had happened.

"It's a disgrace. We've got one of the biggest industrial homicides in New Zealand's history and we've had to wait 16 years to get justice and accountability.

"We are never going to go away until something is done."

Monk said on the 16th anniversary, he would be at the opening of the memorial at the mine site, which he expected would become a major asset for the West Coast and those wanting to commemorate the lives of the 29 men.

'Very odd' events on the night - Brownlee

In response to questions from RNZ’s Nine to Noon host Kathryn Ryan about whether it was appropriate that families were still waiting for a decision on a prosecution, 16 years on, parliament’s Speaker Gerry Brownlee said it was not, but that he had to be careful about what he said.

"What I will say is that it surprised me. I got there a few hours after it happened on the night, up on the mountain, and from that moment - the police who should have been in charge and were in charge legally, had the [Pike River] company representative as a spokesman.

"At the time, I found that very odd because there had to be questions about what actually happened here. That's all I'll say about that."

He said there remained questions over how the Pike River disaster was able to happen.

There was no ability for the methane to escape the mine before the explosion, he said, and it was his personal opinion that there should have been a second entrance into the mine.

"In the film footage you see on television of the stack, so-called breather stack, that's 180 metres above the drift and there were flames coming out of that, so the intensity of that inferno was something wicked. Why wasn't there a second entrance?"

He said he met the mine rescue crew that day with then Prime Minister Sir John Key.

"I met them on the night it happened and they weren't going in there. They made it very clear, no - methane level's too high, it's dangerous."

He and Key met with them again the following Monday and asked each of them if they would go in, and they all said no.

Department of Conservation Greymouth operations manager Chris Hickford said it had been working with the families on the memorial. Details and photos of it would be released once families had been to the site for an invite-only event on Thursday.

The upper valley and memorial would remain closed to the public until visitor access, interpretation, and other facilities were completed - likely in mid-2027, he said.

It would be the first time since the disaster that members of the public were able to visit the portal.
