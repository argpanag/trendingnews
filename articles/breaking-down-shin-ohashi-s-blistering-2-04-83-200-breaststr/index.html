MEN’S 200 BREASTSTROKE – FINAL

  • World Record – 2:05.48, Qin Haiyang (CHN), 2023
  • World Junior Record – 2:06.59, Shin Ohashi (JPN), 2026
  • Asian Record – 2:05.48, Qin Haiyang (CHN), 2023
  • Games Record – 2:07.03, Qin Haiyang (CHN), 2023

GOLD – Shin Ohashi (JPN), 2:04.83 *WORLD RECORD*
SILVER – Denis Petrashov (KGZ), 2:08.67
BRONZE – Cho Sungjae (KOR), 2:08.76

In what has been the biggest swim of the 2026 Asian Games so far, 17-year-old Shin Ohashi unleashed an electrifying 2:04.83 200 breaststroke to notch a new World Record and become the first swimmer ever to crack the 2:05 barrier.

Ohashi, who entered the race as the fastest junior swimmer in history at 2:06.59, shaved nearly two full seconds off that World Junior Record and further etched his name into the history books, surpassing several prominent names in the all-time rankings.

The thought of anybody breaking that 2:05 barrier seemed daunting, not to mention by a TEENAGER. Ohashi was hardly sniffing the 2:05 range entering this meet on home soil, though that clearly didn’t play a factor.

When you look at some of the top talents ever to race this event, Ohashi swam this race quite differently than those who previously sat ahead of him.

Splits Comparison: Top Five Fastest Performers Ever in Men’s LCM 200 Breaststroke

* World Record-breaking swims

When you look at the star power of the top performers in history, look no further than the fact that only three other men (before Ohashi) have managed to swim a sub-2:06 200 breaststroke: China’s Qin Haiyang, France’s Leon Marchand, and Australia’s Zac Stubblety-Cook. Anton Chupkov nearly did in 2019, stopping the clock in 2:06.12 at the World Championships.

If we look at Stubblety-Cook and Chupkov, it’s pretty clear what their race strategy is: come home like a rocket. Both use a primarily kick-dominated stroke and don’t go out exceptionally fast, with both floating around 1:01-high to 1:02-low range, though the magic happens in the back half. Both are the only swimmers in the top five to finish under 32 seconds on their final 50.

Qin and Marchand are much more balanced in their strokes, using solid all-around strength to maintain consistent speed in their race efforts, though Qin has the advantage of pure size and length, standing (or swimming) about six feet four inches tall (not including his reach). Both Qin and Marchand open in the sub-1:01 range but come home a bit differently, with Qin charging back in 32.12 to Marchand’s 32.73.

Ohashi is a rocket out of the gates, splitting 28.27/31.62, the only swimmer in the top five to go out under 1:00 in 59.89. From there, it is just hold on with a more upper-body-dominated stroke. For many, the “holding on” splits can be tough to hold onto, but for the 17-year-old, those splits compare very favorably.

His 3rd 50 of 32.51 is dead-center of the pack when compared to the next four fastest performers in history. His final 50 of 32.43 is 4th, with Marchand being the only one slower to come home.

It cannot be overstated: a 17-year-old throwing down these splits is absurd, and with plenty of big championship meets still to come in his young career, the sky is the limit for what Ohashi can produce.
