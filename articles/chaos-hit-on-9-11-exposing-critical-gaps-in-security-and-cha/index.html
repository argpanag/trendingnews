Chaos hit on 9/11, exposing critical gaps in security and changing NORAD forever

TheTools · 11.09.2026 23:58 · source · permalink

Nine minutes before the first plane hit the north tower of the World Trade Center on Sept. 11, 2001, national guardsmen in upstate New York received word the FAA believed an aircraft had been hijacked. 

“They did not know what flight had been hijacked. They didn’t know where the aircraft was. They didn’t know where it was going. They just knew an aircraft might have been hijacked,” said Lance Blythe, the historian at the North American Aerospace Defense Command. 

Those guardsmen, the first military members to receive such alerts, worked for the Northeast Air Defense Center and passed the word up to commanders in Colorado Springs’ Cheyenne Mountain base, but they never got enough information to act on. 

The guardsmen sent up two F-15 fighters but didn’t have clear targets to give them. 

The threat level also wasn’t immediately clear. Just three weeks prior, a French stuntman had made international news after his motorized paraglider got tangled in the torch of the Statue of Liberty. 

It was also not immediately clear that the report of the hijacked plane and the plane hitting the first tower were the same incident, Blythe said. 

Such a case had never been anticipated. 

“We never built a scenario around a hijacked airplane that would come back and serve as a weapon itself,” recalled retired Lt. Gen. Mike Gould, who was the commander of Cheyenne Mountain Operations Center during the attack, in an interview with The Gazette.

On that disjointed morning, the military leaders at the North American Aerospace Defense Command watched a second plane hit the tower on television like others all over the world. 

News of a fourth hijacked aircraft reached the guardsmen five minutes after a plane crashed into a Pennsylvania field, Blythe said. The passengers and crew fought against the terrorists to keep the plane from reaching its target in Washington, D.C. 

Former Canadian F-18 pilot Rob Martin was among those watching the catastrophe after a friend called to wake him up. He had been asleep after flying night training exercises out of the Canadian Forces Base in Cold Lake, Alberta.

When Martin saw the second plane hit the south tower, he told his friend he had to go because he was needed at work. 

Across Canada and the U.S., Martin, a now-retired lieutenant colonel, was a pilot of one of the 14 fighter planes available to protect cities from an attack. That number had fallen from about 150 planes at the height of the Cold War, Gould said. 

Hours after the event, Martin’s squadron loaded their airplanes with weapons while they awaited orders.

He recalled thinking at the time: “We don’t know if this is going to be a persistent event or a one-time thing.”  

Officials in NORAD also didn’t know what was coming next, if attacks could happen on other population centers or military targets. 

When a plane traveling west wasn’t responding to the FAA’s Air Route Terminal Control Center, NORAD’s commander, Gen. Ed Eberhart, ordered the blast doors closed, in case the plane was headed for the mountain, Gould said.

At the same time, the Colorado Springs-based officials were responsible for briefing the National Military Command Center, a gathering of combatant commanders, White House representatives and other high-level leaders, Gould said. A young lieutenant briefed the council that day, a testament to the effectiveness of regular training, he said. 

“That’s where the discussion occurred about ‘We ought to get everybody on the ground so we can sort out what’s actually happening,’” Gould said. 

FAA grounds planes

The FAA’s first ground order was issued shortly after a third plane hit the Pentagon. 

The National Archives reports more than 4,500 aircraft were flying that morning, on what the FAA described as a “severe clear” — or perfect weather — day.

The ground order stranded passengers across the country and in Canada, where some inbound international flights had been ordered to land. 

A few days after the attack, Martin was among those few fighter-jet pilots in the sky patrolling Western Canada. 

“It was the weirdest feeling ever. We were really the only airplanes flying,” he recalled. They were also the first pilots the air traffic controllers had spoken with in days. 

During the ground order, the roar of F-16s was one of the few sounds.

“I remember walking down Tejon Street (early Tuesday evening), and all the businesses had their doors and windows open and the TVs were just playing the news … and there was nobody out,” said former Gazette reporter Jeremy Meyer, now director of communications for the Colorado Department of Education.

The only sound was of President George W. Bush’s speech reverberating downtown, he said. 

“This is a day when all Americans from every walk of life unite in our resolve for justice and peace. America has stood down enemies before, and we will do so this time,” Bush said, as part of his short address.  

Addressing the intelligence failure 

The day after the attacks, The Washington Post reported questions had been raised about inadequate intelligence to protect the country and the intelligence failure was acknowledged quickly. Years later, the analysis in the 9/11 Commission Report publicly released in 2004 revealed systematic problems with information sharing between agencies, including the FBI and the CIA, in addition to inadequate security screening at airports where the terrorists entered the country. 

“The 9/11 story itself is one of known terrorists moving freely across borders, skirting screening protocols, and altering identification documents to evade law enforcement,” a 2026 review of the 9/11 Commission report said. 

For Gretchen Bliss, a young intelligence professional working inside Cheyenne Mountain, the work to understand the attacks started immediately after they happened. 

“We literally spent the next 48 hours churning through data trying to figure out, is there anything else going on?” she recalled. 

For the next two years, Bliss was among those working 16-hour days, seven days a week to establish how the military could monitor for threats coming from inside the country. NORAD and the DOD as a whole were focused on tracking and responding to external threats, such as Russian bombers coming over the North Pole.  

Some of that work focused on answering basic questions: “What imagery do we need? … Where do we need the imagery to go?” 

While the silos among intelligence agencies still existed, the professionals on the ground were working to make sure information was getting to the right people. 

“I’ve never seen people in the government come together more than in the five years after,” said Bliss, who now works for the University of Colorado Colorado Springs as a program director for cybersecurity. She also later married Martin, who came to work for NORAD later in his career. 

In addition to ramping up efforts to track threats, the staff working in Cheyenne Mountain started setting up Northern Command. The new organization would need to be a combatant command, like Central Command set up to manage military actions in the Middle East, while also working with law enforcement and the National Guard to address threats at home, Bliss said.  

The basic functions of the combatant command were in place in six months, but it took two years for it to be fully operational. It was declared operational on Sept. 11, 2003. 

At the end of five years, the command had a clear handle on all the important policy questions and was able to back off the 12-hour days staff had worked for years, Bliss said. 

To better coordinate the nation’s numerous intelligence agencies, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence was also born. 

The changes helped create a more complete picture of the terrorism threats, Bliss said.

Now, during large events, such as the Olympics, federal agencies are prepositioned with chains of command in place to provide a security response. For example, fighter planes under NORAD’s command ensure airspace remains clear over the Super Bowl, Blythe said. 

20 years of war 

In the wake of the 9/11 attack, the U.S. invaded Afghanistan. Years of peace came to an end.

Colorado Springs resident Jason Shoffit was stationed at McChord Air Force Base outside Tacoma, Washington, when a pre-dawn call from his mother ripped him from sleep, into the Tuesday that changed everything for everyone.

“Are we going to war?” she asked.

Shoffit stuttered in groggy confusion: “Why, what … what happened?”

The then-24-year-old was two years into an Air Force enlistment. Combat deployments were certainly a possibility — it’s what enlistees trained for constantly — but after the Gulf War, the late 1990s seemed to usher in comparatively calmer times on the global conflict front.

“We had a ‘peacetime’ military. Everybody had been living in such peace and tranquility up until that point — yeah we had skirmishes here and there, however, nothing on this scale,” Shoffit said. “All that stopped on 9/11. We’re going to war, that was the mindset.”

President Bush declared the Global War on Terror nine days later, on Sept. 20. The following month, on Oct. 7, forces from the U.S. and United Kingdom began airstrikes in Afghanistan ahead of large-scale ground invasions.

For the next 20 years, Shoffit said his life was “a roller coaster.” His first post-9/11 assignment was in Germany, where he said he worked 12-to-16-hour days as a jet engine mechanic, servicing and repairing aircraft flying sorties in the war zone. “They’re doing nothing but going down to Afghanistan on a daily basis. It was constant combat support, launching aircraft because I was working the C-17s at the time and they were an absolute workhorse.”

It wasn’t until Shoffit’s subsequent duty station, at Royal Air Force Station Mildenhall in Suffolk, England, that he realized he wanted to do more.

He began training as a sensor operator — a “co-pilot” for unmanned military combat drones — officially shifting into that role in 2013. 

When Shoffit officially earned his drone wings, he said he reached out to a man he’d served with while stationed in Texas, whose firefighter brother had responded to and died in the twin towers.

“I asked if it was OK to do a tribute to him. I said, ‘I don’t know when it’ll happen, but you’ll know it when you see it,’” he said. “I finally had an opportunity and was able to do a strike in honor of his brother.”

The honoring was a private affair, but Shoffit let his superiors and colleagues know what was going on in his head and his heart.

“I said, ‘I know we’re going to be doing this (regardless),’” Shoffit said, “but this is why I’m doing it.”

Identifying the next threat

Since 9/11, the U.S. has not seen a large-scale terrorism attack. For many, that tragic day has evolved from defining moments into memory and topics of study.

“I think, as a whole, our country is much better prepared than we were back then,” Gould said. But he would like to see the country renew its commitment to security on this anniversary so that such an attack never happens again. 

From the intelligence perspective, Bliss said, the nation became more consistent and proactive in addressing the terrorism threat, but the agencies tasked with security should be re-evaluated to ensure they are well-structured and new security measures are preventing violence. 

While the 25-year review of the 9/11 Commission report highlighted work that remains, such as introducing biometric tracking of travelers, the five years following the attacks were transformational for the intelligence community and brought together agencies that traditionally hated each other. 

Now Bliss would like to see intelligence professionals have enough time to identify the next national security threat.

“Overall, it’s better. Is it perfect? No. Do we have a lot more work to do? Yes. Are we getting complacent? I think a little bit.”
