Will Grand Theft Auto still rule the world?

TheTools · 15.11.2026 19:43 · source · permalink

At the eastern extremity of Edinburgh’s Old Town two modern buildings stand as next-door neighbours. 

One of them is a parliament with legislative powers affecting every man, woman and child in Scotland. The other building carries far more clout than that.

At best, a few million people are invested in the goings-on at the Scottish parliament. Some 250 yards away at Barclay House, the numbers shift into a whole new stratosphere of influence.

It is the headquarters of gaming giant Rockstar North, which 11 years ago assigned a small group of staff to preliminary work on a new edition of its best-selling product.

The game wasn’t just the company’s top seller. Grand Theft Auto (GTA) 5 was the highest-grossing standalone entertainment product in history.

Since its release in 2013, it has shifted more than three times as many units as the biggest-selling album ever – Michael Jackson’s Thriller – and generated almost three times the box-office take of the biggest movie ever – Avengers: Endgame.

By late 2018, almost all 1,400-plus Edinburgh-based staff were deployed on the follow-up. They formed the creative hub of the project, leading a team of thousands of international programmers across the company’s global network of studios.

As the years rolled by and absolute silence was maintained behind Rockstar’s wall of corporate secrecy, GTA 6 assumed an almost mythical quality. In modern media, no sequel has ever been more eagerly anticipated.

Now the latest instalment in the franchise is only days away from delivery – and a decade of intense, claustrophobic pressure inside the company fortress is spilling out into the open.

For all its staggering influence, the foundations of this digital empire have rarely been stable – and it has never been free from controversy.

The glacial genesis of GTA 6 has played out against a backdrop of competing egos, fractured leadership and bitter legal wrangles. A massive question mark hangs over the company’s tax affairs – and another one over its moral compass. Hardly surprising, perhaps, for a game which wallows in criminality.

The latest flashpoint is a protracted hearing in the Glasgow Tribunals Centre where slices of the raw reality of life in the development studio have been offered up by a succession of disgruntled former employees.

A group of 23 ex-staff are claiming unfair dismissal, arguing they lost their jobs for discussing how to improve their working conditions. Rockstar contends they were sacked for breaching strict confidentiality rules.

There have been protest rallies in Edinburgh and Glasgow, with former staff waving placards designed to shame their ex-employer into rehiring them.

One of them, Jason Lewis, said: ‘None of us imagined that talking to each other about our working conditions would turn our lives upside down and catapult us into the centre of a global media storm.

‘As the world prepares for the release of GTA 6, we are reliving the heartbreak of being thrown out of a project we poured so much of our lives into.’

Another sacked employee, Jack Hoxby, 29, told the Mail: ‘Some of my colleagues were at the company 18 years and then this firing happened… so obviously it is upsetting for them and me, and it’s bittersweet because we’re still seeing our hard work go out there.’

The dispute originates from a 2024 mandate issued by Rockstar compelling all staff to return to full-time office working. Many revolted, signing a petition complaining about the order.

It is claimed that petition formed the basis of a ‘watchlist’ used by bosses to keep tabs on potentially mutinous staff.

But the tribunal’s main battleground is a digital chatroom on the Discord platform, set up by workers to ensure their conversations remained entirely private.

There, frustrations with management were expressed in often profane language.

The tribunal heard the bosses had been branded as ‘cowardly corporate f***s’ in the chatroom. Others railed against ‘toxic management’.

More alarming still – or so the secrecy-obsessed Rockstar claimed – was the chatroom’s security. Its lawyer, Thomas Cordrey, pointed out it had lapsed so severely that former employees who had left to join rival studios remained active members of the forum.

When Rockstar summarily sacked 31 workers in October last year, it maintained the dismissals were strictly for gross misconduct and a catastrophic breach of confidentiality.

The Independent Workers’ Union of Great Britain (IWGB) – representing 23 of the ousted staff – claimed the real motivation was to purge union organisers and thus ward off industrial disputes during the critically important run-up to the game’s release.

Mr Hoxby said that, if anything, the sackings have made the final push towards completion tougher: ‘They definitely wouldn’t have expected the kind of pushback that has resulted from firing us – which, I would argue, has probably made the lead-up to the release more of a challenge.’

The tribunal has heard that a total of 722 posts across 109 Discord threads were passed to human resources staff in 2024 and 2025, and cited in the accusations of gross misconduct against the sacked employees.

But last month the company’s vice president for human resources, Robert Spampinato, told the hearing it was ‘categorically not true’ to suggest he asked staff to keep tabs on union membership at the company.

Mr Spampinato said: ‘We treat people well and we will deal with people fairly and empathetically.’ He said it was not until October 2025 that the company learned who everyone in the Discord server was and that they included a journalist and former employees.

By the time of the sackings, the company was in ‘crisis mode’ and needed its team to be ‘fully focused’.

He said: ‘We were in a moment when we knew we were running late on the development of [GTA 6]. We were actually figuring out the timing of announcing a delay of the game and announcing that to the team.’

As well as accusing Rockstar of ‘union-busting’, the IWGB highlights the company’s contentious tax affairs.

Organiser Fred Carter said: ‘This is a company that benefits from more than £440million in UK tax relief, while displaying a callous and blatant disregard for both the livelihood of workers and the letter of the law.’

The explanation for Rockstar’s massive tax breaks lies in the UK’s Video Game Tax Relief programme which allows developers dramatic reductions in corporation tax bills providing their games can be said to promote British culture.

Many have long wondered how exactly the GTA series does that. Later versions of the game are set in fictional American cities.

As a 2019 report by think-tank TaxWatch observed: ‘It is unlikely that the drafters of that guidance had in mind a game which allows the player to murder prostitutes when formulating the cultural test.’

According to the firm’s UK accounts, it had profits of more than £87million in 2024-25 and a turnover of £508million.

While there is no suggestion the company is breaking the law, one Edinburgh MP, Labour’s Tracy Gilbert, described the situation as ‘Grand Theft Tax’.

Rockstar has argued that its use of tax incentives is a powerful driver of economic growth.

But the political scrutiny and tribunal skirmishes are merely the latest in a series of setbacks which have destabilised the production of GTA 6 almost from day one.

Behind the scenes there was a spectacular fallout between the three ‘Rockstar Principals’, brothers Sam and Dan Houser and Aberdonian game design genius Leslie Benzies.

The Scot, who was president of Rockstar North, acted as the technical anchor behind the whole franchise.

He went on an extended sabbatical in late 2014 but always insisted he intended to return to work. 

When he tried to do so the following year, he found himself locked out of the building. 

Even when he managed to talk his way in, the office manager escorted him out again.

Mr Benzies was ultimately removed from the company with a severance package worth little more than a million, a paltry sum in his eyes considering his key role in the mega-successful GTA 5, and he launched a legal bid to reclaim £110million in what he saw as unpaid royalties.

It rested on a royalty agreement signed by all three in 2008. By then, they were each already rich, but GTA 5 had put them in the super-league.

There were ego clashes and, later, claims from Mr Benzies that Rockstar North was making ‘scurrilous’ allegations about his conduct as a pretext for his dismissal.

One passage in his lawsuit, filed in New York in 2016, claimed: ‘Sam Houser [co-founder of Rockstar Games] himself had orchestrated and encouraged a company culture involving strip clubs, personal photography of employees in sexually compromising positions and other conduct grossly in violation of standard workplace norms.’

A protestor holds up a placard at a rally outside the Glasgow Tribunal Centre

Rockstar Games said the claims were ‘entirely without merit and in many instances downright bizarre’.

Whatever the truth, it was open warfare as work began in earnest to produce the follow-up to GTA 5. One former employee told journalist David Kushner, author of a book on the GTA phenomenon: ‘The money turned them into jackasses very quickly.’

Another said: ‘These people are insanely smart and really good at being mean.’

Ultimately the legal dispute was settled in 2019 but no details were disclosed. Cast out of the empire, Benzies established a rival studio in Edinburgh, Build a Rocket Boy, and enticed several key Rockstar developers into the fold.

They produced a game called MindsEye which, on release last year, proved a critical and commercial disaster.

Some 15 months on, following a string of redundancies and massive financial losses, the company is in administration.

A further blow for Mr Benzies came this February when his name surfaced in unsealed US Department of Justice files tied to the Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking case. In the documents, Epstein survivor Sarah Ransome accused the developer of a drug-fuelled sexual assault dating back to December 2006.

She alleged that Mr Benzies threw banknotes at her before sexually assaulting her. She even claimed he replicated the scene in the GTA series which allows players to attack prostitutes. 

In fact, gamers have been free to subject prostitutes to violence in GTA since 2001.

Mr Benzies accepted that he did have a three-month relationship with his accuser but insisted her remaining claims are false.

If Mr Benzies’s departure represented the loss of GTA’s master builder, the exit of Dan Houser in 2020 stripped it of its voice.

The empire’s London-born co-founder was the lead writer responsible for the satirical edge of every game in the modern 3D series. He walked away abruptly without making clear his reasons.

With his uncompromising pen gone from the franchise, rumours soon began to circulate that GTA 6 would be tame and sanitised in comparison with its predecessors.

There were reports that writers had been instructed to avoid ‘punching down’ at marginalised groups – something of a U-turn for a game whose reputation was built on offending everyone equally.

The widely trailed introduction of a female protagonist called Lucia as a fully playable character heightened suspicions: was GTA about to become ‘woke’?

Those harbouring such fears may take some consolation from the news that the BBC fielded more than 100 complaints after screening a trailer for GTA 6 on BBC Breakfast in August.

Many said it was ‘irresponsible’ to air footage of an 18-rated game featuring violence and sexual content when children could have been watching.

The corporation defended its decision, saying the release of the game was an issue of ‘significant interest’. For some, that is an understatement.

In a speech last year, one of Scotland’s leading lights in the games industry brought some perspective to the scale of the interest in GTA and the nation’s world-leading role in games production.

Dundee-born Chris van der Kuyl of 4J Studios was the man responsible for the console version of Minecraft, another global phenomenon with more than 70 million sales.

It was he who argued that, to the rest of the developed world, the Rockstar North studio was the most important building in Edinburgh.

He said: ‘This game, this cultural phenomenon is the world’s biggest entertainment franchise. This game has taken more time to develop and cost more, somewhere approaching $2billion, than it took to build the Burj Khalifa, the world’s tallest building.

‘Let that settle in for a second. A single video game is a more monumental human endeavour than the largest physical structure we have ever built.

‘That’s what the games industry is. That’s the industry that’s right at the core of many of our businesses. And it’s an industry… in which Scotland leads the world.’

It has been a long and rocky road to the sixth instalment of the world’s best-loved computer game. In its wake it leaves scandals, legal battles, bitter enmities, defections, sackings and more.

Yet even among those with most reason to be furious with the company behind the game, there remains undeniable excitement about its release on Thursday.

‘I always thought it was going to be incredible,’ says Jack Hoxby. ‘So I do think I’d like to play it.’

The politicians at Holyrood may make the laws, but it is their next-door neighbours who rule the world.
