Opinion: Ottawa needs to stop fuelling the underground nicotine market

TheTools · 18.10.2026 10:38 · source · permalink

Treating legal nicotine products as if they were as harmful as smoked tobacco only encourages Canadians to pursue unauthorized suppliers

Canada’s unauthorized market for nicotine pouches is becoming increasingly difficult to ignore. In January 2025, officers at the Cornwall port of entry seized 2,600 cans of nicotine pouches as part of a shipment of products valued at $114,000. In June of last year, officers at the Pigeon River crossing, west of Lake Superior, intercepted 212,000 pouches in a single cube van. By August of this year, seizures had increased to commercial loads measured in tonnes. At the Blue Water Bridge, at the southern end of Lake Huron, officers seized two nicotine pouch shipments valued at more than $3.8 million.

These seizures may count as enforcement successes but they are clear evidence that the government’s current policy on nicotine pouches is failing. Contraband volumes of this magnitude point to established supply chains, sustained consumer demand and substantial profits for operators who evade Canadian law. The irony that the largest shipments are arriving from the United States is hard to miss. At a time when Ottawa is urging us to buy Canadian, its own policies are sending consumer dollars south and costing governments the tax revenue a well-regulated domestic market could generate.

Although the federal government has publicly maintained its current policy position, recent National Post reporting suggests it may revisit its decision to restrict the sale of authorized nicotine pouches to pharmacies. That would be a sensible correction, but it would do little to address the broader market reality.

Canada’s current framework only accommodates nicotine pouches that can be authorized as smoking-cessation therapies, a narrow standard that many of the products driving today’s consumer demand cannot meet. Nicotine products have many uses: helping people to smoke fewer cigarettes, replacing cigarettes in situations where smoking is not possible or desirable, and providing a smoke-free way to consume nicotine. Whichever motivation applies, people moving away from combustible tobacco represents a meaningful public health benefit.

Canada has already recognized that not every nicotine product needs to be regulated as a therapeutic product. When Ottawa established a regulatory framework for vaping in 2018, it did not require every brand to prove it could help people quit smoking. Rather, the government recognized that a consumer market already existed and it created rules governing youth access, product standards, sales, promotion and enforcement.

The same practical approach should apply to nicotine pouches that make no therapeutic claim. Ottawa should create a distinct adult-use category under the Tobacco and Vaping Products Act while preserving the existing pathway for therapeutic products.

The idea is not to weaken safeguards or treat nicotine as risk-free. Nicotine is addictive and young people should not use it — two realities that make effective regulation more important, not less. Rules for a new adult-use category should include: a minimum age of purchase with mandatory age verification; strong product standards and reporting requirements; restrictions on youth-oriented branding and promotion; and meaningful penalties for non-compliance.

More fundamentally, the government needs to acknowledge the reality its current approach has created. Consumers currently have access to just one legal nicotine pouch brand, available only through pharmacies. When legal options do not meet consumer demand, however, that demand does not disappear, it migrates to unauthorized sellers operating outside Canada’s youth protection measures, product standards and taxation framework. And that is exactly what has happened: Canadians can now easily access hundreds of unauthorized products, either online or through informal distribution channels.

Letting authorized cessation pouches be sold in convenience stores would improve access for adults trying to quit smoking. But it would not address the separate market that sits outside the smoking-cessation system, in which demand is growing. Ottawa faces a choice: regulate this category in a way that protects young people, establishes product standards, and generates tax revenue, or watch the unauthorized market continue to squeeze out the legal one.

Jasmine Odlin is director of external affairs at Nuvona, part of Altria Group, a global producer of nicotine pouch products.
