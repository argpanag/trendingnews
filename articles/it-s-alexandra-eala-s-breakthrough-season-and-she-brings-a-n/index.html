It’s Alexandra Eala’s breakthrough season. And she brings a nation with her | CNN

TheTools · 11.11.2026 01:35 · source · permalink

Hues of royal blue, red, white, and yellow flashed at every corner of the tennis court as the crowd rallied around Alexandra Eala.

For a sport constantly looking for the next generational icon, Eala has become a unifying touchstone for an archipelagic nation of over 110 million people – with many of them scattered across the globe.

The left-handed Filipina player had a whirlwind breakout season after defeating several top women’s stars. She ousted defending Wimbledon title holder Iga Świątek in the third round of the grass court major this summer. In August, she then beat Jessica Pegula in the Washington Open to become the first player from the Philippines to win a WTA Tour singles title.

Eala started this year as world No. 53. She’s now ranked 15th, a career-high after she made the final at the Prudential Hong Kong Tennis Open in her last tournament of the season.

The distinction means the 21-year-old is now the highest-ranked Filipino player and Southeast Asian in WTA history.

The Filipino talent has already shown she can beat some of the sport’s biggest names, but with every new game, set and match on tour, it is inevitably harder to stay at the top.

“I think that the expectation rises, but so does the demand in terms of what the sport demands of you and what the level demands of you,” Eala told CNN Sports.

Though she very well may still be asking herself if she can be among the greatest in the sport, Eala knows she’s working towards that consistency and getting better.

“I just look forward to having another season full of hardship, full of joy, and hopefully full of success,” she said with pensive confidence.

Though her bid for another WTA title fell short in Hong Kong, the loss has done little to dim her breakout year.

“This season has changed my life,” Eala told the crowd as tears welled up following her loss to Claire Liu in the Hong Kong Open final.

“It has changed my life for the better. I am incredibly proud of the work we have put in this year. We are the only ones who truly knew what it took to arrive at this point in our journey.”

Eala and Liu had found themselves locked in a fierce, early back-and-forth at Victoria Park in Hong Kong, but the 26-year-old American soon switched gears and took control – at one point winning nine consecutive games – to seal a 6-3, 6-0 victory in an hour and 33 minutes to claim her first-ever WTA title.

“I can’t speak for everyone, but playing against Alex is one of the hardest things because of the crowd. No matter where she goes, she always has so many supporters, which I think is really great in general for tennis,” Liu said in a post-tournament presser.

Beyond the breakthrough

Wherever she played, Filipino fans have clamored for tickets to cheer Eala on, bringing a sense of home to a life spent traveling the global tennis circuit.

Eala’s growing popularity has even seen her swing from the tennis court to haute couture. In September, the Filipino phenom sat front row alongside Anna Wintour at New York Fashion Week’s Michael Kors and Tory Burch shows.

Tennis courts transform into homecoming celebrations when Eala makes an appearance. And it was no exception in Hong Kong, which is home to over 200,000 Filipinos, many of them who work as Overseas Filipino Workers (OFWs).

“We idolize her for her resilience, same as an OFWs who never give up,” Lids Romero, a domestic worker who spent her only day off on Sunday to watch the final at the official fan zone, told CNN Sports.

Moy Volante, who flew in with his family from the Philippines to watch the tournament, told CNN Sports he admired Eala’s determination to push through, even in tough battles, which he hopes his daughter can embody.

“You can really feel the unity and support of Filipinos,” Volante said of the atmosphere throughout the week.

“I love their passion,” Eala said fondly of her fans. “They really support me without fail in so many tournaments.”

Crowds of Filipino domestic workers, young professionals and school children all came out to Causeway Bay to watch Eala in action, even as her matches stretched late into the night over a regular school week.

Young female fans were all over Victoria Park with some holding placards proclaiming, “Let’s Go! Alex” at the fan zone, while others in the stands waved colorful, hand-drawn banners.

“I want them to get inspiration from Alex,” Marlon Avines who was at the park with his three daughters, said to CNN Sports. “The grit, persistence and giving pride to the country.”

Eala has encouraged girls and young women who look up to her “to pursue whatever makes them happy.”

“I think it’s better to go through the hardships doing what you love, and you can always come back to that when things get difficult,” Eala told CNN Sports.

The young, rising star is no stranger to hardships.

She began training in tennis as a preschooler, coached by her late grandfather, who she affectionately calls “Lolo Bob.”

What started as a family pastime soon grew into a relentless pursuit, which Eala once described on Maria Sharapova’s podcast as an obsession like having a “crush on perfection.”

In 2018, as a 12-year-old wild card, she won Les Petits As – a tournament widely considered to be the preeminent under-14 competition in the world – in the process becoming a talent to watch.

A few months later – after turning 13 – she left Manila for Mallorca to train at the Rafa Nadal Academy in Spain on a scholarship.

Eala began to flourish on the international junior circuit, winning her first junior grand slam title at the 2020 Australian Open in girls’ doubles. Just two years later, she became the first Filipina to lift a junior grand slam singles trophy at the US Open in 2022.

Along with that junior success, she officially turned pro in March 2020.

While Eala’s stature in the sport grows, what remains the same is the standard of discipline she sets for herself.

“I’ve learned to push my limits,” Eala told CNN Sports. “I’ve learned a lot about what my limits are … and the bar can always be raised.”
