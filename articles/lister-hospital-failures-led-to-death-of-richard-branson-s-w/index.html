Lister Hospital failures led to death of Richard Branson’s wife

TheTools · 09.09.2026 23:07 · source · permalink

Doctor admits he should have given anti-coagulants to patient before she suffered fatal blood clot

A private hospital’s failure to provide preventative medication contributed to the death of Sir Richard Branson’s wife, an inquest has concluded.

Lady Branson died aged 80 on Nov 24 last year after suffering a cardiac arrest at the Lister Hospital in Chelsea, west London. The hospital is owned and operated by HCA Healthcare.

Westminster coroner’s court heard that she died after suffering a pulmonary embolism, a sudden blockage in a lung artery, resulting from deep-vein thrombosis.

Lady Branson was admitted to the hospital on Nov 17, weeks after suffering fractures of the pelvis and the hip in a fall on Necker Island, the private retreat in the Caribbean where she married Sir Richard in 1989.

Fiona Wilcox, senior coroner for Inner West London, said Lady Branson did not receive prophylactic anticoagulants at the hospital despite a “very high risk of developing deep-vein thrombosis”.

Giving her conclusion, the coroner said: “I do find her death would have likely been prevented, and the lack of anticoagulation did cause or contribute to her death probably.”

Earlier on Wednesday, witness Dr George Adams, a consultant haematologist at North West London NHS Hospitals Trust, told the court that the drugs reduced the risk of a blood clot.

Asked by the coroner whether Lady Branson had been “appropriately treated”, he agreed that anticoagulant drugs “would probably have prevented her death”.

He added that failure to apply compression stockings to Lady Branson’s legs in the first 48 hours of her admission might have made “a small contribution to the death”.

In an email sent to the court, the couple’s daughter, Dr Holly Branson, said her mother had a history of blood clots dating back to 2010 and would not have refused anticoagulants were they offered.

“Had I been aware she was not receiving Enoxaparin injections, I would have been very concerned given her recent long haul travel and medical history,” she wrote.

Dr Branson said her mother had not been given the anti-clot stockings to wear for up to 48 hours after her admission to hospital.

“I noticed she was not wearing them and asked the nurses,” she said. “My impression from the doctors was that this was an oversight on their part.”

Dr Basir Kunduzi, who was on duty at the Lister Hospital when Lady Branson was admitted was asked by the coroner whether he thought she should have been given anti-coagulant injections.

“I thought she should have been,” he told the court, as he accepted it was his responsibility to prescribe the drugs.

Asked to explain why he did not give the drugs, he said: “Since Lady Branson had a fall and had been mobilised since her fall, there was a risk of bleeding.”

He said he decided to delay prescribing anti-coagulants until he had organised a CT scan to ensure there was no bleeding, which the drugs could have worsened.

But he said the decision not to administer the anti-coagulants to Lady Branson would live with him for the rest of his life, adding: “I would like to express my deepest condolences to Lady Branson’s family.”

In a statement read at the inquest by the coroner earlier, family members described Lady Branson as a “deeply loved wife, mother, grandmother and friend” who was “known for her warmth, kindness and unwavering compassion for others”.

On Instagram, Sir Richard described his wife as the “most wonderful mum and grandmum”, writing: “She was my best friend, my rock, my guiding light, my world. Love you forever Joan x.”
