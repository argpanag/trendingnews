Competition Bureau calls on provinces to introduce legislation limiting use of non-competes

TheTools · 29.10.2026 15:25 · source · permalink

Jeanne Pratt, Canada's interim competition commissioner, wrote in a letter to labour ministers that the breadth of non-compete use in Canada is 'concerning.'Justin Tang/The Canadian Press

The Competition Bureau is calling on provinces and territories to introduce legislation that would restrict the use of non-compete agreements in employment contracts, saying that they are creating unnecessary barriers to people switching jobs.

In a letter addressed to the labour ministers of 12 provinces and territories, the Competition Bureau’s interim commissioner, Jeanne Pratt, said that non-competes are unusually prevalent in Canada and their overuse is harming workers and the economy by reducing wages and job mobility, and discouraging entrepreneurship. Ontario was not included because it banned the use of non-competes in employment contracts in December, 2021, except for very senior executives or in certain circumstances where a business is sold.

“Ontario already prohibits most non-competes, subject to defined exceptions, and the federal government has proposed similar limits in federally regulated workplaces. The Competition Bureau encourages other provincial and territorial governments to consider taking similar action,” Ms. Pratt wrote.

An Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development survey of employers and employees conducted in 2025 found that as many as 39 per cent of private-sector employees are covered by a non-compete agreement, making Canada one of the highest-prevalence countries surveyed. The report estimated that several million Canadians may be bound by non-competes, including 13 per cent to 25 per cent of workers with no access to confidential information and 15 per cent to 26 per cent of low-wage workers (earning less than $3,000 per month).

The idea of including a non-compete clause in employment contracts is to prevent senior employees who possess sensitive business information from taking that information with them to other employers. The non-compete clause bars employees from moving to another job within a certain time frame, often months. The Competition Bureau noted that among Canadian firms using non-competes, 30 per cent apply them to every employee, regardless of their role or seniority.

“This breadth of use is concerning,” wrote Ms. Pratt.

Pedestrians walk through Toronto's financial district last month. An OECD report estimates that several million Canadians may be bound by non-compete agreements.Will Cox/The Globe and Mail

At the federal level, changes to the Canada Labour Code were proposed in May, 2026, through the tabling of Bill C-31, which would ban non-competes in federally-regulated workplaces, other than in exceptional circumstances. The bill is still making its way through Parliament.

In the letter, Ms. Pratt noted that given the widespread use of non-competes in the Canadian work force, even modest effects on employee behaviour could result in the loss of a substantial number of job and entrepreneurship opportunities. “Greater clarity and consistency in employment protections would make the rules more transparent for workers, facilitate compliance by employers and extend the benefits of pro-competitive reform across Canada,” she said.

There is a high bar for a court to enforce a non-compete agreement, because an employer would have to justify why an employee should be restricted from finding a new job and whether the employee actually possesses sensitive business information. But often, employees are unaware of their rights and whether a clause will actually hold up in court, so they end up complying with non-competes that would most likely be legally unenforceable.

In the wake of the non-compete ban in Ontario, some employers have pivoted to using non-solicitation agreements, which prevents a former employee from actively poaching clients for a certain time period. Some corporate employees are also asked to sign non-disclosure agreements when they are laid off or leave a firm, agreeing to not publicly share confidential information or proprietary data.
