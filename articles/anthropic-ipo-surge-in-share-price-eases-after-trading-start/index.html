Anthropic IPO: Surge in share price eases after trading started with firm valued ~$2tn

TheTools · 19.11.2026 20:22 · source · permalink

Summary

  • AI firm Anthropic is being listed publicly for the first time on Thursday on the Nasdaq stock market in New York

  • The company's share price started at $111.50, which at that stage gave it a market value close to $2tn - prices have since dropped back slightly to nearer $100 a share

  • That's still a steep jump from the $83 set last night - take a closer look at some of the numbers here

  • Anthropic, the company behind AI chatbot Claude, raised a record $81bn ahead of its stock market debut

  • The firm's flotation comes at a crucial time when many are wondering whether the AI industry's bubble is showing signs of bursting, our business editor writes

  • The seven founders who set up Anthropic in 2021 have now become multi-billionaires - we've looked at why private companies decide to sell shares publicly here

AI firm Anthropic is being listed publicly for the first time on Thursday on the Nasdaq stock market in New York

The company's share price started at $111.50, which at that stage gave it a market value close to $2tn - prices have since dropped back slightly to nearer $100 a share

That's still a steep jump from the $83 set last night - take a closer look at some of the numbers here

Anthropic, the company behind AI chatbot Claude, raised a record $81bn ahead of its stock market debut

The firm's flotation comes at a crucial time when many are wondering whether the AI industry's bubble is showing signs of bursting, our business editor writes

The seven founders who set up Anthropic in 2021 have now become multi-billionaires - we've looked at why private companies decide to sell shares publicly here

  2. Anthropic is on a collision course with data centre opponentspublished at 19:49 GMT
    19:49 GMT

    Bernd Debusmann Jr
    White House reporter in Washington DC

    When Americans cast their ballots in the midterm elections earlier this month, they made one thing abundantly clear: they do not like data centres.

    In 40 of 41 ballot initiatives - public votes to pass new laws - on data centres across the US, voters opted to limit or prevent their development.

    Only in Harwood, North Dakota, did they support a pro-data centre measure.

    Anthropic, however, has already signed contracts totalling over a half trillion dollars in AI infrastructure over the next two decades, adding to its already substantial investments.

    In other words, its post-IPO plans put it on a clear collision course with Americans who are against data centres.

    When it comes to existing contracts, there is little indication that public opinion will be able to change much, and lawmakers have expressed little appetite for reversing agreements that have already been signed.

    But any new Anthropic AI infrastructure projects will likely come up against hostile residents and elected officials who fear being punished at the polls in subsequent elections.

  3. Democrats and Republicans react to Anthropic IPOpublished at 19:34 GMT
    19:34 GMT

    Rebecka Pieder
    Reporting from Washington DC

    US lawmakers are turning to social media to react to Anthropic's public listing.

    "I’m alarmed that Anthropic is barrelling ahead with its IPO today," says Democratic Congresswoman Pramila Jayapal on X, adding that going public “rewards” the company’s “reckless pace”.

    Democratic Senator Chris van Hollen calls the IPO "great for the billionaire class but extremely dangerous for humanity", while fellow Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren calls on Congress to “act quickly to put in place safeguards on advanced AI development".

    Democrats are set to hold majorities in both chambers of Congress come January, giving them the power to potentially enact new AI legislation or restrict data centre expansion.

    Meanwhile, Republican congressman Andrew Garbarino, lauds AI as an essential tool to “stay ahead” of bad actors, in a post on X. And Rich McCormick, another House Republican, writes that “America wins the AI race by unleashing American innovation", adding: "We cannot afford to let Washington decide who gets to compete."

  5. The challenges that still lie ahead for Anthropicpublished at 18:38 GMT
    18:38 GMT

    Jonathan Josephs
    BBC business reporter

    Building one of the world's most valuable companies as quickly as Anthropic is a risky business.

    As a publicly traded firm, there will be even more scrutiny on how it manages those challenges now. The company seems to know that - risk factors take up about a third of its IPO prospectus.

    That includes well documented issues around the safety of AI and it being a competitive industry, as well as being caught up in the US-China trade war.

    On top of that, it points out that getting hold of enough computing power will be key.

    Another risk to the growing company is the huge amounts of power and other resources that the data centres use that sit behind Claude.

    Then there are the more prosaic challenges any publicly traded company faces - and also an acknowledgement that its governance structure could put off "certain institutional investors".

    That appears to be a reference to the fact that the founders including Dario Amodei retain 50.1% of the voting rights.

    Thanks to their voting rights, the founders will have a vital say in just how much risk the company takes in pursuit of growth.

  6. Anthropic's IPO is the largest in historypublished at 18:28 GMT
    18:28 GMT

    Anthropic raised a record-breaking $81bn (£62bn) before it's stock market debut earlier today, making it the largest IPO in history.

    How do we know? Before a company goes public, it enters into negotiations with banks and investors over purchasing shares at an agreed upon price, which, in Anthropic's case was $83 per share.

    As a reminder, an IPO is an 'initial public offering' and is the short-hand term for a private company debuting on the stock-market, allowing the public to purchase shares.

  7. Investors expect volatility in coming weeks as first AI frontier firm goes publicpublished at 18:03 GMT
    18:03 GMT

    Rachel Clun
    Business reporter

    We’ve been reporting on Anthropic’s share price moving around quite a bit in early trading, and analysts expect that to continue.

    Ben Barringer, head of technology research at Quilter Cheviot, says in its initial public offering (IPO), Anthropic seems to have been a success.

    “However, as with many IPOs, the stock is likely to be volatile in the days and weeks afterwards as that initial exuberance wears off," he says.

    Neil Wilson, an investor strategist at Saxo UK, says this public listing is "the Big One, the key test of whether it's the beginning of the end of the AI bubble".

    "Its success or failure will have huge ramifications for the wider AI ecosystem and in particular the timing for OpenAI's own delayed public listing," he said.

    "The question we ask is simple: is it worth it? Can Anthropic justify at $1.5tn valuation, or $2tn for that matter?"

    One key issue for investors, according to Wilson, is that "there's no obvious peer to compare it to".

  10. Anthropic workers have an unusual ability to sell shares todaypublished at 17:32 GMT
    17:32 GMT

    Kali Hays
    US technology reporter

    Everyone who works at Anthropic will not become a millionaire or billionaire today. But most of the company's employees will certainly have reason to celebrate.

    In Anthropic's updated prospectus, it said employees would be able to sell up to 10% of their shares in the company on the first day of trading. They can sell another 35% next year, after Anthropic reports its first full-year results to the public.

    That's a lot of liquidity for Anthropic employees who have received equity in the company, referred to as restricted stock units (RSUs) as part of their compensation packages.

    Companies often prevent workers who hold stock from selling it off for three to six months, in order to avoid too many shares hitting the market too quickly. That would likely drag down the share price.

    Anthropic is apparently not too worried about that.

    How much money an Anthropic employee will make today, however, depends on when they received their RSUs. The earlier they started, the lower Anthropic's valuation was.

    More shares at a lower valuation means they have much more to gain today.

  11. Can Anthropic's record-breaking IPO stop the AI bubble bursting?published at 17:20 GMT
    17:20 GMT

    Simon Jack
    Business editor

    Anthropic's IPO comes at a crucial time when many are wondering whether the industry's bubble is showing signs of bursting.

    Where once investors were "all in" on a technological revolution to rival the railroads, some are now hedging their bets.

    It comes amid arguments that AI prices will be driven down as the technology becomes more common and concerns that a hefty wedge of the AI investment is tech giants investing in each other.

    What a company is worth depends almost entirely on predicted future earnings. Will current and future users of AI be prepared to pay enough to justify the trillions being spent on the chips, data centres, energy and employees?

    There is also a growing public backlash against a technology that threatens to replace millions of jobs and hoover up vast amounts of energy and water.

    But for all the concerns, the hype and the FOMO (fear of missing out) on an undoubtedly profound moment in technological and perhaps human development means that buying interest is high.

  13. Excitement for Anthropic immediately sends shares up more than 30%published at 17:08 GMT
    17:08 GMT

    Kali Hays
    US technology reporter

    As many investors and market experts expected, Anthropic shares flew above its $83 allocation price as soon as they hit the open market.

    While Anthropic is a unique company to go public - it is the first true AI company to be listed on any stock exchange - a jump in share price the day of an IPO is pretty typical for notable tech companies.

    When Twitter went public, for instance, it went from $26 to $45 on its first day. When Facebook hit the market the jump was similar. SpaceX, too, went from $150 a share to $160 a share.

    After weeks, if not months, of a company teasing its listing, talking about how well its business is doing, and often media reports digging into the company's future, individual investors are excited. That excitement sends demand soaring for a limited number of shares.

    What is also pretty typical: a fall in price in the weeks or months that follow an IPO.

    16:55 GMT

    Michelle Fleury

    Anthropic is becoming something of a barometer for investor confidence in the AI industry and for how much appetite there is for AI stocks.

  18. Anthropic listing comes as Federal Reserve expresses concern about growth of AI debtpublished at 16:20 GMT
    16:20 GMT

    Rachel Clun
    Business reporter

    AI financing was a key feature of the Federal Reserve Bank’s last meeting - more broadly, there have been growing concerns about the large amounts of debt being taken on in the AI space.

    According to minutes from the Fed’s October meeting, where it decided to hold rates steady, the central bank noted concerns about the rapid growth of AI-related debt which was being financed through "complex and opaque" structures.

    "Almost all participants commented on risks associated with a scenario in which the returns on AI investment turned out to be disappointing to investors," the bank said.

    A few of the meeting’s participants also noted that "AI-related exposures were becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms".

  19. Is Anthropic's IPO compatible with its 'safety-focused' constitution?published at 16:11 GMT
    16:11 GMT

    Freya Scott-Turner
    Live reporter

    Founded in 2021 by seven ex-employees of OpenAI, Anthropic has cultivated something of a reputation as "the good guy" of AI.

    "If powerful AI is coming regardless, Anthropic believes it’s better to have safety-focused labs at the frontier," says its constitution.

    But recently, former employees have accused Anthropic of neglecting this mission - including a researcher who previously told the BBC that workers were "genuinely frightened" about the speed of its advancements.

    Dr Eleanor Drage, a senior AI ethics researcher at the University of Cambridge, says the idea that Anthropic needs to win an "AI arms race" and out-build competitors in the name of responsibility is "a kind of hype fest" and a "narrative" to justify a risky pace of development.

    The problem is the "culture of development", Drage tells me. AI companies "don't want to [slow down], because they don't know how. They're so terrified they'll lose their competitive edge".

    Because public shareholders enter the mix, IPOs bring in a larger group of people who want to see big financial returns. Is this compatible with safety?

    "We have to believe that there is a compromise," says Drage, who notes that engineers say that safe AI development is possible if we're "temperate and moderate" about the systems we build. "But that's just not what's happening," she adds.

  20. Anthropic determined to be the 'good guys' of AIpublished at 16:06 GMT
    16:06 GMT

    Imran Rahman-Jones
    Technology reporter

    While it wouldn’t explicitly say it out loud, Anthropic has always appeared keen to position itself as the "good guys" of the AI world.

    The company is set up as a Public Benefit Corporation, which means it tries to balance shareholder interest with its responsible AI charter.

    It has an unusual corporate structure, with a “Long-Term Benefit Trust” overseeing the company’s board to make sure it is considering the public interest.

    Anthropic also has its own in-house philosopher, Amanda Askell, who has had a big say over Claude’s personality and the company’s constitution.

    Defenders of the company would point to its spat with the US military over the use of its products in combat situations as an example of where responsibility was prioritised over profit.

    But critics question how the firm’s constant catastrophic language tallies with its continued development of ever-advanced models.

AI financing was a key feature of the Federal Reserve Bank’s last meeting - more broadly, there have been growing concerns about the large amounts of debt being taken on in the AI space.

According to minutes from the Fed’s October meeting, where it decided to hold rates steady, the central bank noted concerns about the rapid growth of AI-related debt which was being financed through "complex and opaque" structures.

"Almost all participants commented on risks associated with a scenario in which the returns on AI investment turned out to be disappointing to investors," the bank said.

A few of the meeting’s participants also noted that "AI-related exposures were becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms".

Is Anthropic's IPO compatible with its 'safety-focused' constitution?published at 16:11 GMT
16:11 GMT

Freya Scott-Turner
Live reporter

Dr Eleanor Drage says that safe development by major AI companies is possible but it needs to be done at "moderate" pace

Founded in 2021 by seven ex-employees of OpenAI, Anthropic has cultivated something of a reputation as "the good guy" of AI.

"If powerful AI is coming regardless, Anthropic believes it’s better to have safety-focused labs at the frontier," says its constitution.

But recently, former employees have accused Anthropic of neglecting this mission - including a researcher who previously told the BBC that workers were "genuinely frightened" about the speed of its advancements.

Dr Eleanor Drage, a senior AI ethics researcher at the University of Cambridge, says the idea that Anthropic needs to win an "AI arms race" and out-build competitors in the name of responsibility is "a kind of hype fest" and a "narrative" to justify a risky pace of development.

The problem is the "culture of development", Drage tells me. AI companies "don't want to [slow down], because they don't know how. They're so terrified they'll lose their competitive edge".

Because public shareholders enter the mix, IPOs bring in a larger group of people who want to see big financial returns. Is this compatible with safety?

"We have to believe that there is a compromise," says Drage, who notes that engineers say that safe AI development is possible if we're "temperate and moderate" about the systems we build. "But that's just not what's happening," she adds.

While it wouldn’t explicitly say it out loud, Anthropic has always appeared keen to position itself as the "good guys" of the AI world.

The company is set up as a Public Benefit Corporation, which means it tries to balance shareholder interest with its responsible AI charter.

It has an unusual corporate structure, with a “Long-Term Benefit Trust” overseeing the company’s board to make sure it is considering the public interest.

Anthropic also has its own in-house philosopher, Amanda Askell, who has had a big say over Claude’s personality and the company’s constitution.

Defenders of the company would point to its spat with the US military over the use of its products in combat situations as an example of where responsibility was prioritised over profit.

But critics question how the firm’s constant catastrophic language tallies with its continued development of ever-advanced models.
