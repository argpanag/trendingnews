Fall begins today, but does that mean frost or freeze is imminent?

TheTools · 22.09.2026 16:20 · source · permalink

Astronomical Fall begins today, but we have had fall like temperatures present across our area persistently for almost 2 weeks now. Temperatures are barely forecast to reach 60 degrees today (Tuesday), with below-average temperatures expected through the remainder of the workweek.

Astronomical Fall is almost never a hard cutoff of summer to fall itself, and it’s only by coincidence that a strong cold front would bring the first bout of fall like temperatures on the same day as the Autumnal Equinox. You can just as easily see colder temperatures arrive well before the start of astronomical fall, as we have the last 2 years, or well after. However, every year, Astronomical Fall marks the time when the sun angle is directly positioned over the equator, delivering almost equal daylight to both the Northern and Southern Hemisphere’s.

Going forward, our day length continues to shorten, leading to longer nights than days over the North Pole. This means more of the suns energy (heat) is let back off into space, allowing colder temperatures to build within the Arctic Circle. As this air builds, it is more susceptible to being whisked south by stronger fronts, dependent on the positioning of the jet stream.

As we delve deeper and deeper into the season, we are more and more likely to see these colder intrusions locally. Typically, our first freeze (first time reaching 32 degrees or lower) takes place right around now, in late-September to early-October. Will this happen this year? Probably not.

While frost has taken place across Northern Wisconsin and Minnesota once or two already, warmer temperatures, thanks to building ridges of high pressure off west, are expected to dominate our weather pattern into early October. This should keep the freezing/subfreezing temperatures away from our local area for another 1-2 weeks barring any significant changes and is why the Climate Prediction Center is issuing higher local odds of above-average temperatures.

What does this mean for fall colors? It turns out that the changing of leaf color is more tied to shorter days as opposed to temperatures for many trees, but the amount of sunshine in the fall is also a contributor. Colder temperatures at night after sunny days do help bring about a more vibrant color display but are not the sole factor in trees changing colors.

We have seen the cooler temperatures lately but have seen very little sun. This is not something that is expected to change anytime, with ample cloud cover in the forecast. And, as mentioned before, overnight low temperatures after tonight will warm up through the weekend, back into the mid-upper 50’s. With that said, it’s more likely that color change will be tied to shorter days as opposed to the weather conditions. Because of this, at least so far, Minnesota has been a bit behind and/or duller when it comes to color change. This could mean a later peak, but only time can truly be the judge of that.
