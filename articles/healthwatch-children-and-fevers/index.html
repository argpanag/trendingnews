HealthWatch - Children and fevers

TheTools · 11.09.2026 06:04 · source · permalink

RAPID CITY, S.D. (KOTA) - It’s a challenge for every parent, knowing just how concerned to be when their child is sick, especially if they’re running a fever. Dr. Cara Hamilton with Black Hills Pediatrics has tips in this week’s edition of HealthWatch.

Fevers are part of the immune system’s process

“Today, I’m here to rebrand fevers. Fevers aren’t all bad. In fact, they’re part of your immune system’s process of fighting off infection. But pay attention til the end because I have a few exceptions,” Hamilton said.

“Your immune system, think of it as having a thermostat. When your body gets sick with a virus or a bacteria, your body will set the thermostat up higher to maybe 103. As your or your child’s temperature is rising, it’s still under 103. So they feel cold. They might pull blankets up over them. They might be chilly and shivering until they reach 103. If they never reach 103 before the fever breaks, then they might start sweating and pulling those blankets back off of them again,” Hamilton said.

“And this is kind of a normal fever cycle and process. This is your body’s defense mechanism. One of the many things that it does to try to fight off infection. Your body’s not going to make a dangerous temperature for you or your child. And that’s not to say that consequences couldn’t arise from fevers like rashes or febrile seizures, but those are really difficult to prevent. So don’t worry yourself about that,” Hamilton said.

When to watch more closely

“The fever that doesn’t come along with a runny nose, a cough or congestion, that might be something worth looking into. More likely to be a bacterial infection or something a little more serious. The fever that did start with a cold, runny nose and cough, but lasted a little longer, maybe four or five days, or improved and then got worse again, could be a sign of a secondary infection,” Hamilton said.

“And the other cases that I want you to pay attention to are when your child has any difficulty breathing or lethargy with their fevers. If they can’t stay hydrated because they’re not feeling well, those would be the signs and symptoms I’d worry more about than the fever itself,” Hamilton said.

“When fevers themselves matter, no matter what, would be in those immunosuppressed patients or in kids who are less than two months. These kids are at higher risk of certain types of infections that could be more serious and so would be good to get checked out no matter what. But most other cases, it’s not as nearly big of a deal as we’ve always thought it was,” Hamilton said.

“So if you have questions about fevers, make sure you’re talking to your pediatrician,” Hamilton said.
