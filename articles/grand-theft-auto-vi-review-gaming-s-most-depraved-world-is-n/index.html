Grand Theft Auto VI review – gaming’s most depraved world is now also its greatest love story

TheTools · 18.11.2026 11:00 · source · permalink

With realism that defies belief, this vast game easily accommodates its trademark foulmouthed, OTT chaos – as well as new empathy for the fugitive couple at its centre

  • Tell us: are you playing Grand Theft Auto VI?

Tell us: are you playing Grand Theft Auto VI?

In GTA VI, Rockstar has created the most extraordinarily, indulgently detailed virtual world that we have yet seen. Reviewing it feels a bit like trying to review, say, Paris: it contains so many potential experiences that it’s hard to know where to start. If you had been spying on me playing this game over the past few weeks you may well have caught me robbing banks, shooting up a weed farm or tearing along the highway at sunset blaring Bad Bunny on the in-game radio, but you might equally have caught me doing squats at the gym, shopping, assembling a coffee table or walking along the beach eating ice-cream. There is such a multiplicity of things on offer here that it is difficult to nail down what this game definitively is. Is it an interactive crime movie? A city simulator? A driving game? A sort of absurdly expansive version of The Sims in which the people you’re dressing up and looking after just happen to be professional criminals? It will be different for everyone – that is the beauty of it.

For me, though, this game is about Jason and Lucia, the fugitive couple through whom you experience all that Leonida has to offer. Jason is muscular, handsome, ex-military, steady and unflappable, sincere but spare with words. Lucia has been boosting cars since the age of 14, has a wicked sense of humour and takes zero bullshit. (Lucia is the scarier one; I found myself turning much more quickly to violence when playing as her.) At the start, Lucia is incarcerated and Jason is making a go of things in the sunbeaten Keys, working for a dodgy guy down on the marina and renting a run-down house on stilts with a jetty round the back and a pet snake. When Lucia gets out of prison, the two of them try to make some kind of life for themselves – but before long they get caught up with the wrong people and have to flee the cosy life they’ve just started building.

For a long time you really are two-bit criminals on the run, getting in with every dirtbag petty thief and drug-runner you come across in order to scrape some cash together and stay under the radar. You meet hillbilly pill-heads and biker gangs, friendly conspiracy theorists, a shady doctor, Lucia’s former prison friends and their lowlife boyfriends. By the time I actually got to Vice City – a glitzy den of sin, the centre of all Leonida’s pleasures and all its most extravagant criminality, where the gangbangers hustle on social media and even the narcos are obsessed with their Instagram clout – I had been playing for 30 hours. It took me 70 hours to finish the game and I could have taken longer.

Leonida is so lifelike that at times it beggars belief, so vast that it feels overwhelming, and outside the story missions you can do whatever you please. There are nightclubs, gyms, flea markets, malls, restaurants, clothes shops, basketball courts, shooting ranges, aircraft hangars, projects and mansions, places to hunt and fish or go kayaking, gas stations and thousands of individuals engaged in conversation as you pass by. Interesting things are always happening in your peripheral vision. You might witness the cops chasing someone down to plant drugs on them, or overhear an interesting confrontation, or see a car speeding away and wonder where it’s going – though interesting things always seemed to happen when I was driving a stolen car at high speed towards a discerning buyer, or otherwise unable to stop and follow my nose. Thankfully, another opportunity would always present itself.

I often felt that the only thing messing up Rockstar’s perfect simulation was me. All this detail and realism comes with some disadvantages, namely complex controls and a tendency for the game’s systems to collide with my intentions in unexpected and sometimes annoying ways. Here I am, driving at high speed through the city before clipping the kerb and flipping my car, causing a massive pile-up. Here I am pressing the wrong button and accidentally punching a random passerby rather than greeting them as intended, kicking off a ridiculous skirmish in which I have to knock out half the customers at a restaurant before running from the cops. Here I am again pressing the wrong button at a bike rack and ending up pinned beneath a tumbling row of bicycles. Missions can go south very quickly and for reasons that aren’t always obvious. In every street race I wiped out spectacularly by crashing into something I couldn’t properly see, while my opponents magically recovered from every collision and sped ahead. This can be a frustrating game at times: unpredictable, laborious, unwieldy and, occasionally, unfair. That is because you are not the master of the game world. You simply exist within it.

Like most compelling crime fiction, Jason and Lucia’s story involves an escalating series of messy events that pull them further and further from where they started, and have you constantly wondering how on earth they’re going to get out of the newest trouble they’re in. To avoid spoilers, substantive discussion of this story can only happen in the coming weeks. But I will say that I was rooting so hard for Jason and Lucia by the end that I felt quite sick with worry about what was going to happen to them, pacing back and forth in front of my TV throughout the final missions. Among all the expectations I had for GTA VI, I did not expect it to make me so emotional.

The key to this is the bond you form with Jason and Lucia. After tens of hours in their company, watching them together – how she smiles fondly at him when they dance together, their playful banter, the stick-ups they pull off as a duo, their resilience and faith in themselves and each other in the face of escalating disaster – I found it impossible not to care for them. They are our avatars in GTA VI’s world but they are not blank stand-ins for the player: you decide their actions but not their personalities. They feel like real people rather than puppets dancing to your whim. It made me care about them much more. When the story gave me opportunities to decide what would happen next, what came to mind for me was what they would do, not what I would do.

This is indicative of a tonal shift for Grand Theft Auto that became more obvious the more I played. This series is famous for its violence, misanthropy and pitch-black humour. I have been invested in its characters before, have even rooted for some of them, but I have rarely liked them or related to them. This is the first GTA that wants to encourage some measure of empathy alongside its trademark violent chaos. I was astonished to discover that the game offers you small incentives for acts of kindness, such as texting your partner a selfie, doing your neighbour a favour or throwing your rubbish in the bin rather than dropping it on the ground. Jason and Lucia are not extravagant caricatures, but two people trying to make it in a world totally stacked against them. Pretty much everyone in this Floridian fever-dream is a total crook, on the take or mixed up in some bullshit scheme. It’s so easy to get away with crime in this city, and so difficult to do things the right way. The events of this game might be totally outlandish but these characters and their motivations keep things at least somewhat grounded.

If you thought this newfound heart might tone down GTA’s 18-rated provocativeness, however, you would be very wrong. The protagonists may be surprisingly relatable but they exist in a world that is absurdly foul-mouthed, filthy and over-the-top offensive in every imaginable way. I have never played a game that has more thoroughly earned its 18 rating. Barely a half-hour ever goes by without someone snorting coke off a knife blade, or some full-frontal nudity. There are missions set inside a pornographer’s mansion and a small-town strip club; you can visit those clubs and get a private dance (as either Jason or Lucia, for what it’s worth, or together). At one point Lucia opens the wrong hotel room door to see a woman in a corset wielding a golf club, thwacking balls at a guy’s testicles. On the in-game TV there is a reality show about horny geriatrics. It does not so much push the boundaries of taste as ignore the existence of taste. It contains every perverted scenario you could imagine and several more that would never occur to you.

Sometimes it is a little jarring to emerge from a really pivotal and moving scene between Jason and Lucia into a virtual city where the billboards read things like “WANTED for shitting everywhere: THE SHIT BEAST.” But I found these two sides of GTA’s personality surprisingly easy to accommodate. It’s not as though the story is without its many moments of absurd levity and humour, too: at one point I was taking a bunch of terrifying gang leaders with face tattoos on a real-estate tour, where they sincerely admired the outdoor space with brick pizza oven, and another time I was chasing a man wearing nothing but an alligator thong through a well-to-do suburban neighbourhood. A game this huge can accommodate both pathos and lewdness, and much more.

I did find, however, that Lucia – Grand Theft Auto’s first female protagonist – changed how a lot of this landed for me. GTA has never been particularly kind to women (or men, for that fact, but in past games it has exclusively been the women who were sexualised). There is still a lot of sexism in GTA VI’s world, but now we have Lucia to react to it. This is the first GTA in which I have felt the women are treated as people, not only Lucia but also the many other female characters in the story, from up-and-coming rappers to Lucia’s mother, from a jaded strip-club worker to a badass middle-aged drug-runner whose custom purple carbine rifle is her prized possession. This is a game in which women can be as violent, visibly tough and deeply messed-up as the men. And without wanting to give too much away: there are male strippers in Leonida, too.

I am not about to make a case for GTA VI as a feminist game – there is much to be offended by here, if you are so inclined – but I will say that as a woman who has played and enjoyed every GTA game while also often rolling my eyes at their casual sniggering misogyny, GTA VI hits different for me. I liked it more because the women aren’t always the butt of a joke. Also for the first time, I did not find the violence gratuitous or overblown, despite the fact that this is a story about crime and criminals. There is nothing here like GTA V’s infamous torture mission, which made me feel sick. It says a lot that of all the immoral opportunities afforded to me in Vice City, the only thing I straight-up said “no” to was getting involved in a metaverse scam. THAT would be going too far.

I keep reaching for something that sums up GTA VI, as much as it’s possible to sum up a game as vast as this, and the closest I come is the nightclubs. These show GTA VI at its most extravagant: hundreds of virtual people under the pulsing lights, all dressed up to go out, dancing to a fastidiously curated soundtrack featuring some of the real world’s most famous artists as the controller pulses to the bassline in your hands. These big nights out are an opportunity for both Vice City’s sleaziest denizens and Rockstar’s developers to flash their cash: they are the game’s most ostentatiously expensive and showy scenarios. But at the centre of it all, there are Jason and Lucia, dancing together, focused on each other in the midst of all this chaos and excess.
