The 4 simple habits Mary Berry, 91, swears by for better mental health – from brisk walking to communal eating

TheTools · 21.11.2026 09:29 · source · permalink

It's been more than six decades since Dame Mary Berry mastered the perfect lemon drizzle cake and now the 91-year-old's thoughts are turning to a different subject: mental health. Her own and that of the nation's. The two subjects may sound dramatically different but, as it turns out, they might be intimately related.

Back in 1960, when she first cemented her credentials as a baking hero, 'There wasn't such a thing as being able to get any [mental health] help,' she says. 'It was really just, "Get yourself a bit more organised, get a grip and get going" – no time to question it. You might have a word with your mother or your best friend, but on the whole, you bottled it all up, kept it to yourself and tried not worry anybody.'

Dame Mary's Bake Off inspiration

Paul Hollywood and Mary Berry together for the Sport Relief special of Celebrity Bake Off, 2012.Comic Relief - Getty Images

Dame Mary knows more than most about navigating mental health challenges. At the age of 13, a simple cold and headache worsened till she was diagnosed with polio by the family doctor, Dr Love. She spent a month in an isolation hospital, only able to wave at her parents through the glass wall of her room, followed by another two months at an orthopaedic hospital. The virus permanently affected the left side of her body, resulting in a slightly curved spine (scoliosis), a weaker and smaller left hand and a thinner left arm.

She returned to school 'better, but very behind', and disappointed that she couldn't take part physical activity due to her illness. But luckily for the young Mary (and, as it would later turn out, the nation), her school had a new domestic science department, presided over by the aptly named Miss Date. 'She became a very important figure in my life, because I was not very good at the academic subjects, but cooking was something I was thrilled to do.' While her left arm changed the way she rolled pastry, 'Miss Date really encouraged me and said, "Well, you can do it," and "Wasn't that delicious?" and "You've done well." Nobody had ever said I'd done well.'

Those years at school still echo through her work today. For one thing, Miss Date's teaching became the model for Mary's approach on The Great British Bake Off: 'As a judge, what I like to do is to encourage people, praise people and yet be quite firm and put things right. But to be kind and helpful and so that people are rewarded with praise.'

Cooking for children's mental health

Miss Date's impact was not only professional, but was personal too. Cooking, Dame Mary says, became a way for her to manage her mental health, 'because you're busy, you're learning and the great joy is you'll share it – somebody is going to eat it'.

The mental health crisis among young people today is just one reason she believes firmly that cooking deserves a central place on the curriculum. 'It's very good for our mental health – for the boys, too, because it's very rewarding however bright children are,' she says. 'When they leave school, they might be going to university, they might have an apprenticeship, they might be moving to one single room in a new town because they found a job. Either way, they really should be able to cook something that's nutritious, something that they'll like and they can do on a small cooker…'

It's important that these skills need to be taught at school, she says, because the pressures of the modern economy mean both that, 'The young will be short of money, most likely,' and also that, 'Many parents now sometimes don't cook at all. They just buy all the food in.'

Perhaps most important of all, 'Cooking gets them off their screens,' she says. Eating together can also be powerful: 'When you sit around the table and they're all there and their tummies are full, they'll begin to tell you what happened at school that day, what their future is, their plans and things like that, because they're relaxed.'

The 'black dog'

While her school-time struggles are well known, Dame Mary talks less often about what happened to her after she left. 'When I left school and I hadn't done very well and I hadn't yet decided to go to domestic science college, I remember feeling very low,' she says now. 'I used to think it was a black dog on my back. My mother sort of knew something was wrong, and that I was feeling very low, and she used to say, "Oh, let's go shopping," or "Shall we have a cream cake?" But even so, it didn't go.' This, she says, is one of the moments in her life when, looking back, she wishes talking therapy had been available to her.

Instead, she pushed on with her famed resilience, eventually securing a job as a home service adviser for the South Western Electricity Board in Bath. The young Mary travelled to church halls across the region, baking Victoria sponges in order to demonstrate the efficacy of a brand-new form of technology – the thermostatically controlled electric cooker. A job in London followed, where she somewhat miraculously managed to persuade her manager to fund a Cordon Bleu cooking course in Paris. From there, she eventually landed a job as temporary cooking editor at Housewife magazine. A star was born.

Motherhood guilt

Things weren't all plain sailing from there, however. Dame Mary went back to work just five weeks after her three children were born. 'Because I loved what I do,' she stresses, 'but I must say it was not good for my mental health… going out of the door when they're five weeks old.. It was not a nice feeling... And, looking back, I would love to have had a bit of help with my mental health.'

'It was only very rich people who employed somebody to help them and they were thought to be a bit neurotic,' she explains. 'Now we've realised how much better you feel when you have had a chat and some help.'

As part of Bupa's Mindplace Sessions, Dame Mary recently sat down with psychotherapist and author Owen O'Kane, and found herself opening up about her life. 'Very quickly, I found that I was telling him all sorts of things that I wouldn't have told my mother or even my friends,' she says. 'It was going back a long time, to when I was working, when the children were small, and I felt I didn't read to them as much as I should have done when I got home. That was so many years ago and the children have always said, "Oh, we had wonderful fun, Mummy!" but I, at times, thought I hadn't been a good mother.'

The power of food

Artur Kozlov - Getty Images

Dame Mary's understanding of mental health has come along way, but it's come as the result of numerous challenges. Her son William Hunnings died in a car crash in 1989 when he was only 19. She remembers one night, when he'd just left home for university. 'He came home on the Friday night,' she reflects today. 'I had cooked roast lamb, mint sauce, roast potatoes, his favourite things. And I remember him opening the door and he said, "Mum, who's coming?" And I said, "Darling, it's you. It's so lovely to see you at home." And when we have lamb shoulder now, I think of William and it makes me happy.'

Today, aged 91 and having overcome so much, her recipe for wellbeing is incredibly sensible: brisk walks ('very good for our mental health… it's so much better to put the phones down'); eating together as a family; a bit of talking therapy, before things get too much; and a lot of hands-on therapy through cooking.

Oh, and finally, there's one thing she won't go without: 'Toast with marmalade. I liked it before Paddington. I really do love home-made marmalade. Really well-done toast, with lovely creamy butter – absolutely delicious. You feel a lot better after that.'
