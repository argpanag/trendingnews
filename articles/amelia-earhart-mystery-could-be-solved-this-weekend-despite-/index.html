The search for Amelia Earhart's missing plane has hit another obstacle.

A 17-person expedition searching near Nikumaroro Island was forced to pause field operations after strong winds and rough seas made boat travel unsafe, Fox News reported on Friday, October 16, citing an update from Purdue University.

The team is investigating a mysterious feature known as the Taraia Object in the island's lagoon. Researchers believe it could be the wreckage of Earhart's Lockheed Electra 10-E.

Purdue University said the team had already begun collecting information about the object but warned that it was too early to determine what researchers had found.

"Despite Thursday's weather delay, the expedition remains on schedule, with two more days of field operations planned — and an update on the identification of the Taraia Object expected — before the team must depart the atoll and return to Tarawa, Kiribati," the university said, per the report.

Because Nikumaroro is 17 hours ahead of the eastern United States, an announcement could come as early as Saturday, October 17. However, Purdue has not provided an exact time.

"We understand the tremendous interest in what we're doing at Nikumaroro, and we appreciate everyone's patience," Rick Pettigrew, executive director of the Archaeological Legacy Institute and one of the expedition's leaders, said in a statement.

"This is a complex scientific process," he added. "You don't simply arrive at a site and immediately identify what's there. We have to carefully collect the data, analyze it and reach sound conclusions."

The team arrived at Nikumaroro on Monday, October 12, after a five-day voyage across the Pacific Ocean. The uninhabited island is part of the Republic of Kiribati.

As Globe previously reported, the expedition has faced another unusual challenge: giant coconut crabs living on the island.

The crabs have powerful pincers capable of cracking open coconuts and are especially active at night. Because of their size and nighttime activity, the researchers decided to sleep aboard their vessel, the Marin Mainiku, rather than camp on the island.

Although the team encountered the large crabs while working on Nikumaroro, the university said in a report they "posed no threat to the operation."

Earhart, who worked as a counselor at Purdue, was 39 when she disappeared while attempting to fly around the world with navigator Fred Noonan. The pair vanished on July 2, 1937, after leaving Lae, New Guinea, for Howland Island during one of the final legs of their journey.

Their disappearance remains one of aviation's most enduring mysteries.

Purdue became involved in the latest effort in February 2025 after Pettigrew approached the Purdue Research Foundation about investigating the Taraia Object.

If researchers confirm that the object is Earhart's plane, the team hopes to return next year for a full archaeological excavation, provided it receives the necessary permissions.

"The eventual curation of the wreckage has been the subject of discussions with [the Republic of] Kiribati, and although nothing has been finalized, ALI [the Archaeological Legacy Institute] and the Purdue Research Foundation share a dream of returning the plane to Purdue to be displayed, consistent with Amelia's original wishes," Purdue spokesperson Trevor Peters said, via Fox News.
