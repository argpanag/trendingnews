Magnitude-6.6 earthquake prompts scientist's 'drop, cover, hold' reminder

In short:

A magnitude-6.6 earthquake that struck Indonesia this morning was felt across the Top End of Australia.

Geoscience Australia said the quake began 42 kilometres under the Banda Sea.

More than 460 reports have been made so far by Australians who felt the tremors.

Hundreds of Australians have reported feeling tremors from a magnitude-6.6 earthquake that struck Indonesia this morning.

According to the Bureau of Meteorology (BOM), the earthquake struck Tanimbar Islands about 6:41am, Darwin time.

Geoscience Australia said the quake began 42 kilometres under the Banda Sea, with more than 460 reports made so far by Australians who felt the tremors.

The BOM said no part of Australia's mainland or islands faced a tsunami threat from the earthquake.

Residents in several suburbs across Darwin, including the CBD, have reported feeling earthquake tremors in the past 24 hours. (Supplied: Geoscience Australia)

A map of reports received by Geoscience Australia shows tremors were felt across the Northern Territory's Top End, from suburban Darwin and its CBD through to Palmerston and the surrounding rural areas, and as far west as over the WA–NT border.

Over the past 24 hours, nine felt reports were recorded around Katherine and several remote NT regions — including Jabiru, Maningrida, Milingimbi and Nhulunbuy — also experienced tremors.

Short earthquake 'widely felt'

Geoscience Australia seismologist Tanja Pejic described the earthquake as "relatively shallow", but said it was "widely felt".

"We've received in excess of 450 felt reports so far, mostly coming from Darwin, Katherine and Nhulunbuy, and we've got a couple in Kununurra as well," she said.

The seismologist explained the tectonic plate Australia sits upon "is moving at a relatively high speed", pushing north "into Indonesia".

"In the Banda Sea, we have what's called a subduction zone, where that Australian plate actually sinks beneath another plate," she said.

"This is all happening over geological timescales — we're talking millions of years — but the movements that occur at the contact of those two plates, this is what we record as earthquakes."

Ms Pejic said those higher up, including residents in multi-storey homes, usually felt more tremors from an earthquake.

"If you think about such a tall building being like a grandfather clock pendulum, but upside-down, then that upper portion of the building is the part of the pendulum that will swing — so you would really feel [the seismic waves]," she said.

In case of an earthquake, Ms Pejic suggested the "drop, cover and hold" routine: lower yourself down to all-fours to minimise the risk of falling, duck for cover under a table if you can, then use your hands to either hold onto the table or to protect your head and neck.

A number of Territorians have spoken to 105.7 ABC Darwin this morning, sharing their experience of the earthquake.

The Australian government's shake map shows most of the tremors felt in the NT were either weak or light. (Supplied: Geoscience Australia)
