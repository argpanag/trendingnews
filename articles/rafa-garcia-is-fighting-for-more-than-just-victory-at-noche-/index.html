Rafa Garcia is fighting for more than just victory at Noche UFC

TheTools · 11.09.2026 22:57 · source · permalink

PHOENIX – When lightweight Rafa Garcia steps into the octagon for Noche UFC on Saturday at Desert Diamond Arena in Glendale, he’ll be carrying more than a 19-4 record, a three-bout winning streak and the hope of climbing above his current No. 15 ranking.

He’ll be carrying the legacy of his extended family at Bloodline Combat Sports, an agency whose name captures the family approach of its co-founders, UFC Hall of Famer Cub Swanson and longtime manager Kami Safdari, who died of a heart attack on Dec. 22, 2020, at 49 years old.

Safdari served as Garcia’s manager up until his passing. When Garcia made his UFC debut in March 2021, he dedicated the milestone fight to Safdari and Garcia’s uncle Dany, who also died in 2020.

“I love to represent my people.” Garcia said this week ahead of Noche.

Garcia’s Bloodline ties began in 2017 with Swanson, who was watching his own brother, Steve, fight under an organization known as Combate Americas (now Combate Global) in Mexico City. A man cold-approached Swanson and asked if he would be willing to train his son in mixed martial arts, to which Swanson responded: “Just show up.” 

That son was Garcia, who did show up. He quit his full-time job as a seed deliverer and traveled an hour and a half to Palm Springs, California to train with Swanson under the watchful eye of Swanson’s then-manager and mentor, Safdari. The two became good friends, and Swanson even invited Garcia to his training camp in New Mexico to prepare for his bout against Brian Ortega in December 2017.

“It’s been amazing,” Swanson said about his nine years mentoring Garcia. “He’s literally everyone’s dream. He’s such a hard worker, and he doesn’t say no.”

Garcia’s relationship with Swanson has fostered that approach.

“Cub is my coach and manager, but I see him as one of my closest friends,” said Garcia, 32, who has been fighting professionally since 2014.

Although Swanson constantly trained younger fighters between bouts of his own, he didn’t know if that path would still call to him post-retirement.

“I’ve coached almost my whole career between fights,” Swanson said. “I always thought I’d do something away from the sport post-fighting career, just to test my ego … (see) if I could go back to being a nobody in my mind.” 

The training path came back into focus following Safdari’s death. Safdari, a new father at the time, left behind a tightly knit group of fighters who also viewed him as a paternal figure. 

“He was not only my manager,” Swanson said in an Instagram post about Safdari. “He was a mentor, a father figure, an older brother, a best friend, a business partner, the godfather to my children, the only person to be with me at all 38 of my professional fights, all of my surgeries, and the best man at my wedding.”

Safdari balanced a demanding career as a licensed anesthesiologist and fourth-degree black belt in Brazilian jiu-jitsu with managing Swanson’s fighting career “because he loved it.” He began managing fighters in 2004, beginning with Swanson’s first professional bout in 2004. 

Over the next 15 years, their professional relationship grew into a close personal bond as Swanson established himself as one of the UFC’s most accomplished featherweights. Swanson currently holds the UFC featherweight record for most post-fight bonuses, with eleven, and has notable wins over former UFC champions Dustin Poirier and Charles Oliveira.

“Kami was a guy who would be there at a drop of a dime,” former Bellator and Rizin world champion Juan Archuleta said. “He was always willing to give the shirt off his back. He was the true definition of ‘treat people how you want to be treated.’”

Safdari’s cousin, Mani Ahmadi, was handling the contractual and legal side of Swanson’s fighting career when the unexpected loss struck, so he and Swanson went on to build the agency together, carrying forward Safdari’s legacy and the relationships he had cultivated throughout his years in combat sports. 

“Kami had always asked me to professionalize (Bloodline), do it together, but I always thought we had more time,” Ahmadi said. “God had other plans.”

Shortly after Safdari’s passing, other management agencies began contacting the fighters he represented. Swanson was still an active UFC fighter and Ahmadi was working as a corporate attorney, but neither wanted the group that Safdari built to fracture.

The name “Bloodline” was inspired by Safdari and the power of fighting for your family and lineage. Now, the family-inspired agency represents multiple successful fighters such as Garcia, Danny Silva (11-2), Richie “El Machete” Miranda (13-2) and Lerryan “Gunslinger” Douglas (14-6).

For years, Bloodline operated out of borrowed space, but Swanson had already learned what he did and did not want from owning a gym. He decided that he wanted to bet on himself and build a gym in honor of Safdari.

An abandoned indoor pool became the centerpiece of the project: a sunken space the team converted into a martial arts training pit. For the first time, the team had a facility of its own, with Swanson able to coach everyone under one roof. Construction on the pool conversion began in mid-2025.

As attention turned toward his final professional fight at UFC 327, Swanson used that spotlight to show people the next chapter. He was no longer simply preparing for another fight. He was preparing for life after fighting.

Then, almost symbolically, the transition occurred within days. Swanson fought his final bout on a Saturday, and Bloodline opened its new gym in Orange, California to public classes that Monday.

His career as a fighter was over. His life as a coach, gym owner and mentor had formally begun.

“I have so much experience with highs and lows, failures and accomplishments, that I felt like I’m the perfect person to give feedback to people that want to pursue (MMA),” Swanson said.

Garcia’s development, including his first UFC knockout at the last year’s Noche event, is evidence of what can happen when a fighter and coach continue to invest in each other over the long term.

“I feel like I’m fighting the best (I’ve ever fought) right now,” Garcia said. “I’ve matured a lot in my career and am taking things more seriously.”

With the gym now open to the public, the Bloodline team sees martial arts as something that can serve a much broader purpose. A Garcia victory against Zhu Rong this Saturday in Glendale would be an exclamation point on the team’s legacy — one that sees no end in sight.
