Jonatan Vseviov sets out a new vision for Estonia, but difficult questions remain

TheTools · 01.11.2026 18:16 · source · permalink

Jonatan Vseviov, a former secretary general of Estonia’s foreign ministry who left his post to join the Social Democratic Party and run for a seat in the parliament, wants Estonia to become a more influential European power, revive its economy and strengthen social security; his first major political speech offered an ambitious programme, but left its financing and implementation largely unexplained.

On 22 October, Vseviov announced that he was leaving his post as secretary general of Estonia’s foreign ministry to join the Social Democratic Party and run for a seat in the Estonian parliament. His departure took effect on 1 November, ending a lengthy career at the highest levels of Estonia’s diplomatic and defence establishment. Before heading the foreign ministry, he served as Estonia’s ambassador to the United States and as secretary general of the defence ministry.

The move marked a significant transition for a senior civil servant closely associated with Estonia’s security policy and its response to Russian aggression. Vseviov said he had concluded that safeguarding and developing Estonia required more than he could achieve as a government official.

In his first major political speech, delivered to the Social Democratic Party’s council on 1 November, Vseviov argued that the policies which had secured Estonia’s success since the restoration of independence in 1991 were no longer sufficient.

“What brought us here will no longer take us forward,” Vseviov told party members, warning that a changing international order, technological upheaval and economic stagnation demanded more ambitious political leadership.

His speech presented a broad programme built around three priorities: strengthening Estonia’s influence in Europe, restoring economic growth and providing greater security for its citizens.

It also marked a significant expansion of his public profile beyond foreign and defence policy, the fields in which he has spent much of his career.

Estonia must help shape Europe’s future

Vseviov’s principal foreign-policy argument was that Estonia should no longer be content with following decisions made by larger allies.

Instead, it should seek a place among the countries shaping Europe’s security arrangements, particularly as Russia’s war against Ukraine and uncertainty over American commitments force European governments to assume greater responsibility for their defence.

“The most important guarantee of Estonia’s security is that our freedom should also be essential to the rest of Europe,” he said.

Vseviov argued that the outcome of the war in Ukraine would determine whether Europe remained committed to sovereignty and territorial integrity or returned to a system in which larger powers could dictate the future of smaller countries.

His assessment reflected his longstanding work in Estonian security policy. However, although he called for Estonia to join Europe’s political and intellectual core, he offered few details about the institutional changes he would support to achieve that ambition.

The speech did not, for example, address whether Estonia should support deeper European political integration, changes to EU foreign-policy voting rules or new common defence-financing arrangements.

A more active role for the state

On economic policy, Vseviov challenged the model that helped turn Estonia into a successful post-Soviet economy.

He argued that fiscal discipline, relatively light regulation and openness to foreign investment had delivered considerable progress, but were no longer enough to secure future prosperity.

Pointing to Denmark’s development of internationally competitive industries, he called for closer cooperation between government, businesses and research institutions.

He identified personalised medicine, cyber security, advanced defence manufacturing and the processing of critical raw materials as areas in which Estonia could develop new competitive advantages.

“The state’s role is not to conduct business on behalf of entrepreneurs,” he said. “Its role is to create conditions in which entrepreneurs can do things they would otherwise be unable to do.”

The approach represents a departure from the emphasis on limited state intervention traditionally associated with Estonia’s economic policy.

Yet it also raises questions about how governments should select industries for support, avoid favouring politically influential businesses and assess whether public investments have succeeded.

Vseviov proposed improving access to growth capital, including creating conditions for Estonian pension savings to be invested more extensively in domestic businesses. He did not specify how such arrangements would protect pension savers from additional investment risks.

Energy promises and public spending

Energy policy provided some of the speech’s most concrete commitments.

Vseviov called for renewable electricity production to increase at least threefold, accelerated approval procedures for strategically important wind farms and additional gas-fired generation to maintain security of supply.

His stated objective was to reduce Estonia’s average wholesale electricity price to at least the level prevailing in Finland.

However, the speech did not set out the projected cost of these measures or explain how the government would manage the additional investment required in electricity networks, reserve generation and other infrastructure.

Nor did it address the potential role of nuclear power, which remains part of Estonia’s wider debate over long-term energy security.

The financing question extended beyond energy. Vseviov criticised Estonia’s rising public debt while advocating greater investment in infrastructure, innovation, education and social protection.

He did not explain whether these commitments would be financed through higher taxation, borrowing or reductions elsewhere in public spending.

A centrist message from the Social Democrats

Vseviov’s domestic agenda placed considerable emphasis on economic security, equal opportunities and the importance of public services to national competitiveness.

He argued that inequality and insecurity weakened not only social cohesion but also Estonia’s economy and defence.

Nevertheless, his speech contained relatively little about traditional social-democratic priorities such as progressive taxation, collective bargaining or redistribution.

Instead, he sought to combine economic enterprise with stronger social protections, presenting the two as complementary rather than competing objectives.

That approach gives the Social Democrats, led by Lauri Läänemets, a platform extending beyond conventional left-wing economic arguments. It also leaves open the question of how Vseviov’s proposed policies would differ in practice from those of Estonia’s centrist and economically liberal parties.

His regional proposals included securing at least one major strategic investment in every county. Although intended to spread economic opportunities beyond Tallinn, the pledge leaves unanswered how investment decisions by private companies could be reconciled with geographical targets.

From diplomacy to government

Vseviov concluded by drawing on his experience in the civil service and arguing that Estonia now required a different kind of political leadership.

He said his work on national defence, NATO’s collective security and the response to Russian aggression had helped strengthen Estonia, but that his ability to influence its future as a civil servant had reached its limits.

The move into electoral politics presents a different test.

Diplomatic influence rests heavily on expertise, negotiation and relationships. Government requires decisions on taxation, spending and competing domestic interests, often with immediate political consequences.

Vseviov’s speech established the broad direction of his political ambitions. It connected Estonia’s security, economic competitiveness and social cohesion in a programme that went considerably beyond the foreign-policy issues with which he is most closely associated.

What remains less clear is how those objectives would be prioritised, financed and delivered.

For a politician seeking to move from the foreign ministry to the prime minister’s office, answering those questions will be as important as setting out the vision itself.
