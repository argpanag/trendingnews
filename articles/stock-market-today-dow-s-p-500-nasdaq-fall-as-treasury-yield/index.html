US stocks fell on Monday as US-Iran tensions resurfaced, lifting oil prices, and Treasury yields jumped to start an economic data-packed week.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI) dropped 0.7%, the S&P 500 (^GSPC) declined by about 0.8%, and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC) fell by 0.9%.

The 10-year Treasury yield (^TNX) climbed 6 basis points to 5.24%, its highest level since 2007, while the 30-year yield (^TYX) rose to 5.56%, its highest level since 2004.

(^DJI )

Markets were on edge after President Trump rejected Tehran's proposal, which was similar to a previous memorandum of understanding, to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and end the war. With officials back at the negotiating table, Trump told Axios that talks would resume this week. Brent crude (BZ=F), the international benchmark, rose to $98 per barrel.

Nvidia (NVDA) stock jumped after the AI chip leader released two new open-sourced tools, OpenShell and Nvidia Sentry, to help control rogue AI agents. Nvidia also announced a whopping $150 billion share buyback authorization.

That bucked the trend in the semiconductor industry as chip stocks faced pressure after OpenAI (OPAI.PVT) disclosed that one of its agentic AI models escaped its container and accessed the internet. That incident served as the latest in a string of AI breaches that have prompted some top AI leaders, including Anthropic (ANTH.PVT) CEO Dario Amodei, to call for slowing the pace of AI development. It comes as Anthropic and OpenAI are reportedly targeting public market debuts in the next 12 months.

Meanwhile, a packed week of inflation and labor market data awaits investors. From the Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) report on Wednesday to the monthly employment report on Friday, investors will field new insights on the health of the economy.

Jefferies Financial Group (JEF) and Vail Resorts (MTN) report quarterly results on Monday, with Micron (MU) and Nike (NKE) results highlighting the earnings calendar later in the week.

  • S&P 500 stock valuations become reasonable again ahead of Q3 earnings season

    Yahoo Finance's Brian Sozzi reports:

    Investors remain cautious about a market trading near a record high, as seen in the good old-fashioned price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio on the S&P 500 (^GSPC).

    Despite a 14% year-to-date return for the S&P 500, the forward P/E multiple on the index has dropped to 19 times from 22 times. It now matches its 10-year average.

    "One reason for the decline in valuations is the rise in interest rates," Goldman Sachs strategist Ben Snider explained. "The S&P 500 equity risk premium has remained near the current 3% level for most of the last two years. The lower P/E also reflects investor concerns that the AI investment boom is causing companies to 'over-earn' relative to both cash flows and a sustainable level of profits."

    An argument could easily be made that the P/E ratio should be above its long-term average, given higher oil prices and Treasury yields. That argument revolves around the strength of corporate profits.

    Analysts have increased their third quarter earnings projections for S&P 500 companies by 1.3% since June 30, FactSet pointed out. Usually, analysts lower their earnings estimates during the quarter.

  • Bank stocks slide as AI adds to deposit cost worries

    US bank stocks came under renewed pressure Monday as investors weighed a combination of higher interest rates and fresh concerns about how artificial intelligence could hike funding costs for lenders

    The KBW Nasdaq Bank Index (^BKX) fell by 1% shortly after Monday's open and is down more than 3.5% over the last five days.

    The latest concern is that increasingly capable AI agents could make it easier for customers to continuously shop around for better returns on their cash, threatening the cheap deposit base that help underpin profit margins for plain vanilla lenders.

    Over the weekend, Apollo chief economist Torsten Sløk laid out the hypothetical worst-case scenario. (Disclosure: Yahoo is a portfolio company of funds managed by affiliates of Apollo Global Management.)

    Sløk argued that AI agents could eventually automatically sweep household cash from checking accounts, paying around 0.1%, into alternatives offering yields of 3.3% to 5%. If adopted broadly enough, he warned, that could cause banks to lose a significant portion of the low-cost deposits they rely on to fund lending.
