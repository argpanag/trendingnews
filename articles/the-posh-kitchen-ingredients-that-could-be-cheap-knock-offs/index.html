The posh kitchen ingredients that could be cheap knock-offs

TheTools · 16.11.2026 10:39 · source · permalink

You might think the posh ingredients sitting in your kitchen cupboards are exactly what they claim to be.

But after beloved British brand Maldon Sea Salt was revealed to use German rock salt, experts are warning that a host of premium foods may not be quite what they seem.

Fancy products including olive oil, manuka honey, balsamic vinegar and avocado oil are regularly bulked out with cheap alternatives.

In some cases, shoppers may be paying premium prices for products that contain little of the prized ingredient advertised on the label.

This is to help keep production costs down and speed up the manufacturing process, while cashing in on the growing popularity of premium and health–focused foods.

Luckily, there are a number of ways to tell whether your products are the real deal.

While a healthy splash of olive oil can improve almost any dish, prices of the delightful vegetable oil are at an all-time high.

A 500ml bottle of Filippo Berio's extra virgin olive oil now costs around £7.50 – an increase of £3.75 from 2022 – due to a series of poor harvests pushing up costs.

These rising prices have fuelled a boom in fraud, with some products being diluted with cheaper oils or falsely marketed as extra virgin.

In extreme cases, investigators have even uncovered batches coloured to resemble genuine olive oil.

Professor Maurizio Servili, an olive oil expert at the University of Perugia, told the Daily Mail: 'Olive oil is not a cheap product. It's easier and more economical to make frauds of an expensive product than a cheap product.'

Experts say shoppers should look for 'extra virgin' labels, traceable origins and DOP certification. Proper olive oil has a bitter, peppery taste which is hard to replicate.

However, viral tests, such as putting oil in the fridge, are unlikely to provide much insight.

Premium Manuka honey, which owes its reputation to methylglyoxal (MGO), a compound linked to antibacterial activity, can cost upwards of £50 a jar.

But its high price tag has encouraged some producers to dilute it with cheaper honey varieties and syrups. 

An EU investigation found more than 46 per cent of imported samples showed signs of tampering, with at least one marker of additional sugar sources detected.

Of the ten UK samples the European Commission's From the Hives investigated, every single jar was found to be a fake.

Ian Campbell of the British Beekeepers Association said: 'Honey is widely considered one of the most adulterated foodstuffs globally.

'Honey fraud is international and extensive. It's something of an "arms race" between testers and those adulterating honey.'

Experts warn that at-home tests that usually involve water, fire or so-called 'genetic memory' to check authenticity are unreliable and recommend buying from local beekeepers whenever possible.

Researchers at the University of California found that 89 per cent of avocado oil-labelled products contained other oils 

Foods marketed as containing avocado oil may not contain as much of the trendy ingredient as consumers expect.

A new study by researchers at the University of California found that 89 per cent of avocado oil-labelled processed foods contained other oils.

'Consumers are increasingly paying a premium for products made with avocado oil or olive oil,' lead author Professor Selina Wang said.

'They deserve to get what they pay for.'

The study suggests contamination can occur deep within global supply chains, making it difficult for brands to identify the source of the problem.

Traditional sourdough is made using a live starter culture and can take up to a week to ferment.

However, campaigners warn that many supermarket loaves are actually 'sourfaux' products produced in just a few hours using commercial yeast and additives.

Shoppers are advised to check ingredient lists carefully, as genuine sourdough should contain only flour, water, salt and starter culture.

Authentic balsamic vinegar is made from 100 percent 'cooked grape must' (a product that contains the juice, skins, seeds and stems), with no additives and aged over a minimum of 12 years. It costs upwards of £200 per 100ml.

But cheaper supermarket versions are often blended with wine vinegar, caramel colouring and thickeners.

Experts recommend checking the ingredients list, as products containing only cooked grape must are generally closer to the authentic article.

The more ingredients listed, the more likely it is to be a mass-produced imitation rather than the real deal.

Italy-based Mazzetti, which produces balsamic vinegar of Modena, said: 'The list of ingredients on the back label of the bottle says a lot about the quality of the final product.

'The ingredients are listed by quantity: and as a general rule, more premium balsamic vinegars are made with more grape must than wine vinegar.'

It's the trendy drink every coffee shop whisks up these days, with supermarkets lining their shelves with promises of authentic 'ceremonial-grade' matcha powders.

And while the term sounds like a mark of authority, each company can set its own standard of what is required.

'Ceremonial-grade' is primarily a marketing term used in the West, while Japan does not have an official regulated grading system.

While the words suggests quality, grading is not tightly regulated in the UK.

'Higher-quality matcha can offer more antioxidants, but the label alone isn't a guarantee,' celebrity nutritionist Gabriela Peacock said. 'Colour, freshness and sourcing matter just as much.'

Authentic and high-quality matcha tea is made from the finely ground powder of green tea leaves specially grown in shade.

The texture of high-quality matcha powder is also noticeably fine and silky smooth between the fingers.  

Kombucha has become popular because of its probiotic content, but not all bottles offer the same health benefits.

Some manufacturers pasteurise the drink to extend its shelf-life, a process that kills many of the live microbes consumers are paying for.

Researchers warned that pasteurisation 'inevitably kills vast amounts of healthy, friendly bacteria, too, cancelling out their health benefits'.

To get the real thing, experts recommend choosing chilled products labelled 'raw' and 'unpasteurised'.
