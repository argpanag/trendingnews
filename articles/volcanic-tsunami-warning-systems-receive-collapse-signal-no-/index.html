Volcanic Tsunami Warning Systems Receive Collapse Signal; No Algorithm Converts It to Alert

TheTools · 10.09.2026 14:34 · source · permalink

When Anak Krakatau erupted for 25 continuous hours beginning September 4, 2026, grounding nearly 3,000 flights and stranding an estimated 341,000 passengers across eight Indonesian airports, the monitors watching for a repeat of the 2018 disaster that killed 437 people correctly concluded no tsunami threat had materialized. The rebuilt cone was still too small to collapse catastrophically. The system worked — but only because the volcano cooperated.

What Indonesia's monitors could not have told anyone is whether those same monitors would catch a collapse if it happened. That question got a precise and uncomfortable answer this week. A new study published in Science Advances and highlighted today by Nature in a Nature research highlight has confirmed that the 2022 Hunga Tonga eruption broadcast an underwater acoustic signal announcing its caldera collapse 17 minutes before the resulting wave reached coastal Tonga — a signal that propagated through the ocean to monitoring stations more than 1,616 miles (2,600 km) away. The signal was there. The world heard it. No warning was issued, because no one had written code to recognize what it meant.

The finding, from a team led by University of Auckland volcanologist Shane Cronin, amounts to a technical audit of the world's volcanic tsunami warning gap. The gap is not, it turns out, a hardware problem. The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization already operates a global hydroacoustic monitoring network that sends data in near-real time to 24 warning centers in 23 countries — most recently adding Mexico in August 2026. What that pipeline does not yet do is translate a volcanic caldera-collapse signal into an alarm. Closing that gap is an algorithm development problem, not an infrastructure construction project.

Two Minutes of Eruption, 17 Minutes of Warning: What Tonga's Caldera Collapse Produced

The 2022 Hunga eruption did not produce a single tsunami. It produced several, from different causes, and the new research establishes clearly that they were not equally dangerous.

The first waves arrived within minutes of the eruption's opening explosions — with runup heights of 3 to 13 feet (1 to 4 meters) on the main Tongan island of Tongatapu, according to the Cronin 2026 study. Damaging, but survivable. Most residents had already evacuated. Then, roughly 90 minutes after the eruption began, the center of the Hunga volcano began caving inward.

The collapse ultimately carved out a depression approximately 2.5 miles (4 km) wide and more than 2,789 feet (850 m) deep. Displacing that volume of rock and seawater in a matter of minutes generated a second tsunami — with runup heights of 59 to 131 feet (18 to 40 m) on islands within 62 miles (100 km) of Hunga — that destroyed villages across southern Tonga and killed at least three people.

For the existing earthquake-based warning network in the Pacific, this second event — the lethal one — was nearly undetectable. The nearest seismometer was in Fiji, approximately 466 miles (750 km) away, a distance at which many volcanic signals are too attenuated by solid rock to interpret usefully.

But the ocean carried the signal anyway.

A telecommunications tower at Kanokupolu on the western coast of Tongatapu, standing 591 feet (180 m) inland and 43 feet (13 m) above sea level, had survived the first waves. It did not survive the second. Working with Tonga Communications Corporation, the research team pinpointed the exact moment transmissions ceased — 6:45:24 p.m. local time — providing a forensic timestamp for the wave's arrival. Cross-referencing that timestamp against records from 14 seismic stations scattered across the southwest Pacific, the team found a massive hydroacoustic signal — called a T-wave, or tertiary wave — that had traveled through the ocean at 0.93 miles per second (1.5 km/s). The T-wave indicated the caldera had begun collapsing at approximately 6:28 p.m. The tower was destroyed 17 minutes later.

That 17-minute gap is the article's most important number. It represents warning time that did not exist for coastal Tonga in 2022 — and that the Cronin study suggests could theoretically exist in the future.

What the Warning Pipe Is Missing

Sound travels through seawater at roughly 0.93 miles per second (1.5 km/s). Open-ocean tsunamis travel at roughly 0.12 miles per second (200 m/s) — more than seven times slower, with additional deceleration as they enter shallow coastal water. The T-wave from a caldera collapse therefore arrives at a distant monitoring station long before the destructive wave reaches shore. At a station 1,243 miles (2,000 km) from a volcanic event, the acoustic signal arrives roughly 22 minutes before the open-ocean tsunami would. That window shrinks for closer islands but does not vanish.

The physics were always there. What was missing was an automated system designed to recognize what it was hearing.

The CTBTO's International Monitoring System operates 11 dedicated hydroacoustic stations — six hydrophone arrays and five T-phase (island-mounted seismometer) stations — distributed across the world's oceans for the original purpose of detecting nuclear tests. Data from these stations, combined with the IMS's 164 additional seismic stations, travels to the International Data Centre in Vienna and is then forwarded in near-real time to 24 national tsunami warning centers under formal Tsunami Warning Agreements. During the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami, CTBTO data led other sources by up to three minutes at the Pacific region's warning centers.

That pipeline is fast, global, and operational. It is also, at present, tuned to a different kind of signal.

The algorithms that process CTBTO hydroacoustic data for tsunami-warning purposes were developed around earthquake-generated tsunamis. Seismic ruptures produce a characteristic set of P-wave and S-wave arrivals that precede any tsunami wave and that the IDC's automated processing can classify rapidly. The volcanic caldera-collapse T-wave is a different signal class — it originates from a different physical process (gravitational subsidence of a volcanic edifice rather than tectonic plate slip), produces a different acoustic signature, and does not come accompanied by the type of seismic precursors that the earthquake-warning algorithms are designed to look for.

CTBTO's own published documentation on hydroacoustic monitoring for tsunami warning explicitly describes researchers as "exploring the potential for these sound waves to provide information about the earthquake's nature" — not the volcanic collapse case. The GREAT v1.0 real-time assessment tool, which has been running on CTBTO hydroacoustic data since June 2024 and has been validated against more than 40,000 historical records including Sumatra 2004 and Tohoku 2011, was designed and benchmarked for earthquake sources.

None of this is a criticism of the existing system. It does what it was built to do, and it does it well. The problem is that what it was built to do is not what the 2022 Tonga disaster, the 2018 Anak Krakatau disaster, and last week's Krakatau near-miss collectively demanded.

Bathymetric Occlusion: What Stands Between Proof-of-Concept and Operational Alert

The Cronin study is not the only new research expanding the technical picture. A parallel paper published earlier in 2026 in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth by a separate team examined how T-wave signals from the same Tonga eruption were distorted by seafloor topography along their propagation paths. The researchers found that bathymetric features — underwater ridges, seamounts, and ocean floor topography — explain 88% of T-wave variability across the 14-station network.

This finding, which the researchers describe as a model of "bathymetric occlusion," is the key implementation challenge separating the Cronin study's proof-of-concept from an operational warning system. Knowing that a T-wave was detected at multiple stations is useful. Knowing precisely where the collapse occurred — and therefore which coastlines are threatened and how much time they have — requires correcting for the distortion each signal accumulates as it navigates the ocean floor between the source and the sensor. Without bathymetric correction, multi-station hydroacoustic networks produce noisy, ambiguous source locations; with it, the Simon et al. modeling suggests the accuracy needed for timely, directed alerts becomes achievable.

The SOFAR channel — the deep-ocean sound waveguide where sound velocity reaches its minimum, typically around 0.6 miles (1 km) below the surface at mid-latitudes — enables T-waves to propagate with remarkably little energy loss over thousands of miles. (Energy attenuates following cylindrical rather than spherical spreading — a physical property that makes the channel uniquely efficient for long-range acoustic monitoring.) But that same propagation path is interrupted wherever a seamount, island, or submarine ridge rises high enough to block or deflect the signal. An underwater volcano rising from the ocean floor functions, in the Cronin team's framing, like an underwater bell — radiating sound outward through the ocean — but the signal that reaches a distant sensor has been shaped by every topographic feature it encountered along the way.

Correcting for that effect, and doing so in the seconds to minutes available during an active eruption, is the specific computational problem that must be solved before T-wave detection can drive automated volcanic tsunami alerts.

Why the CTBT's Nuclear-Monitoring Network Is the Right Starting Point

An assessment of the implementation pathway would be incomplete without examining what already exists that the proposed system could build on.

The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty's International Monitoring System was designed and built to detect nuclear explosions anywhere on Earth, including underwater and in remote ocean environments where no other monitoring infrastructure exists. Its hydroacoustic stations are positioned for maximum ocean coverage, not for proximity to the world's most tectonically active regions. As it happens, this global distribution makes the network well-suited to monitoring submarine volcanic events, which are also distributed across ocean basins along tectonic boundaries — specifically the Pacific and Indian Ocean volcanic arcs where most volcanic tsunamis originate.

The CTBTO detected the Hunga Tonga eruption extensively. Months before the eruption, the IMS's strategically placed stations detected hydroacoustic signals indicating volcanic activity; following the eruption, the CTBTO's network recorded Hunga's full acoustic signature across seismic, infrasound, and hydroacoustic channels. What it could not do in real time was classify the caldera-collapse T-wave as a tsunami-generating event and route that classification to the 24 warning centers already plugged into its data pipeline.

The path from current capability to operational volcanic warning does not require laying new cable, deploying new buoys, or negotiating new international agreements — the TWAs already exist. It requires developing, validating, and deploying algorithms specifically trained to distinguish caldera-collapse T-wave signatures from other volcanic and seismic signals, in real time, and to translate those classifications into the source-location outputs that warning centers use to issue directional alerts.

Indonesia's own monitoring agency, BMKG, appears to have recognized this urgency before last week's Krakatau eruption: in September 2025, a BMKG scientist presented on Indonesia's volcanic tsunami threat at the CTBT Science and Technology Conference in Vienna, specifically addressing non-seismic tsunamis from volcanic collapses as an important new disaster challenge following the 2018 Palu and Sunda Strait events. One year later, Anak Krakatau erupted again.

Stakes Along the Ring of Fire

The IOC-UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission formed a dedicated working group on volcanically generated tsunamis following the 2022 Hunga event, and its 2024 report on monitoring volcanic tsunamis formally acknowledged the structural gap in volcanic tsunami detection, calling for complementary detection methods beyond seismic networks. The IOC's Ocean Decade Tsunami Programme has set a stated goal of an all-sources early warning system by 2030, outlined in its Ocean Decade Tsunami Programme.

For Pacific and Indian Ocean island nations, the practical context for this timeline is immediate. Tonga sits along the Tonga-Kermadec arc, one of Earth's most volcanically active submarine environments, with more submarine volcanoes per unit length than almost any other zone on the planet. The 2022 eruption killed a minimum of three people and caused an estimated $182 million in damage — a figure representing a substantial fraction of the nation's GDP. The relatively low death toll owed partly to geography: the caldera's orientation directed its most destructive waves away from Tonga's most densely populated areas. Future eruptions on the Ring of Fire are unlikely to be so fortuitously positioned.

The Anak Krakatau precedent is instructive. The 2018 Krakatau collapse study, which documented the flank collapse that killed 437 people and displaced more than 30,000, produced no earthquake signature large enough to trigger any existing warning alert, because no large earthquake occurred. The warning gap for volcanic tsunamis has been documented, measured, and mourned twice within seven years. The Cronin study's significance is not only that it confirms T-waves from caldera collapse propagate to distant sensors before the wave arrives — it is that it specifies what kind of signal to write the code for.

"If monitoring systems can automatically recognize and locate these signals, they could provide early warning of volcanic tsunamis," Cronin and USC co-author Jose Carlos Borrero wrote in an explanatory essay accompanying the research, "much as existing systems do for those triggered by earthquakes."

The infrastructure already pipes the signal to the right people. The signal's translation into human language is what remains to be written.

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is the algorithm gap in volcanic tsunami warning, and why does it matter now?

The world's nuclear-test monitoring network — operated by the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization — already sends hydroacoustic data from 11 underwater stations to 24 national tsunami warning centers in near-real time. The existing algorithms that process this data were designed to detect earthquake-generated tsunamis, which produce a predictable set of seismic precursors. Volcanic caldera collapses generate a different type of underwater acoustic signal — a T-wave — that existing algorithms are not trained to classify or locate as a tsunami source. The Cronin 2026 study confirmed that this T-wave is detectable at warning-relevant distances before the tsunami arrives, but building the detection algorithm — training it on the Tonga signal's characteristics, correcting for seafloor topography, and integrating it with the existing warning pipeline — has not yet been done. The September 2026 Anak Krakatau eruption underscored the urgency: the monitoring infrastructure correctly assessed the rebuilt cone posed no collapse risk this time, but the same infrastructure could not have issued a T-wave-based warning if a collapse had occurred.

What made the Tonga caldera collapse different from the initial explosions, and why did it generate a bigger tsunami?

The opening explosions of the 2022 Hunga eruption generated waves with runup heights of 3 to 13 feet (1 to 4 m) on Tongatapu — damaging but survivable. The caldera collapse, which occurred roughly 90 minutes into the eruption when the eruption had emptied the underlying magma chamber sufficiently to cause the volcanic summit to cave inward, displaced a far larger volume of rock and seawater in a much shorter time. The collapse carved out a depression approximately 2.5 miles (4 km) wide and 2,789 feet (850 m) deep. Moving that volume produced waves with runup heights of 59 to 131 feet (18 to 40 m) — six to thirteen stories — that destroyed communities within 62 miles of Hunga. The key distinction for warning purposes is timing: the caldera collapse occurred 90 minutes into the eruption, long after some coastal residents may have concluded the worst had passed following the initial waves.

Could the Anak Krakatau 2026 eruption have triggered a tsunami, and what would have warned people if it had?

Indonesia's volcanic monitoring agency PVMBG assessed that the rebuilt Anak Krakatau cone — which has been growing since the 2018 collapse reduced the volcano to about 515 feet (157 m) in elevation — was not yet large enough to pose catastrophic collapse risk in the September 2026 eruption. BMKG confirmed no sea-level anomalies were detected during the eruption. That assessment held, and no tsunami occurred. What the Cronin study reveals, however, is that if a caldera collapse or flank failure had occurred, the CTBTO's global hydroacoustic network would almost certainly have recorded a T-wave from it — but the existing alert algorithms would not have converted that signal into an issued warning. The detection infrastructure exists; the classification and alerting software for volcanic-source T-waves does not.

What technical problems still need to be solved before the T-wave approach can provide operational warnings?

Two principal challenges remain. The first is signal classification: developing and validating algorithms that can distinguish a caldera-collapse T-wave from other volcanic acoustic signals — eruption explosions, flank landslides, submarine venting — in real time and with enough confidence to trigger a warning without excessive false alarms. The second is source geolocation: accounting for "bathymetric occlusion," the distortion of T-wave signals by seafloor topography (ridges, seamounts, continental shelves) between the source and the sensor. A 2026 study in the Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth found that seafloor topography drives 88% of variability in T-wave signal strength across the monitoring network; correcting for this distortion is necessary to pinpoint the collapse location accurately enough to determine which coastlines are threatened and how much time they have. The IOC-UNESCO has set a target of an all-sources tsunami early warning system by 2030, but no publicly committed timeline exists for volcanic T-wave algorithm development specifically.
