Risks unavoidable, says Racing Victoria, amid fallout from Caulfield Cup death

Racing Victoria has labelled Saturday’s Caulfield Cup horse death “an unfortunate accident” and says the risk of injury and accidents cannot be eliminated as it backs in its safety protocols ahead of the Cox Plate and Melbourne Cup.

The industry was left reeling after Chris Waller’s mare Ohope Wins was brought crashing to the ground, alongside last year’s winner Half Yours, in the final moments of the $6 million race and had to be put down in the home straight.

Both jockeys involved in the fall, Mark Zahra and Jye McNeil, were released from hospital on Sunday after escaping serious injury.

In addition, trainer Tony McEvoy spoke of his relief that Half Yours had emerged safely from the two-horse fall, but was equally shattered for Waller and his stable.

Racing Victoria stewards spoke to three jockeys – Jabez Johnstone (rider of Kings Valley), Rachel King (Green Spaces) and Ethan Brown (Zahrann) – following the mid-race carnage, but adjourned their inquiry until they can speak to Zahra and McNeill.

They will seek to determine the cause of the incident and whether any rider has breached the Rules of Racing.

“The sport has made significant progress over the past five years with world-leading veterinary protocols setting a new global safety standard for our feature races, alongside strong regulatory oversight from our stewards policing the conduct of races,” a Racing Victoria spokesperson said.

“Unfortunately, like all sports, you can never eliminate all risks of injury and, in the case of racing, unfortunate accidents like the one that occurred yesterday.”

The fall brings renewed scrutiny on the Melbourne Cup, which faced extinction in 2020 after seven horse deaths in seven years.

Unlike the Caulfield fall, the majority of the Cup deaths were seen as preventable soundness issues.

“The spring racing carnival is a major social and economic contributor to the state of Victoria and engagement levels have continued to grow over recent years,” Racing Victoria said.

“We expect our feature meetings throughout the remainder of spring will continue to be well embraced by locals and out-of-state visitors.”

The Victoria Racing Club, which governs Flemington, said it remained focused on “preparing for a safe and successful Melbourne Cup carnival”.

“Equine wellbeing is paramount to the Victoria Racing Club, and our thoughts are with everyone connected to Ohope Wins,” a club spokesperson said.

The Coalition for the Protection of Racehorses said the sport was too dangerous “not just for the horses but also for the jockeys”.

“It is just unacceptable that they [the racing industry] expect horses to be killed on the racetrack,” the coalition’s campaign director, Elio Celotto, said.

“It really was no surprise to us, and we are just appalled that it happened.”

The coalition said its “death-watch team” counted five horses dying on Australian racetracks every fortnight.

The VRC said Victorian racing operated under world-leading veterinary and welfare protocols.

The industry has invested more than $50 million in equine welfare and safety initiatives since 2017, and the Melbourne Cup has been fatality free since new protocols were introduced in 2021.

McEvoy described the moment the fall happened as “gut-wrenching”.

The trainer dashed onto the track, fearing for his horse’s safety, while his son, Calvin, sprinted towards the jockeys.

McEvoy said it was doubtful Half Yours would defend his Melbourne Cup title, and would likely be sent for a spell.

“We are very lucky. He is a bit battered and bruised, he’s got a couple of nicks and cuts, but all in all he is bright and well,” McEvoy said.

“We are very pleased to still have him.”

The trainer said he reached out to Waller and Yulong, the owner of Ohope Wins, on Saturday night to offer his support.

Zahra posted a message on social media to reassure those interested in his welfare: “Thanks everyone for the calls and messages,” he said. “I’m OK and home. No serious damage. Very stiff and sore but nothing a cold glass of champagne can’t fix.”

Chris Waller, who trained Ohope Wins, put out a statement as connections dealt with the devastation of losing the horse. He was contacted for comment on Sunday.

Champion trainer Peter Moody addressed the falls in an interview on ABC Queensland on Sunday morning.

“It’s very sad and very disappointing,” Moody said.

“It’s just one of those unfortunate accidents. No one sets out for that to happen. Everyone wants to see the horses and the riders come home safely.”

A Racing Victoria spokesperson told this masthead on Sunday that the industry felt for the connections and staff who worked and cared for Ohope Wins daily.

“They are feeling her loss more than anyone else, particularly in such unique circumstances,” the spokesperson said.

Moody said the number of incidents had dropped dramatically since racing administrators increased protective measures following a number of accidents in big races.

This has included CT and MRI scans for horses before they are passed fit to run in the Melbourne Cup, which became mandatory after a spate of international runners died in the race. The results of the tests determine whether the horse is cleared to run.
