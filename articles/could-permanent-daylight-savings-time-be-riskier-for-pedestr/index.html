Could permanent daylight savings time be riskier for pedestrians?

TheTools · 08.11.2026 10:00 · source · permalink

ASphotowed/iStockPhoto / Getty Images

With all the news coverage of permanent daylight saving time here, I’ve heard worries that there could be more pedestrians hit by cars. Why is that? I’m not a fan of the idea, but since days are so short in the winter already, could changing sunrise and sunset by an hour make that much difference? – Brynn, Calgary

When it comes to road safety, there might not be a bright side for permanent daylight saving time.

With later sunrises, more of us will be walking to school or work in the dark – but, worse, many drivers won’t be fully awake, an expert said.

“You’re not as alert – your body is saying, ‘No, you should be in bed,’” said Michael Antle, a professor and head of the psychology department at the University of Calgary. “Your reaction times are slower. You might not notice [pedestrians], you might not respond quickly enough and you might make the wrong decision.”

Because your internal clock relies on morning sunlight to tell you when to wake up, leaving for school or work when it’s still dark can leave you feeling jet-lagged, said Antle, who researches circadian rhythms.

That’s because on Nov. 1, British Columbia, Alberta, Manitoba, parts of northwestern Ontario and the Northwest Territories didn’t turn their clocks back to standard time, joining Yukon and Saskatchewan, which already stay on the same time year-round.

In Calgary, for instance, the sun now rises around 8:40 a.m., an hour later than it would under standard time. And by Dec. 31, it won’t come up until around 9:40 a.m.

Alberta made the move to permanent daylight time even though voters narrowly rejected the idea in a 2021 referendum – and the province said it will “gather feedback” after the first full year.

“This weekend we’ll hit the latest sunrise ever for our location,” Antle said. “And we’re only a week into this experiment.”

For some people, that morning fatigue could linger until February or March, as days start to get longer again, he said.

“Everybody’s going to be a little bit different,” Antle said.

Dark days ahead?

So, will skipping the clock change lead to more crashes?

“It’s too soon to tell, but I have no doubt that it will have an impact in the morning,” said Staff Sergeant Andy Woodward of the Calgary Police Service’s traffic unit. “Mornings are going to be the area of concern [because] kids are going to school.”

While Woodward said Calgary didn’t see serious morning crashes in the first few days of keeping clocks ahead, a pedestrian was seriously injured the week before in a 7:23 a.m. crash where the driver hadn’t seen the pedestrian.

“This week at [8 a.m.] it’s as dark as it would be at midnight,” he said. “Your view is going to be so much more limited because of the darkness and you really need to slow down – and perhaps go even lower than the posted speed limit [around] schools and pedestrians.”

We checked with police in a few other cities in B.C. and Alberta to see whether they’d seen a spike in collisions this week and didn’t get an immediate answer.

But research suggests dark mornings are riskier for pedestrians – especially kids. A recent U.S. study found that fatal morning crashes involving school-age pedestrians were about twice as likely when it was dark.

And, unless schools move to later start times, permanent daylight time will mean more kids are walking to school or being dropped off in the dark.

If school starts at 8:30 a.m., for example, kids in Calgary will be heading there before sunrise until March. That’s why Woodward recommends that all pedestrians, including kids, wear reflective clothing and consider carrying a flashlight.

Safer in the evening?

Some supporters of permanent daylight time say that a later sunset could mean fewer pedestrian crashes during the evening rush hour. But research is limited because nearly everywhere that has tried permanent daylight time – including Russia and the U.S. – has switched either back to changing the clocks or to permanent standard time, Antle said. In Calgary the sun will now set around 5:30 p.m. in mid-December - instead of 4:30 p.m.

But that means the sun will be low in the sky – potentially making it tough to see if you’re driving into it – in the middle of the evening commute. When the sun is low, it shines straight into drivers’ eyes, and that causes crashes, Woodward said. But the bigger safety issue isn’t about clocks – it’s how people drive, he said.

“We’ve seen speeds in excess of 200 kilometres an hour [on some freeways],” Woodward said. “This kind of driving has just got to stop because there are so many more vehicles on the road now than we’ve ever had before.”

Calgary has seen 33 traffic deaths, including 10 pedestrians, so far this year. Last year, the city had 38 fatalities, including 15 pedestrians – the most in a decade.

Most crashes come down to a combination of speed and distraction, Woodward said.

”I think a lot of people think it will never happen to them," he said. “Unfortunately, statistics say [that] at some point, it will.”
