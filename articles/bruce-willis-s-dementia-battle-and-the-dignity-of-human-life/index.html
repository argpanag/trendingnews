Bruce Willis’s Dementia Battle and the Dignity of Human Life

TheTools · 27.11.2026 11:00 · source · permalink

by Kathryn Jean Lopez

Share this story

Bruce Willis in Shanghai, China, in 2019

VCG/VCG via Getty Images

Key Takeaways

  • Bruce Willis’s family offers a moving reminder: Illness cannot erase human dignity or diminish our obligation to love.
  • Governor Casey’s pro-life convictions expose today’s partisan failures and challenge public servants to defend the vulnerable with courage.

No, I am not about to write about Die Hard being a Christmas movie.

If you know about the endless annual social media debate, you know all too well and would like us to quickly move on — or I’ve already lost you to eggnog and the movie itself. (Die Hard, by the way, is most obviously not a Christmas movie, considering that its most famous quote involves a word we should not be saying in polite company — or ever — never mind to celebrate the birth of Christ.)

I saw the other day that one of Bruce Willis’s daughters posted a photo of herself with her father before deleting it and apologizing. It’s unclear to me whether someone said something to her — plenty of commenters apparently found it sweet. Obviously, if she thought differently about it hours later, that’s her prerogative — she said it was too intimate. She owes the public, so to speak, nothing, as she accompanies her father on this painful journey with frontotemporal dementia.

But I do hope Tallulah Willis or someone in the Willis family sees this: Thank you. Thank you for sharing your father/husband/friend with us in this most vulnerable time.

Yes, they have resources and support. They are getting tons of messages of love and prayers. And even if the actor agreed early on to share details of his disease, as his symptoms take over their lives, he can’t consent, and there have to be a million different feelings about his privacy going through their hearts and minds. We know him for his dashing humor on Moonlighting and as a rugged NYPD hero in his most famous movie franchise.

They are doing this naturally, as people living their lives in the public eye. He’s changed, but he’s still alive, and he’s still beloved.

That photo that was deleted shows him surrounded by love. He’s clearly, visually different, and yet still obviously Bruce Willis.

Ben Sasse, with cancer, is actively able to choose to show how he is making every minute count, talking about it bluntly and with amazing grace. The mere existence of Bruce Willis, loved, is a grace and lesson without speeches and an upcoming book about redeeming the time. And still, they are accompanying him and loving him — the whole Willis family, which includes in the broken, complicated ways of the world his former wife, Demi Moore, mother of three of his daughters including their youngest, Tallulah.

John Paul II, Ben Sasse, Bruce Willis. Not the trinity you might have been expecting to show us how to live while we are dying, and yet, here we are.

Willis, by the way, has expressed skepticism about religion, suggesting it is “mythology.” But that was in George magazine, if you are old enough to remember that (and increasingly our hires at NR aren’t — which is a healthy sign of life and hope in and of itself), and so a good while ago.

Pray for him and his family. I suspect they would not mind.

And, actually, in fact, in that interview with George around the release of Armageddon, this is what he said:

“The bastions of Catholicism and Christianity have a very strong hold on this country,” he says. “Organized religions in general, in my opinion, are dying forms. They were all very important when we didn’t know why the sun moved, why weather changed, why hurricanes occur or volcanoes happened. Modern religion is the end trail of modern mythology. But there are people who interpret the Bible literally. Literally! I choose not to believe that’s the way. And that’s what makes America cool, you know?”

We could actually take that as a pretty robust defense of religious freedom, God bless him.

They Don’t Make Them Like Governor Casey Anymore

Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro was tweeting last weekend about abortion. And so I couldn’t help but miss, as I often do, his predecessor, Governor Robert Casey.

He said this at a conference in 1993; it is as true as it ever was (entered in the Congressional Record by then-Representative Jim Talent, the Republican from Missouri):

All of us are joined in our conviction that abortion is a bad thing. And although many of us are Catholics, we are also joined in the conviction that abortion is not simply a Catholic concern. It’s a catholic concern with the small “c”—the concern of anyone who rejects the idea of human life as a disposable commodity. The concern of anyone with eyes to see, a mind to reason, and a heart to feel.
It is not an arrogant boast, but a demographic fact, that most Americans share this conviction. Anytime the question is put squarely to them, “Do you oppose abortion on demand?” more than two out of three Americans answer yes. Asked if they favor restrictions on abortion such as we have enacted in Pennsylvania, again a majority of 70 to 80 percent say yes. Perhaps the most telling survey of all found that 78 percent of the people would outlaw 93 percent of all abortions—all but the familiar hard cases. Even in the last election, in which all sides sought to shelve the issue of abortion, exit polls revealed its central importance in the minds of most voters.
To those who favor liberal abortion policies, this persistent opposition is a mystery, a disturbing sign of something backward and intolerant in our society. Sometimes the abortion lobby pretty much concedes that Americans by and large favor restrictions on abortion—as when Pennsylvania’s abortion laws were upheld by the Supreme Court. Such setbacks to their cause leave abortion advocates bewildered and alarmed, convinced that Americans still need to be “educated on the issue.” . . .
As I read the polls showing our continuing unease with abortion, nothing makes me more proud to call myself an American. Among the “herd of independent minds” who make up our opinion leaders, abortion may be taken as a mark of progress. But most Americans have not followed. In the abortion lobby’s strange sense of the word, America has never been a “progressive” nation. For we know—and this used to be the credo of my party—that progress can never come by exploiting or sacrificing any one class of people. Progress is a hollow word unless everyone is counted in and no one written off, especially the most weak and vulnerable among us. You cannot stifle this debate with a piece of paper. No edict, no federal mandate can put to rest the grave doubts of the American people. Legal abortion will never rest easy on this nation’s conscience. It will continue to haunt the consciences of men and women everywhere. The plain facts of biology, the profound appeals of the heart, are far too unsettling to ever fade away.

And for a rant on that old “personally opposed” line from politicians, see Scott Colom from Mississippi in last week’s Lifeline.

Governor Casey was more respected by people outside his party than by fellow Democrats. There really is no excuse for their extremism on abortion — even if the Republican Party has no moral leadership spine — and so many of his words are as relevant as they were when he delivered them.

We need more pro-life Democrats. We need more pro-life people in public service. We need more pro-life people with courage and love.
