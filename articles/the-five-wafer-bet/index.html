The Five-Wafer Bet

TheTools · 27.11.2026 07:29 · source · permalink

A paper buried in the ISSCC advance program suggests Huawei is building high-bandwidth memory a different way.

Daniel almost skipped the email. The ISSCC advance program lands in his inbox every November, a PDF thick with session titles and author affiliations, and most years he scans it in ten minutes. This year, a title on page forty-one stopped him.

"A 64-Channel 4096-DQ Custom HBM with Memory Controllers and 5-Layer Wafer-on-Wafer Hybrid Bonding."

He read it twice. Then he opened a spreadsheet and typed the numbers.

HBM3E runs a 1024-bit interface. HBM4 doubles that to 2048. This paper claimed 4096. He checked the affiliation. Huawei, Shenzhen. Five layers of wafer-on-wafer bonding. Memory controllers integrated into the stack.

Daniel had spent eleven years covering the memory supply chain from a desk in Taipei. A 4096-bit interface was not a mystery to him. Neither was the fact that nobody had built one. The standard roadmap ran through narrower interfaces at higher frequencies, each generation squeezing more speed from the same pin count. This design did the opposite. It widened the pipe and slowed the clock.

He pulled up the hybrid bonding literature. Wafer-on-wafer stacking had been around for years, mostly in image sensors and NAND. ChangXin had shipped 232-layer NAND using multi-wafer bonding. But DRAM was different. DRAM needed speed, and stacking added latency. Or so the conventional wisdom went.

At lunch, Daniel met his old colleague James, who had left the analyst world for a packaging startup in Hsinchu. James ordered beef noodles and listened.

"Five layers," James said. "That's four bonding interfaces. You lose one layer to a defect, the whole stack dies."

"Unless the yield per layer is high enough," Daniel said.

"Unless," James agreed. He stirred his soup. "But here's the thing. If you skip chip-on-wafer, you skip the pick-and-place step. That's where a lot of the cost sits. And you skip the TSVs. Those things eat area and yield."

Daniel wrote that down. No through-silicon vias meant more room for memory cells. No chip-on-wafer meant fewer assembly steps. The trade was frequency for width, and the packaging did the work that lithography usually did.

After lunch, he called a source in Shenzhen. The source confirmed the paper was connected to Huawei's HiZQ program but would not give numbers. Frequency, capacity, bandwidth, the division of labor among the five layers. All embargoed until February.

"What about ChangXin?" Daniel asked.

"Watch the same conference," the source said. "There's a paper on wafer-on-wafer DRAM with gate-all-around."

Daniel hung up and sat with that. ChangXin was working on wafer-stacked DRAM. Huawei was working on a wide-interface HBM built from stacked wafers. If the two pieces fit together, the result was an HBM product built on mature process nodes, assembled through packaging rather than lithography.

He pulled up ChangXin's public roadmap. The company had been expanding capacity at its Hefei fab. A four-layer DRAM stack on an older node, combined with a wide interface, could reach density levels that usually required a more advanced process. Stack four of those, and you had something that looked like HBM. The math was speculative, but the direction was not.

He kept coming back to power. Wide interfaces consume more energy per bit than narrow, high-speed ones. A 4096-bit bus running at lower frequency might hit the same bandwidth as a 2048-bit bus at higher frequency, but the energy per bit would be higher. Inside an accelerator power budget, that mattered. The ISSCC paper would include power figures. Until then, the design was a hypothesis.

That evening, he walked through the Raohe night market, past the stinky tofu stands and the claw machines, and thought about what the industry had assumed for a decade. The assumption was that HBM required leading-edge DRAM. You needed the smallest node to fit enough cells in a small enough area. You needed the fastest transistors to hit the signaling rates.

This design questioned both. If the interface was wide enough, the DRAM could run slower. If the stacking was good enough, the density came from the vertical dimension, not the lateral one. A fab running an older node could, in theory, produce the memory. A packaging line could do the rest.

The catch was standardization. A custom HBM worked for Huawei's own accelerators. It did not plug into JEDEC sockets. It did not run on Nvidia's roadmap. The addressable market was narrow until the design became a standard product, and that required years of ecosystem work. Drivers, compilers, board designs. None of that happened at a conference.

But Daniel had covered enough technology transitions to know that the first version rarely mattered. What mattered was whether the approach worked at all. If it did, the cost curve bent in a new direction. The companies that controlled packaging would gain leverage over the companies that controlled lithography.

He stopped at a bubble tea stand and ordered oolong. The vendor asked if he wanted less sugar. He said yes.

Back at his desk, he opened a blank document and typed a single line: "Huawei's ISSCC paper suggests a path to HBM that does not require leading-edge DRAM."

Then he saved it and went to bed. The paper would not be presented for three months. The questions about power efficiency, yield at five layers, and software support would not be answered until then. But the direction was clear enough to write down.
