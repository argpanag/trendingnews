Triple lock to boost pensions by 3.9pc as Reynolds refuses to rule out tax raid

TheTools · 15.09.2026 18:51 · source · permalink

Key moments

The Business Secretary refused four times to rule out retirees paying tax on the state pension from next year.

Jonathan Reynolds declined to reassure pensioners that they would not pay income tax on the benefit for the first time, saying it was a question for the Chancellor.

The comments on Tuesday raised concern for millions of retired people expecting to see their state pension surpass £13,000 from April, forcing them to pay income tax.

However, pensions minister Torsten Bell later insisted Labour would not put up taxes on people who rely on the state pension.

It is still unclear how this will be implemented given the complexity of separating the tax arrangements of different groups of pensioners.

The rise under the triple lock guarantee would drag many pensioners into paying income tax for the first time because their state income would surpass the personal allowance of £12,570.

Rachel Reeves, the former chancellor, said last year that people solely reliant on the state pension would not have to pay the tax.

However, Mr Reynolds was repeatedly asked on BBC Breakfast whether Labour would honour this commitment after the next increase in the state pension in April. He declined to answer four times.

He said: “I’m sorry, that is for the Chancellor and for a Budget.

“The majority of pensioners in this country don’t just rely on the state pensions. Some do, I accept that, but the majority don’t.

“Sometimes when this is looked at, we’re focusing just on the state pension side of this. You’ve got to consider the full range of income retired people in the UK have.”

However Mr Bell, the pensions minister, walked back from Mr Reynolds comments.

He said: “In line with the commitment made at Budget 2025, pensioners who only just exceed the personal allowance will not have the administrative burden of paying small amounts of tax in this Parliament.

“The Chancellor will set out further details on how that commitment will be delivered at the Budget.”

Former pensions minister Sir Steve Webb said only one in 16 pensioners would benefit from this policy.

He said: “Those on the new state pension can expect to see an increase of nearly £500 per year next April.  

“But the sting in the tail is that this will take the standard rate of the new state pension above the tax threshold.”

The comments came after official figures on Tuesday indicated the triple lock will rise by 3.9pc from April.

Under the triple lock, the state pension rises each April in line with inflation, average wages or 2.5pc, whichever is highest.

Total pay, including bonuses, rose by 3.9pc in the three months to July, down from 4.2pc in the three months to June.

Pensioners may yet receive a bigger increase if September’s inflation figure is higher than 3.9pc. That will be revealed next month but a higher figure is thought to be unlikely.

A minister has insisted Labour will not put up taxes on people who rely on the state pension after one of his frontbench colleagues refused to rule out the measure.

Triple lock ‘adding to fiscal pressure’

The impending jump in the state is increasing pressure on the public finances ahead of John Healey’s first Budget, economists said.

The state pension is expected to rise by 3.9pc under the triple lock, in line with wage growth in the three months to July, which is not expected to be superseded by September’s inflation figures next month.

Liam McLaughlin of the National Institute of Economic and Social Research, said: “With CPI unlikely to surpass earnings by September, earnings will set the increase at 3.9pc, adding fiscal pressure at a time when the triple lock is already under scrutiny.”

State pension to rise by less than £10 a week

The state pension is on track to rise at the slowest pace in five years under the triple lock in a blow to pensioners battling the cost of living.

The vast majority of state pensions will rise by less than £10 a week after the 3.9pc increase in average wages in the three months to July, one of three key metrics used to calculate the annual rise.

For almost two million elderly people living in poverty, it will also do little to rescue their finances.

The latest increase will also ramp up pressure to ditch the triple lock, particularly among workers who received lower pay increases than millions of pensioners will now be getting.

At a cost of over £12bn a year and rising, the mechanism has created a Mexican stand-off between every major political party – and no one wants to blink first.

It also risks a so-called ‘ratchet effect’, where pay rises now lead to higher inflation later, handing retirees substantial back-to-back increases to their payouts that today’s workers will have to pay for.

Traders scale back bets on higher interest rates

Traders slightly reduced their bets on interest rate rises after the latest official employment figures.

Money markets suggest there is a 22pc chance of policymakers raising interest rates on Thursday, down from 25pc on Monday, after wage growth fell back from 4.2pc to 3.9pc in the three months to July.

In a sign of a weakening jobs market, the number of people on payrolls slumped by 26,000 during August to 30.2 million, following a 19,000 drop in July.

The number of vacancies fell to 702,000, which is the lowest since spring 2021.

Andrew Wishart, an economist at Berenberg, said: “Much looser labour market conditions than during the last major energy price shock in 2022 make a new price-wage spiral highly unlikely. 

“Nonetheless, we expect the Bank of England to signal that it will hike in November unless energy prices fall back, to guard against the risk of persistent high inflation.”

Labour is party of unemployment, say Tories

The new shadow chancellor said Labour’s refusal to rule out more tax rises was causing uncertainty for businesses.

Andrew Griffith MP said Labour was the “party of unemployment” after the Office for National Statistics (ONS) said there were around 8,000 fewer vacancies in the three months to August, and flagged wage pressures on small businesses.

Mr Griffith said: “Under Labour, job vacancies are at their lowest level in a decade outside the pandemic, 101,000 payrolled jobs have been lost over the past year, and the claimant count is surging.

“Labour is, and always has been, the party of unemployment.

“Businesses are still absorbing the cost of Angela Rayner’s disastrous (Un)Employment Rights Act, and Labour’s refusal to rule out more tax rises is causing huge uncertainty for employers.

“Only the Conservatives have a plan to get Britain working again. We’ll cut the red tape that makes it harder for employers to hire and deliver the lower, simpler taxes our country needs.”

Private sector workers worse off than last year

The cost of living crisis is still haunting British workers. In the private sector, the average worker’s weekly earnings are now £2 lower in real terms than last October, according to the Resolution Foundation. 

This is bad news at a time when the war in Iran and climate change are driving up fuel and food costs, with knock-on effects across the economy. 

Inflation is poised to rise, just as private sector wage growth has slipped to its lowest in six years. Finding a job is tougher than it has been in a long time too: vacancies have slipped further to 702,000. Outside of Covid, this is the lowest since 2014. 

Julia Diniz, economist at the Resolution Foundation, said: “The big winners from today’s ONS data are pensioners, who are set for another large rise in the state pension next spring thanks to the triple lock.

“The biggest losers are workers in the private sector who are already earning less than they were last autumn. With wage growth slumping to its lowest rate in nearly six years, the UK’s private sector pay squeeze will tighten over the coming months as inflation rises.”

Ms Diniz warned that young people are in for a rough ride too, as the slump in vacancies means finding work is difficult.

Reynolds refuses to rule out tax raid on state pensioners

The Business Secretary refused four times to rule out pensioners being taxed on the state pension next year.

Jonathan Reynolds declined to reassure pensioners that they would not pay taxes on their income, saying it was a question for the Chancellor.

The state pension is in line to surpass £13,000 for the first time from April after official figures on Tuesday showed average wages including bonuses rose by 3.9pc in the three months to July.

The rise under the triple lock would drag many pensioners into paying tax but former chancellor Rachel Reeves said last year that people whose only income comes from the state pension would not have to pay tax.

Asked if pensioners would have to pay tax, Mr Reynolds told BBC Breakfast: “A pension is income, and of course, the vast majority of people in Britain have their own private pension provision alongside the state pension.

“So this wouldn’t be a substantial change for them. They’d already certainly received an income in that case above the personal allowance.

“Now, any changes to personal allowances to tax rates, you know, I’m going to say, take place in the Budget. That’s on the 28th of October. But I do think sometimes when we talk about this, and of course the government’s commitment to the triple lock means that that’s the reason we’re seeing significant increases in the state pension.”

Asked if the Government would stick to Ms Reeves’s pledge, he said: “I’m sorry, that is for the Chancellor and for a Budget.

“The majority of pensions in this country don’t just rely on the state pensions. Some do, I accept that, but the majority don’t.

“So I think sometimes when this is looked at, we’re focusing just on the state pension side of this. You’ve got to consider the full range of income retired people in the UK have living the good lives they deserve in their retirement.”

Rise in triple lock to reignite public debate over costs

Pensioners will next year likely get a 3.9pc pay rise, thanks to the triple lock. 

The figure is linked to average total earnings for workers in May to July. 

The triple lock guarantees that state pensions rise in line with the highest of average wages, inflation or a baseline 2.5pc. 

But the costs are putting increasing pressure on the public finances. 

State pensions already cost £16bn more a year than they would, had the triple lock not been introduced, according to the Institute for Fiscal Studies.  

As a result, spending on the state pension is £47bn higher after adjusting for inflation than in 2010. 

This is a significant rise at a time when a hard-pressed Chancellor must find billions of pounds more for defence, borrowing costs are high and public services are creaking. 

Many big names across the political spectrum - from Sir Jeremy Hunt to Harriet Harman - have called for it to go. 

Slowly, murmurs also are growing louder in Westminster of finding a cross-party consensus to slay the voter favourite. 

In the meantime, the bill is growing larger.

Borrowing costs hit 19-year high as state pension to surpass £13,000

The cost of government borrowing hit the highest level since 2007 after official figures showed the state pension is on track to surpass £13,000 for the first time.

The yield on 10-year gilts rose from 5.37pc to a fresh 19-year high of 5.41pc after official figures showed the triple lock will rise by at least 3.9pc next year.

The IFS said that under current forecasts from the Office for Budget Responsibility, the triple lock will push up state pension spending by £600m per year in 2029–30.

Heidi Karjalainen, senior research economist at the IFS, said: “Unless these figures are revised, this implies that the full new state pension will increase from the current £241.30 per week (or around £12,500 per year), to £250.70 per week (around £13,000 per year) next April.”

Government headcount at record high, as private sector struggles

The UK job market may be bad, but not for everyone. Central government has never employed more people, at 4,080,000 in June – up 34,000 in a year.

The Office for National Statistics said the driver of the rise was more hiring by the Civil Service, as well as some local authority schools becoming academies. This meant those school staff switched from being employed by local authorities to central government.

Employment in the public sector as a whole is at a 15-year-high, at 6,207,000.

It comes as payroll data shows that the number of employees across the economy is down, falling 145,000 in the year to August.

Retail and wholesale jobs took the biggest hit, losing 76,000 employees in a year.

Helen Dickinson, chief executive at the British Retail Consortium, said: “These job losses have not happened by accident. Retailers are being hit by a tidal wave of employment costs.

“In just two years, increases to National Insurance Contributions and the National Living Wage have added £6.5 billion in costs, while further employment reforms risk making it even harder to create and sustain jobs.”

It comes as public sector workers saw their pay increase by 6.3pc, compared to increases of only 2.9pc in the private sector.

Vacancies hit five-year low

Job vacancies fell to a fresh five-year low as small businesses continue to hold back from hiring over soaring wage costs, according to official figures.

The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said there were around 8,000 fewer vacancies quarter on quarter in the three months to August, at 702,000, which remains the lowest since spring 2021, or, outside the Covid pandemic years, for over a decade.

Small firms are flagging ongoing pressures from rising wage bills as a factor holding them back from taking on staff, according to the ONS.

The data showed regular average weekly earnings growth remained unchanged at 3.5pc in the three months to July, while it continues to outstrip inflation, rising by 0.8pc with the Consumer Prices Index taken into account.

The UK unemployment rate also remained unchanged at 4.9pc in the three months to July, but more timely data estimates that workers on payrolls slumped by 26,000 during August to 30.2 million, following a 19,000 drop in July, though the latest figures are subject to revision.

Liz McKeown, ONS director of economic statistics, said: “Payrolled employee numbers continue to edge down, with falls over the past year particularly evident in the retail and hospitality sectors.

“Vacancies remain at their lowest level outside the pandemic period for more than a decade, with smaller businesses continuing to report that increased labour costs are affecting hiring decisions.”
