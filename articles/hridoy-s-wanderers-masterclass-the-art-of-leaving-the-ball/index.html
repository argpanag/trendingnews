Hridoy’s Wanderers masterclass: The art of leaving the ball

TheTools · 16.11.2026 09:16 · source · permalink

In an era when T20 cricket has made batting synonymous with hitting, Tawhid Hridoy offered a timely reminder at the Wanderers on Sunday that Test cricket's greatest weapon can sometimes be the bat that never moves.

There is something deeply satisfying about watching a batter dispatch a fast bowler through the covers. The crack of the bat, the ball racing to the boundary and the bowler's frustrated walk back to his mark are among Test cricket's enduring pleasures.

But there is another sight, perhaps less celebrated but equally beautiful: a batter watching a delivery outside off stump pass harmlessly through to the wicketkeeper.

At Johannesburg's Wanderers, Hridoy reminded cricket lovers of the disappearing art of leaving the ball, demonstrating that sometimes the best way to dominate a world-class bowling attack is to refuse to play its deliveries.

The timing of his masterclass was particularly significant. Modern cricket, increasingly influenced by the explosive demands of T20, has transformed batting into an exercise in aggression. The prevailing instinct is to hit, manufacture a scoring opportunity and put the bowler under pressure. The distinction between a good delivery and a scoring opportunity has become increasingly blurred. Even in longer formats, batters sometimes appear reluctant to let a ball go untouched.

But Test cricket demands a different conversation. It is as much about survival, judgement and occupation of the crease as it is about scoring runs. And Hridoy understood that perfectly.

Bangladesh's morning at the Wanderers had begun disastrously. Kagiso Rabada, breathing fire with the new ball, ripped through the top order to leave the visitors reeling at 22 for 3 inside nine overs. The pitch with a tinge of grass offered movement, the bounce was unpredictable, and South Africa sensed an opportunity to wrap up the innings cheaply.

Hridoy, however, refused to be drawn into the trap.

Against Rabada's pace and Marco Jansen's awkward angle and steep bounce, he repeatedly demonstrated the discipline to leave deliveries outside his off stump. Some were close enough to tempt an ambitious drive; others demanded considerable judgement to let pass. Yet Hridoy trusted his judgement of the off stump and resisted the instinct to chase.

It was not defensive batting born out of fear. It was calculated defiance.

Every leave was a small victory. Every ball that passed harmlessly through to the wicketkeeper was another reminder to the bowler that Hridoy would not play on his terms. Rather than offer Rabada the outside edge or allow Jansen to exploit his reach, he made them search for a different line and length.

At the other end, Litton Das was taking notice.

After the day's play, Litton explained why Hridoy's approach had such a significant influence on his own innings.

"The discipline with which he batted, leaving the ball, gave me more confidence. We wanted to bat long."

It was a revealing admission. Hridoy's discipline was not merely protecting his own wicket; it was reassuring his partner that there was no need to force the pace.

In a format where confidence can quickly disappear under sustained pressure, Hridoy's composure became contagious.

The two produced a remarkable sixth-wicket partnership of 206, Bangladesh's highest stand against South Africa for any wicket. From the ruins of 22 for 3, they took their side to 327 for 6 at stumps, establishing a position that few could have imagined during the morning session.

Hridoy's contribution was magnificent: 137 off 227 deliveries, including 14 fours and a six. He became only the second Bangladesh batter, after Mahmudul Hasan Joy, to score a Test century against South Africa on South African soil.

His achievement also earned the admiration of the very bowler who had terrorised Bangladesh's top order. Rabada, reflecting on the partnership, acknowledged the resistance: "They really applied themselves." He also offered Hridoy a generous tribute: "We threw everything at him. We did. What can I say? Hats off."

Coming from a bowler of Rabada's quality, those words carried considerable weight. He had tested Hridoy with pace, bounce and movement, yet the Bangladesh batter refused to be drawn into playing deliveries that offered little scoring opportunity but considerable risk. 

It was a battle of patience as much as technique, and Hridoy had shown that sometimes the best way to counter a fast bowler is to deny him the response he is looking for.

There is a reason the leave has traditionally been considered one of cricket's most sophisticated skills. It requires an understanding of the bowler's release, the pitch, the movement off the surface and, above all, the precise position of the stumps. As cricket writer Gideon Haigh has observed, it is arguably the subtlest act in the game.

Geoffrey Boycott, in the MCC Masterclass coaching manual, also explained how leaving deliveries outside off stump can manipulate a bowler into changing his line and bowling where the batter wants. The leave, therefore, is not merely a defensive technique; it is a way of making the bowler operate on the batter's terms.

Hridoy demonstrated that at the Wanderers. His century was built on an understanding that patience is not the opposite of aggression; it is often the foundation of it.

He eventually fell to Marco Jansen on the final delivery of the day, trapped lbw for 137 after an unsuccessful review. But that final ball could not diminish what had preceded it.

The Wanderers witnessed more than a young batter scoring a hundred in difficult conditions. They witnessed a cricketer rediscovering the value of judgement, restraint and self-belief.

At the Wanderers, Hridoy showed that in a game increasingly obsessed with how far a batter can hit the ball, knowing which balls not to hit remains an art worth celebrating.
