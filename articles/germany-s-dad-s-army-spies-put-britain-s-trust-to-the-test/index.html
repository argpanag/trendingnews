Germany’s ‘Dad’s Army’ spies put Britain’s trust to the test

TheTools · 07.10.2026 07:04 · source · permalink

Arrest of former espionage chief exposes doubts over Federal Intelligence Service agency

The arrest of a former spy chief has exposed lingering doubts over whether Britain can trust Germany’s intelligence agency.

The Federal Intelligence Service (BND) has suffered from decades of underinvestment since the end of the Cold War.

Successive German governments seized the “peace dividend” to make deep cuts to an agency that was once a valuable ally to the UK.

They created an underfunded and bureaucratic “Dad’s Army” with agents that until recently were banned from firing their own weapons.

August Hanning, the German spy chief arrested for espionage and treason on Tuesday, was head of the BND from 1998 to 2005, during this period of underinvestment.

The BND was founded in 1956. Its predecessor was an American-backed group of ex-Nazi military intelligence agents called the Gehlen Organisation.

It was a fearsome opponent of Warsaw bloc spies during the 1950s and 1960s. It provided reliable intelligence on Soviet forces behind the Iron Curtain to Nato.

With the CIA, it was the secret owner of the world’s most successful cryptography company, enabling it to read supposedly secret diplomatic messages sent around the world.

In 1967, it predicted the outbreak of the Six Day War fought by Israel in the Middle East almost to the hour before it began.

Such accuracy contrasted sharply with the German insistence that Vladimir Putin was not about to invade Ukraine in February 2022.

But even during the BND’s heyday, there were high-profile infiltrations by the Stasi, the East German secret police.

Most notoriously, there was the former SS officer Heinz Felfe, a BND spy turned double agent for the Soviet Union in the 1950s and 1960s.

Mindful of the shadow cast by the Gestapo, the agency was never celebrated by Germany’s politicians.

During the Cold War, the Stasi in Russia-controlled East Germany carried out extensive surveillance and wiretapping.

That dark history created a society deeply suspicious of surveillance and jealously protective of its privacy and data.

When the Cold War ended in 1990, the cuts began. They continued for more than two decades in a period in which Germany reduced its defence and security spending by more than half.

In 1991, defence spending was 3.9 per cent of total government spending. It was down to 1.1 per cent of GDP when Russia annexed Crimea in 2014.

The BND was left vulnerable, defanged and dependent on US and British intelligence.

It was not just the lack of money that constrained the BND but laws preventing it from taking the muscular action one would associate with the CIA, Mossad or MI6.

German intelligence was banned from holding arrest, police or executive powers from its foundation. In 1978, laws were passed forcing its bosses to answer to politicians to prevent it becoming a new secret police.

There was anger in Germany after the Edward Snowden leaks in 2013, which revealed the BND had helped the US National Security Agency spy on European allies, including France.

In the fallout, a new restrictive law was passed limiting data-sharing with the UK and US in 2016.

Four years later, the Federal Constitutional Court ruled that privacy protections applied to everyone worldwide and not just Germans, making it illegal for the BND to intercept foreign telecommunications abroad.

Bruno Kahl, a former head of the BND, described the agency as “being tied up with a thousand threads like the giant Gulliver”.

The folly of shackling a nation’s spies and starving them of funding was laid bare by Putin’s invasion of Ukraine and fears over the reliability of Donald Trump’s America.

Under Friedrich Merz, the German chancellor, a substantial rearmament drive is under way to put more cash into the 6,500-strong BND. Its budget has been raised by about a quarter to €1.51bn (£1.28bn) this year as it looks to modernise.

Reforms passed over the summer will, for the first time, allow BND secret agents to fire their service weapons, commit sabotage abroad and spread disinformation to further an operation.

Changing the culture of a bureaucratic and risk-averse organisation will not be easy, and neither will losing the BND its unwanted reputation for infiltration.

In late 2022, a senior BND officer called Carsten L was arrested for high treason. He is accused of passing highly classified documents on the war in Ukraine and intelligence gathering to Moscow. The trial continues.

Establishment politicians in Germany accuse the pro-Putin Alternative for Germany, currently the country’s most popular party, of taking money from the Kremlin, which it denies.

Germany has long been described as a “weak link” within Nato because of the foothold Russian agents have in the country.

In 2024, a recording of a conversation between members of the German air force was broadcast on Russian television.

The officers’ conference call revealed details about British “people on the ground” deployed in Ukraine to assist the war-torn country.

At the time, Mr Hanning said the leak could just be the “tip of the iceberg”.

There is a certain level of distrust of Berlin’s security protocols which has probably influenced how much information is shared with Germany.

Insiders warn that successive German governments have lived in denial about the Kremlin foothold in Germany for decades, even after their allies confronted them.

The Germans spent months, if not years, trying to scupper proposals for a ban on the free movement of Russian diplomats around the EU’s Schengen Zone.

It had been argued that many of these Russian envoys were using diplomatic cover for their work as spies.

Berlin often argued about the possible retaliations facing its own diplomats without listening to the arguments being put forward by European allies about the threats posed by the Russians working within the EU.

Last autumn, Mr Merz said it was “our ambition that the BND operates at the highest level of intelligence”. The chancellor has also vowed to make the German army Europe’s strongest.

But such promises are undermined by suspicions that hundreds of Russian agents are still roaming a Germany shocked by the arrest of one of its most senior spy chiefs.

The scandal may also serve as a warning to Britain not to skimp when it comes to funding defence, spies and security.
