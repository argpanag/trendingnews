Apple (AAPL) unveiled its first foldable phone, the iPhone Duo, and a slew of product updates at its annual fall event at its Cupertino, Calif., headquarters today. Newly minted CEO John Ternus, who took over from Tim Cook on Sept. 1, hosted the keynote.

Duo features a passport-like design that opens and closes like a book. Up front, the device sports a 5.4-inch display. Open the phone to reveal a massive 7.6-inch display that's ideal for watching movies, playing games, doing work, and multitasking.

When unfolded, Apple says the Duo is its thinnest iPhone yet. Pricing starts at $1,999.

The company also unveiled its iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max. The phones have a similar look and feel to last year's 17 Pro and Pro Max, but get faster A20 Pro processors with improved CPUs, GPUs, and neural engine upgrades for running everything from games to AI models.

The batteries are also updated, with the 18 Pro getting 24 hours of charge and the iPhone 18 Pro Max getting 30 hours.

They also come with a variable mechanical aperture for better control of light and depth.

Apple also showed off its latest Apple Watch Series 12 and Ultra 4, as well as AirPods 5.

  • Analyst reacts to iPhone Duo: The software is the differentiator

    Creative Strategies CEO and principal analyst Ben Bajarin expects demand for Apple's new iPhone Duo will be "very high" once consumers get their hands on the new tech.

    "We think they're going to sell all they can make," Bajarin said. "I do think there will be a huge swing of demand for iPhone Duo. I think the software here is one of those things that really shines with Apple's vertical integration. Yes, the hardware is good, there are good other foldable hardwares, but I think the software really is where they differentiated this with the animations."

    Bajarin noted that at a roughly $2,000 price point, the iPhone Duo is expensive, "but not as expensive as rumored."

    At that level, Bajarin predicts Apple will be able to grab 20% to 25% of the foldable smartphone market with its new offering.

    That's nowhere near the scale of the iPhone 18 and iPhone 18 Pro, but still a "good chunk of the market," he noted.

  • What it's like to use the iPhone Duo

    Apple's first foldable is here. The iPhone Duo marks the biggest redesign of the company's most important products and one of its most ambitious devices to date.

    It's also pretty fun to use. I got to try out the Duo for a few fleeting moments following Apple's keynote at its headquarters in Cupertino, Calif., and was surprised to see how smoothly the whole package came together.

    Thanks to its passport-like design, the book-style foldable sat nicely in my hand when closed. The 5.6-inch front display is as clear and crisp as you'd expect from an iPhone.

    Unfolding the Duo is satisfyingly easy. While it closes tightly, there's a slight assist when you open it up. The main 7.6-inch screen has a nano-texture finish that gives it a matte look, which Apple says helps reduce glare.

  • Apple turns the iPhone Duo into 'a value engine': IDC

    IDC's Francisco Jeromino weighed in on Apple's new foldable phone, the iPhone Duo, as well as multiple product updates at its annual fall event in Cupertino, Calif.

    "Each new product adds meaningful improvements, but the iPhone Duo is the event's strategic flagship and a direct test of whether Apple can use design, ecosystem integration, and premium positioning to reshape a segment that has so far remained selective.

  • AT&T CEO John Stankey on $1,999 Apple iPhone Duo: 'This is not new'

    AT&T CEO John Stankey weighed in on Apple's new Duo foldable iPhone on Wednesday, saying that the wireless carrier will have to wait and see whether it moves the needle for Apple fans and consumers.

    "The best characterization I can give is, looking at the foldable phones that have been out in the market, this is not new," Stankey told Yahoo Finance's Brian Sozzi from the Goldman Sachs Communacopia & Tech Conference.

    Stankey reminded Apple fans that Android has already had foldable phones available for purchase.

    "Android users are as passionate about Android as iOS users are about iOS," Stankey said. "[They're a] smaller percentage of the US market, but they are still passionate, and there's been some really good foldable devices in the Android ecosystem for a couple years. And what we've seen is that it tends to function more as a specific niche type application as opposed to it's broadly accepted."

    Still, there will be a market for Apple's latest device, particularly among those die-hard Apple fans.

    "Will iOS users view it differently or will there be some unique take that Apple comes up with that changes that penetration dynamic a little bit?" he said. "I suppose it's possible, but my guess right now is we're going to kind of see it contained to a portion of the user base that wants that larger device and is maybe willing to invest more money to pick it up and get it."

  • The most-anticipated day in Apple's recent history

    Apple's annual iPhone launch event today will come with several firsts, Yahoo Finance's Hamza Shaban and Ethan Wolff-Mann pointed out in today's Morning Brief newsletter. They write:

    The stakes are higher and more urgent than the anticipated hardware debuts.

    Wall Street and the public alike are still waiting for Apple to unveil more of its AI strategy and get a more concrete understanding of how an upgraded Siri will turbocharge the iPhone experience.

    And on the question of AI development, how will Ternus expand Apple's famous "walled garden" to include AI agents and an ecosystem of AI tools that has largely grown outside of Cupertino's purview?

    There are device unveilings, the AI and innovation questions, and then curiosity and anticipation around the man himself.

    If Tim Cook was a skilled diplomat and supply chain wizard, how might Ternus differentiate himself and answer the years-long criticism of a rudderless, aura-deficient Apple?

    The keynote won't reveal everything. But first impressions, especially for a company so heavily reliant on its brand, will set the tone. Ternus can show off a nifty, foldable phone and a smarter Siri. But most of all, he'll have to tell an eager audience what happens next.

  • Foldables are a small portion of the smartphone market, but they're worth it for companies

    The foldable smartphone segment makes up a small slice of the broader market. According to International Data Corporation (IDC) analyst Francisco Jeronimo, foldables will account for 2.2% of global units in 2026, rising to just 3.1% by 2030.

    But the category's higher average selling price (ASP) means they make up a larger revenue share of 6.9% in 2026 and 10% in 2030.

    In other words, Apple (AAPL) won't have to sell as many foldables as its standard phones if it launches with its anticipated $2,000 price tag.

    Still, the company has some serious competition in the form of Samsung and Google here in the US, and Huawei in China. Jeronimo notes that the Huawei in particular will prove troublesome for Apple, as it accounted for 79.4% of foldable units in China and 82.2% of foldable revenue in Q2 this year.

    But he says Apple could steal the value lead from Huawei as long as the foldable is a success.

  • Apple iPhone changes represent a huge opportunity for the company. They also come with risks.

    Apple's (AAPL) iPhone is the company's biggest money maker by a long shot.

    In 2025, the segment accounted for $209.5 billion of Apple's $416.1 billion in total revenue. It's second largest business, its Services segment, generated $109.1 billion.

    That makes any changes to the phone, like the expected foldable iPhone, a major challenge for Apple.

    If it's a success out of the gate, the foldable will be a massive win for the company after years of commentary that it hasn't done enough to evolve its flagship smartphone.

    But a slip-up could prove to be a serious headwind for Apple. After all, first impressions are everything in the consumer tech space. And any disruption to the company's most important product would be especially troublesome for both Apple and investors.

  • Why Apple investors may not need to worry about a $2,000 iPhone price tag

    With gas prices back on the rise, there is some chatter on the Street about whether people will be willing to spend a rumored $2,000 on a new Apple (AAPL) iPhone that folds like a passport.

    The data shows, however, that consumers continue to shop for ever more expensive iPhones, no matter if filling up the tank is costlier or economic uncertainty is the theme of the day.

    JPMorgan analyst Samik Chatterjee wrote in a new note that there is a limited relationship between price and volume on the iPhone over a multiyear period. iPhone units increased about 10% between fiscal year 2015 and fiscal year 2026 despite a roughly 50% increase in average selling prices and a smartphone market that peaked around 2016-17.

    The mix — what consumers are buying from Apple's iPhone lineup — has also shifted toward premium devices, with the Pro and Pro Max increasing from about 37% of shipments in 2020 to roughly 65% in 2025

Foldables are a small portion of the smartphone market, but they're worth it for companies

The foldable smartphone segment makes up a small slice of the broader market. According to International Data Corporation (IDC) analyst Francisco Jeronimo, foldables will account for 2.2% of global units in 2026, rising to just 3.1% by 2030.

But the category's higher average selling price (ASP) means they make up a larger revenue share of 6.9% in 2026 and 10% in 2030.

In other words, Apple (AAPL) won't have to sell as many foldables as its standard phones if it launches with its anticipated $2,000 price tag.

Still, the company has some serious competition in the form of Samsung and Google here in the US, and Huawei in China. Jeronimo notes that the Huawei in particular will prove troublesome for Apple, as it accounted for 79.4% of foldable units in China and 82.2% of foldable revenue in Q2 this year.

But he says Apple could steal the value lead from Huawei as long as the foldable is a success.

Apple iPhone changes represent a huge opportunity for the company. They also come with risks.

Apple's (AAPL) iPhone is the company's biggest money maker by a long shot.

In 2025, the segment accounted for $209.5 billion of Apple's $416.1 billion in total revenue. It's second largest business, its Services segment, generated $109.1 billion.

That makes any changes to the phone, like the expected foldable iPhone, a major challenge for Apple.

If it's a success out of the gate, the foldable will be a massive win for the company after years of commentary that it hasn't done enough to evolve its flagship smartphone.

But a slip-up could prove to be a serious headwind for Apple. After all, first impressions are everything in the consumer tech space. And any disruption to the company's most important product would be especially troublesome for both Apple and investors.

Why Apple investors may not need to worry about a $2,000 iPhone price tag

With gas prices back on the rise, there is some chatter on the Street about whether people will be willing to spend a rumored $2,000 on a new Apple (AAPL) iPhone that folds like a passport.

The data shows, however, that consumers continue to shop for ever more expensive iPhones, no matter if filling up the tank is costlier or economic uncertainty is the theme of the day.

JPMorgan analyst Samik Chatterjee wrote in a new note that there is a limited relationship between price and volume on the iPhone over a multiyear period. iPhone units increased about 10% between fiscal year 2015 and fiscal year 2026 despite a roughly 50% increase in average selling prices and a smartphone market that peaked around 2016-17.
