Animals review – Ben Affleck’s dull Netflix thriller is lacking in bark and bite

TheTools · 08.10.2026 23:00 · source · permalink

Actor-director’s listless LA-set crime thriller makes for a surprisingly anonymous effort and one that feels very similar to many other, better films

Ben Affleck has pushed through multiple Hollywood comebacks, at least one predicated on his facility behind the camera, directing Boston-based thrillers both artistically ambitious (Gone Baby Gone) and juicily crowd-pleasing (The Town). So now that his acting career seems particularly stable, as he works steadily and produces movies via the company he founded with longtime friend and collaborator Matt Damon, why does his directing career remain in what seems like post-Oscar doldrums? (Though he has never been nominated for a best director Oscar, his film Argo won best picture in 2013.) His new movie Animals appears perfectly situated within his wheelhouse: it’s a Los Angeles-based crime-and-politics thriller to match his earlier east coast work. The real surprise, however, is just how listless it can get.

Affleck himself plays Mark Kimball, a Angeleno running for mayor of his home town. His status as an independent non-party-affiliated candidate (and one who scarcely exhibits a trace of a platform in what little we see of his stump speeches and interviews) is only one element of his unlikely backstory, which also includes history as a teacher, a lawyer and a veteran of the first Gulf war. (Not sure the math on all that checks out, but let’s allow it as evidence of a fusion between Mark’s dedication to service and a rapacious ambition.) He’s leveraged his family’s wealth to take him through the home stretch of a close campaign, and tensions are medium-high with his wife, Abby, (Kerry Washington), who’s hiding some problems of her own. Things get considerably worse when, on Halloween, he receives a phone call: Kidnappers have taken his preteen son Denny (Christopher Woodley). They’re demanding $7m that Mark doesn’t have to get him back safely.

Mark and Abby both panic, but still find time to disagree about how, precisely, to handle this nightmare scenario. They’re especially divided on whether or not to heed the advice of Mark’s right-hand man Sam (Steven Yeun), who advocates employing a mysterious unnamed fixer (Gillian Anderson) – which itself will also cost the Kimballs plenty of money they will not get back unless Mark wins the election (which in turn involves him attending a crucial campaign event inconveniently scheduled on Halloween night). All the ingredients – starry cast; ticking clock; complicated dilemmas centered on money, power, and ambition – are in place for a high-toned thriller, even if it is on Netflix with a few stray theatrical engagements.

And then, once Affleck gets his ducks in a row … very little. For a movie that attempts to position Mark as both a product and a potential savior of Los Angeles, shockingly much of Animals takes place on Mark and Abby’s property, in a series of dull rich-folks rooms. It’s the kind of environment usually fit for a stage production, and the actors certainly play to the cheap seats – especially Washington, who can overact a whisper if called upon to do so. But the screenplay by Affleck, Billy Ray, and Connor O McIntyre doesn’t lend much substance to any of the arguments. Some secrets are revealed, then turn out not to matter. Mark, Abby and Sam talk in irritated circles. The kidnapper keeps calling, using voice modulation that makes him sound like Jigsaw. Only Anderson, resplendent in an all-white suit, has settled on whether this thriller is serious business or pulpy fun. (She chooses the latter, probably wisely though not particularly in sync with anyone else on screen.)

One particular mid-movie plot turn so closely aligns Animals with another recent kidnapping thriller, Spike Lee’s Kurosawa remake Highest 2 Lowest, that those who have seen Lee’s movie may drop their jaws in disbelief. Of course, this movie was likely written and maybe even shot before Affleck would have seen that film, but someone involved in this production must have seen Kurosawa’s original High and Low, nicking just enough for the story to count as homage rather than an outright redo. There’s nothing wrong with that on its face, but Animals has shockingly few ideas about how to transpose the material into a different city, time period or milieu. It may not be an entirely fair comparison, but it’s hard to think of Lee’s bravura ransom-drop sequence set on a long subway ride traversing Manhattan without suppressing laughter at the obligatory Los Angeles river-basin standoff that serves as the newer film’s all-wet climax. If Affleck has any greater insight into Los Angeles politics than the most basic and bludgeoning messaging of the film’s final moments, he’s kept it to himself, or cut it out of a movie that runs about 100 minutes without credits.

Affleck the actor is still fun to watch, especially playing a politician visibly straining to not look like one – a charismatic guy nonetheless laboring to resemble a narrative version who may be a little nicer and more down-to-earth than he really is. But despite employing ace cinematographer Robert Elswit, who shot Affleck’s The Town as well as There Will Be Blood, among many others, the look of Animals is pretty Netflix-y – slick, lacking in texture and ultimately a bit dull. In other words, Affleck has – presumably by accident – imitated his character. This is a movie that just wants to get the job done with frictionless likability, with seemingly no one inquiring about why.
