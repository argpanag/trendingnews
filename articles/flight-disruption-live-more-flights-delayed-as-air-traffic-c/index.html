Flight disruption live: More flights delayed as air traffic control boss apologies for disruption

TheTools · 09.09.2026 09:00 · source · permalink

Summary

  • More than 150 flights have been cancelled today and others delayed because of knock-on effects from yesterday's technical problem at air traffic control

  • Some 1,750 flights were cancelled on Tuesday, according to aviation analytics firm Cirium

  • Nats, the air traffic control provider, blamed a technical glitch - its boss Martin Rolfe has apologised and says there will be a "very, very thorough" investigation

  • Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander has summoned Rolfe to a meeting later to answer questions

  • She is also expected to tell MPs that a cyber attack was "categorically" not the cause of the fault

    09:57 BST

    Rozina Sini
    BBC Your Voice

    I got back in touch with Phil White, who has spent a night in a hotel after his flight from London Gatwick to Lanzarote on Tuesday afternoon was cancelled.

    "After nearly four hours sitting on the plane they cancelled the flight," Phil says, adding that baggage reclaim was "carnage".

    "All the bags from every cancelled flight were sent to one reclaim carousel. So there were a thousand or so people trying to get close enough to get their cases," Phil says.

    "The baggage hall was the worst and they're very lucky that there wasn't any sort of incident as tempers frayed and the concourse was getting hotter," he tells the BBC.

    He says he's booked on a flight today at 14:15. "Fingers crossed."

  5. Air traffic control boss apologises for disruptionpublished at 09:11 BST
    09:11 BST

    The boss of Nats, the UK's air traffic control provider, has apologised to passengers after a technical error led to hundreds of cancelled flights and widespread disruption on Tuesday.

    Speaking to Radio 4's Today programme, Martin Rolfe says "I absolutely understand the frustration... we absolutely take accountability and responsibility for that".

    On what went wrong, Rolfe says Nats has ruled out a cyber attack and says it will be doing a "very, very thorough" investigation.

    "Inevitably things to go wrong in complex systems, we never want them to, we hope they don't, but we absolutely plan for them," Rolfe says.

    Asked if things go "as wrong" in other countries, Rolfe says "absolutely", adding the UK has "one of the best reputation in the world for its air traffic control service".

    He says Nats has invested more than £1bn in technology over the past 25 years and its services have "consistently improved from a very good base".

    "But all of these things do not detract from the fact that when these things happen it is disappointing and upsetting, for which I apologise."

  8. Nats is fit for purpose, says air traffic control bosspublished at 22:18 BST 8 September
    22:18 BST 8 September

    Katy Austin
    Transport correspondent

    Martin Rolfe, the chief executive of Nats, tells the BBC that his organisation's systems are fit for purpose, and has apologised for Tuesday's issue.

    Speaking to the BBC, he says that while the system was now operating normally, “of course, there's a huge backlog” of flights and passengers.

    When pushed on how long systems weren’t working properly, Rolfe wasn’t sure but agrees it was “probably” in the ballpark of six hours.

    Rolfe says the focus is "about recovery" and they are "having a full investigation" into what went wrong.

    Asked if an incident like this showed Nats’ systems aren't fit for purpose, he responds: “I don't think so, no."

    He says: “Whilst we never want these things to happen - and I absolutely apologise for the fact it has happened today - I do think we're fit for purpose."

    Asked if he is considering his position, Rolfe says: "I’m obviously focusing on making sure that we get this solved.

    "My focus today has been making sure the team's in the right place, we have the right resources, the technical issues are being investigated, and the recovery happens.

    "There'll be lots of time for that later, but that's not now,” he adds.
