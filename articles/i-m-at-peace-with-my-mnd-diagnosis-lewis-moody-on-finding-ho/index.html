‘I’m at peace with my MND diagnosis’: Lewis Moody on finding hope amid the darkness

TheTools · 24.09.2026 07:51 · source · permalink

How often do you notice it?” Lewis Moody asks his wife Annie in the final scene of a poignant new documentary about his diagnosis with the incurable, degenerative motor neurone disease (MND). “Honestly?” asks Annie. When her husband responds in the affirmative, she pauses for a moment before quietly saying: “Well, I see change all the time.”

The answer appears to catch Moody slightly off guard, perhaps because in the 12 months since his MND diagnosis in September 2025 there hasn’t been any obvious sign of mental or physical deterioration – at least to the untrained eye. He is still the same hulking, physically intimidating – but always smiling – presence that won 71 rugby union caps for England, captained his country, helped them to World Cup glory in 2003 and played Test matches for the British & Irish Lions. He even completed a 500-mile charity cycling challenge earlier this summer, raising more than £500,000 in the fight against MND.

But, of course, his wife of 20 years, who sees him every day and lies next to him every night, has noticed a difference. “Just your shape,” explains Annie gently. “Or your fasciculations [involuntary muscle twitches]. I’m just aware of it, I suppose. Then that sort of draws my attention to what the future holds.”

And that’s the heartbreaking reality running through the background of Lewis Moody vs MND – an hour-long documentary co-produced by 3 Rock Productions and TNT Sports that premieres on TNT Sports 1 and HBO Max on Friday 25 September at 10pm. It’s often unspoken but the knowledge of how, in the coming months and years, MND will steadily attack Moody’s body until there’s nothing left – and the challenges that will bring – hangs over everything.

Thanks partly to the incredible work of trailblazers such as fellow rugby players Doddie Weir and Rob Burrow, who have both since passed away from the disease, awareness of MND, and the horrific impact it has, is greater than ever.

The documentary reminds you it is a disease that damages the nerves in the brain and spinal cord, impacting the muscles that allow you to move, speak, swallow and breathe. A third of patients die within a year of diagnosis and 50 per cent within two years. Only 20 per cent live for five to 10 years and just 10 per cent live longer than that. There is no cure and no treatment to slow the symptoms.

By passing the year mark since diagnosis with minimal deterioration, Moody is already beating the odds. In the documentary, he visits former Leicester Tigers teammate Ed Slater, who was diagnosed with MND in July 2022 and is much further along in his journey. The 38-year-old is now in a wheelchair and can only communicate via a text-to-speech voice machine. He offers words of encouragement to Moody, who admits he is “regretful” at not visiting sooner. He, understandably, wasn’t ready until now to witness this glimpse into his future.

But Lewis Moody vs MND is far from a pity party. It is emotional in places, without being emotionally manipulative. The most effective, and affecting, scenes are Moody’s chats with Annie and those with his two sons – Dylan, 18, and 16-year-old Ethan.

In an interview with media ahead of the premiere of the documentary, Moody recounts the heart-wrenching conversation he had with his children, revealing his initial diagnosis.

“Dyls was quite measured but Ethan was just an emotional mess,” he remembers. “He was just wailing, like when you see people at The Wailing Wall. If you see outpourings of grief in the Middle East, it is much more open and out there – literally wailing. That’s what he was like. It was horrible.

“So when it came to the conversation in the documentary, actually it was much easier. Almost, it was a relief. They knew it was coming and I wasn’t sure what I would get out of them but they were just awesome. It actually afforded us some genuine honest and open conversation. I wouldn’t have had that experience without the documentary.”

Those scenes are the heartbeat of Lewis Moody vs MND. In a landscape where young men are consistently maligned and accused of being radicalised by the ‘manosphere’ and Andrew Tate-influenced misogynistic online content, Dylan and Ethan Moody provide a refreshing, admirable antidote.

Both physically take after their dad, with not just the shock of blond hair but an athlete’s build, and are on the road to becoming professional sportsmen themselves. Dylan is a goalkeeper at Championship football team Southampton, and has represented England at age-group level, while Ethan is a burgeoning rugby player.

But both are remarkably candid about their struggles as they come to terms with their father’s diagnosis. Ethan admits he is yet to find a way to cope with it, at one point saying: “You can put it to the side and ignore it but you can’t forget about it,” before heartbreakingly adding, “I mean, I’ve cried the last two nights, so...”.

Meanwhile, Dylan is battling the reality of being away, at Southampton, rather than by his family’s side. “If I’m here for a week, four weeks, two weeks at a time, I don’t know what I’ll return home to,” he explains. “Some days I don’t want to be here, I’d rather be at home... if your life may be shorter.” In a touching moment, he implores his dad “you speak now”, as he fights back tears.

But there is a streak of hope coursing throughout the entire documentary, a lot of which is driven by Moody’s naturally positive and determined demeanour. Firstly, he seeks an answer to whether the fact he was an elite rugby player, who spent his professional life taking huge hits from opponents, is to blame for his condition.

“I literally asked that direct question to Professor Martin Turner, who is my specialist,” reveals Moody. “It genuinely wouldn’t have mattered to me because I think I knew from the start that if you repeatedly hit your head – like in football, rugby or boxing – you can’t think everything’s going to be fine.

“But he said rugby isn’t the reason you have MND. The reason you have Motor Neurone Disease is because there are a number of things that come together and coincidentally or not, you are more predisposed to it than any anyone else. I suppose that was a relief in many ways.”

We see a version of that conversation with Prof Turner in the documentary, where the doctor acknowledges that “one of the factors that makes motor neurone disease more likely to grow is people who have the kind of nervous system that is built for sport” but explains “that doesn’t necessarily mean it caused their condition.”

“I don’t think that you should be thinking ‘if I hadn’t been a rugby player, this would never have happened’,” he assures Moody. “I just don’t think the evidence is there for that.”

As a player, Moody was renowned for his commitment. He would, to coin a phrase, put his head where other players wouldn’t even put their boot. In a sport where being physically brave is an entry requirement, Moody reached new levels – he was tough as teak, a rugby player’s rugby player. And you’d be searching for an awfully long time to find anyone with a bad word to say about Lewis Moody the man.

Sadly, MND isn’t discerning when selecting its victims. “I actually had a tip off the day before Lewis’s public announcement that one of the ‘03 [World Cup-winning] squad had MND,” Moody’s World Cup-winning teammate Ben Kay tells The Independent. “I was wracking my brain just trying to think who it might be. I went through everyone and it just didn’t occur to me that it could be Lewis.”

Moody is now turning those battling qualities to the biggest fight of his life. “I’m at peace with my diagnosis,” he explains. “I was at peace almost immediately. That might sound strange but the uncertainty is the most harrowing part of it. Not knowing what it is, therefore there’s nothing you can do to change your circumstance.

“Of course the irony is that now I’ve found out, there is still little I can do to change my circumstance other than use my energy to help those doing the fighting against this disease. So, I am using my energy to gain more resources, gain more money, so that the specialists, doctors and researchers have the weapons against this disease to find things that mean we’re able to have the outcomes that we all want.”

And the conversation around MND is changing. When Weir revealed his diagnosis in June 2017 and set up the “My Name'5 Doddie” foundation to fund MND research later that year, it was mainly in the service of raising awareness, with no realistic prospect of a major breakthrough in his lifetime.

Burrow, and his great mate Kevin Sinfield, then picked up the mantle as the most visible face in the UK fighting the disease and drove remarkable levels of fundraising that are slowly starting to show results.

“It isn’t far off,” says an optimistic Moody. “If you speak to different specialists, they’ll talk about hope and how, for the first time in a long time, they feel hopeful about a... cure is not the right word but symptom-repressing drugs. There is a genuine conversation.

“When Doddie was diagnosed, there wasn’t even a clinical trial he could go on. Not a single trial. Now there are plenty. There’s a lot still to be done, but you never know what’s around the corner, right?”

Moody is under no illusions that any meaningful clinical breakthrough is unlikely to be able to help him before his body fully succumbs to the effect of MND but that has simply focussed his mind. As for what he hopes to achieve by doing this documentary?

“For me it’s really simple, it’s a call to action,” he says. “It’s about having as much impact, with the platform that I have and the time I have left, to move this needle. Take the baton on from Doddie and Rob and all those guys that have passed on and significantly move it, if not over the line, then near the line.

“I suppose what I really want is that anyone watching this, that they feel inspired and motivated to join me in the fight. We need more people doing more. If we can achieve that, then this has been a success.”

Lewis Moody vs MND was co-produced between 3 Rock Productions and TNT Sports. It premieres on TNT Sports 1 and HBO Max on Friday 25th September at 10pm
