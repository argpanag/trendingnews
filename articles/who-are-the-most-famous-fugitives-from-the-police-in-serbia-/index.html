Who are the most famous fugitives from the police in Serbia and BiH?

TheTools · 18.10.2026 14:23 · source · permalink

Chronicle

Serbia and Bosnia and Herzegovina have refused to extradite certain persons sought by neighboring jurisdictions, citing, among other things, citizenship and legal restrictions.

The case of Svetozar Marović, the former president of the state union of Serbia and Montenegro, reopened the question of why those convicted and accused of serious crimes avoid trial for years or serving a sentence in the countries of the region. Although the countries of the Western Balkans have extradition agreements, their implementation often encounters obstacles, especially when it comes to dual citizenship and war crimes, writes Radio Free Europe.

On October 10, 2026, the sentence to which he was sentenced in Montenegro for corruption expired. He went to Serbia after the sentencing in 2016, and Belgrade refused to extradite him despite the agreement with Podgorica, which includes certain crimes of organized crime and corruption.

Law professor Vesna Rakić Vodinelić stated that political connections have a great influence on decisions on rejecting extradition requests from neighboring countries. According to her assessment, Marović should have been extradited.

 

Why is extradition often not possible?

Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro and Kosovo have similar laws on international legal assistance in criminal matters. Procedure extradition it can include a request from another state, the arrest of a wanted person, extradition detention and a court decision, with the role of the Ministry of Justice.

Bilateral agreements that the countries of the region mostly started signing around 2010 enable the extradition of their own citizens under certain conditions, especially in cases of organized crime and corruption. However, those agreements do not cover acts committed before their signing, and the agreements between Serbia and BiH do not refer to war crimes.

In the European Union, the procedure is accelerated by the European Arrest Warrant, which applies between member states.

War crimes and the accused from Kosovo in Serbia

One of the most famous examples is Novak Đukić, former general of the Republika Srpska Army, who fled to Serbia in 2014, on the eve of the sentencing in Bosnia and Herzegovina. The court in Sarajevo sentenced him to 20 years in prison for the war crime and murder of 71 civilians at the Tuzla Gate. Serbia refuses to extradite him because he is its citizen.

Tomislav Kovač, Mirko Vrućinić and Duško Kornjača, who are wanted by the BiH judiciary for war crimes, also live in Serbia.

Milan Radoičić, who took responsibility for the armed attack on the Kosovo police in Banjska, is also at large. Kosovo issued several warrants for him, while the USA and Great Britain sanctioned him due to his ties to international crime. On October 9, Radoičić appeared at the pre-election rally of the ruling Serbian Progressive Party.
Serbia does not recognize Kosovo and does not have an extradition agreement with Pristina. The European Union mission in Kosovo (EULEX) is in charge of cooperation between the two sides, while some persons wanted by Kosovo are tried in absentia.

Asylum and citizenship for foreigners sought by other jurisdictions

Serbia also provided refuge to individual citizens of other countries who were under investigation or indictment in their own countries.

Bulgarian businessman Cvetan Vasilev, accused in his country in connection with 144 cases of money embezzlement, fled to Serbia in 2014, and was granted political asylum seven years later.

Romanian businessman and former MP Sebastian Gica, against whom several investigations were conducted, including those due to suspicion of corruption, was also granted asylum in Serbia. In 2019, the Serbian government granted citizenship to the former Prime Minister of Thailand, Yingluck Shinawatra, who was sentenced in her country to five years in prison for corruption.
Rakić Vodinelić believes that in the cases of rich people who avoid extradition, it is possible that corruption plays a significant role.

Among the more recent cases are Chinese national Cui Guanghai, who was wanted by the US on charges of espionage and smuggling of military technology. He disappeared from house arrest in Belgrade in 2025. Former Ukrainian intelligence officer Andrey Naumov, whose extradition was requested by Ukraine, remained in Serbia, where he was sentenced to one year in prison in 2025 for money laundering, in a separate proceeding.

Zdravko and Zoran Mamić found refuge in BiH

A similar pattern exists in Bosnia and Herzegovina, where individuals sought by the Croatian judiciary have avoided extradition by using dual citizenship.

Zdravko Mamić, a long-time official of Dinamo Zagreb, was sentenced in 2018 in Croatia to six and a half years in prison for embezzling money from the club. Before the sentencing, he fled to Bosnia and Herzegovina, whose citizenship he obtained on the basis of origin. The BiH authorities rejected Croatia's request for his extradition.

His brother Zoran Mamić, a former football player and coach, was sentenced to four years and eight months in prison in Croatia for extracting money from Dinamo. He also avoided serving his sentence in Croatia, and today he works at the Sarajevo Football Club as an advisor to the owner.
Other people wanted by the Croatian judiciary found refuge in Bosnia and Herzegovina, among them Miroslav Kutle and Jozo Ćurković, convicted or accused in cases of economic crime.

Cases from Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina and other countries in the region show that the existence of an extradition agreement does not in itself guarantee that a person will be handed over to the requesting state. Dual citizenship, time limits on agreements, exemptions for certain types of crimes and political circumstances can lead to judgments remaining unenforced and proceedings lasting years or conducted in absentia.
