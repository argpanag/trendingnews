Rolling classroom tackles tough topics at Vacaville High School

TheTools · 21.10.2026 23:39 · source · permalink

Mobile Museum of Tolerance gives Vacaville students space to discuss discrimination, hate and belonging

Vacaville High School students in Tricia Cowen’s first-period ethnic studies class got a welcome break from the classroom Wednesday to visit a museum. An added bonus: They didn’t have to travel far or spend the early morning on a bus. Instead, the museum came to them — housed in a 32-seat classroom on wheels parked right outside of their classroom.

The Simon Wiesenthal Center’s Mobile Museum of Tolerance, based at the Museum of Tolerance in Los Angeles, arrived at Vacaville High on Monday for a week of interactive workshops on civil rights, the Holocaust and digital media literacy, all aboard the traveling educational center. The museum’s first visit to Vacaville High will continue through Friday. The state-funded visit was coordinated by Danny Over, the school’s public safety teacher.

“I hope it brings a greater understanding of the effects of discrimination, segregation and hate that are still out there in the world, especially through social media,” Over said.

The first Mobile Museum of Tolerance began in 2020 in Illinois, as a first-of-its-kind mobile museum and education center teaching human rights in the U.S. Drawing some of its curriculum from the permanent L.A. museum which focuses on Holocaust education, the presentations are interactive and technology-driven, to engage with students in an immersive way while discussing tough topics. The museum’s mission is to turn dialogue into meaningful action, to help students combat hate, embrace tolerance, and fight for social change.

When students step onto the bus, Ewa Geisler, an educator with the L.A.-based museum who led all of Wednesday’s demonstrations, tends to hear them compare the interior to an airplane cabin.

The bus itself was cleverly designed for the experience: seats that spanned horizontally across folded up for extra space, and the walls were dark to draw focus onto the three large TV screens that accompanied the lesson. On those screens, images and video clips of marches, protests and diner sit-ins faded in and out in a dynamic display.

Geisler used the projections to introduce Wednesday’s first period students to the Civil Rights Movement and its leaders, who fought to ensure equal rights for Black Americans in the 1960s.

As a former sixth-grade teacher, Geisler says her job encompasses many of the “fun parts” of being a classroom teacher — engaging with students in an impactful way — without the burden of grading or parent-teacher conferences.

“I think what also is really fun about this is that I only have 45 minutes to interact with these students,” Geisler said. “And so we can get right down to it, get to know each other in the few minutes that we have together.”

While the civil rights presentation is Geisler’s personal favorite, the mobile museum also delivered two others to Vacaville students. In a workshop on the Holocaust, students learned about the everyday individuals who resisted during Nazi Germany’s persecution of Jewish people in the 1930s. Other students learned how to combat hate in digital spaces.

Geisler recognized that a big part of teaching students to combat hate is giving them a chance to express themselves.

“A lot of students, maybe they don’t even get to talk to anyone during the day,” Geisler said. “But here, they get a chance to talk to the people next to them, and maybe they don’t know them, or they have an opportunity to hear their voice in a classroom setting like this.”

In a partnered activity with Cowen’s class, Geisler brought out influential civil rights-era poems for students to study and highlight phrases from. Some standout messages were: “I am somebody” and “My face is different, but I must be respected.”

When Vacaville High School senior Silva Foster received his poem, the repeated words “Who am I?” stood out to him, because it pointed to a question of identity and belonging surfacing from facing racial discrimination, like many of the other poems.

“They’re questioning who they are,” Foster said. “Among the other people.”

Over hopes that throughout the week students will gain greater understanding of the harms of discrimination, hate and segregation that still exist, and emphasized the unique role social media has in its spread.

“I also hope it will empower students to realize they, too, have the power to change things as ordinary people,” Over said. “Being kind to others can have a lasting and sometimes life-changing effect.”

Vacaville High School Principal Adam Wight shared a similar hope, while emphasizing the program was a compelling experience for students.

“What an exciting opportunity for our students to learn and grow as they experience learning in a different way,” said Wight “I am hopeful that the lessons of empathy, tolerance and mutual respect that the museum programs emphasize leave a lasting impression on our kids.”
