As Democrats took a victory lap, President Trump distanced himself from a dismal GOP showing as the White House was left to reckon with a historic rout.

Democrats continue to celebrate one of the most lopsided midterm election victories in modern U.S. history, routing their Republican rivals to gain control of the U.S. House, U.S. Senate and statehouses across the country.

In the Senate, Democrats flipped seven seats and now hold 54 to the Republican Party's 44. Two seats — nail-biting contests in Alaska and Mississippi — remain undeclared.

To do so, Democrats won a series of key battleground contests. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed defeated Mike Rogers in one of the most closely watched races of the midterms. James Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton in Texas, becoming the first Democrat to win a U.S. Senate seat in the Lone Star State since 1988.  Annie Andrews beat Sen. Darline Graham in South Carolina to flip a Senate seat. In Maine, Democrat Troy Jackson defeated incumbent Sen. Susan Collins, putting an end to her three-decade tenure. Sen. Jon Ossoff comfortably won reelection in Georgia. Other Senate seats to flip to the Democratic Party included Roy Cooper in North Carolina, Sherrod Brown in Ohio, Josh Turek in Iowa and Adam Hamilton in Kansas.

In the House, Democrats held 253 House seats to the GOP's 157 as of 2.30 a.m. ET Friday, comfortably passing the majority threshold of 218, with 25 races still to be called. The latest estimates show that the party is on track to win its largest majority since 2008. And there are still seats it could flip, including Colorado's Fourth District, which is held by Republican incumbent Rep. Lauren Boebert, a staunch Trump ally, whose race against Democrat Eileen Laubacher remains tight.

There were also 36 gubernatorial races, mayoral contests and numerous ballot measures — and plenty of firsts. Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms of Georgia became the first Black woman elected governor in U.S. history, while Democrat Amy Acton became the first woman elected governor of Ohio. In Florida, Republican Byron Donalds became the state's first Black governor-elect. El-Sayed will be America's first Muslim senator.

At the White House, President Trump signaled he would work with Democrats during his remaining two years in office but said he wouldn't hesitate to use his veto power. The president also acknowledged Wednesday that the Iran war contributed to Republican losses, but defended his decision to pursue the conflict. "If I had to do it again, I'd have to do it again," he said, adding: "You can't let Iran have a nuclear weapon."

Vice President JD Vance had earlier blamed the scale of GOP losses on the war with Iran.

"The real reason we lost is obvious to almost everyone," he wrote in a post on X. "The reason we lost is the war."

      Mike Bebernes

      We may not know the results in Alaska for 2 more weeks. Here's why.

      All eyes have been on Alaska for the past two days as votes are slowly tallied in the state's Senate race. As of Thursday evening, Democrat Mary Peltola was leading Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan by just over 600 votes, with 74% of votes tallied. 

      But having only a quarter of the vote left uncounted does not necessarily mean a decision is imminent. That's because Alaska uses ranked choice voting, a system that allows voters to list each of the four candidates on the ballot in order of preference.

      Under ranked choice voting, any candidate who gets more than 50% of the vote in the first count is declared the winner. If no one breaks that threshold, then the person with the fewest first-choice votes is eliminated. Voters who picked the eliminated candidate will have their votes transferred to their next-highest-ranked candidate who remains in the race. The process repeats until one candidate receives more than 50% of the votes remaining in the count.

      At the moment, it appears that neither Peltola nor Sullivan will break 50%, which means the race could come down to ranked choice votes. The rub is that Alaska law doesn't allow the state to begin tabulating ranked choice results until 15 days after Election Day. So if all the votes are counted and no one gets more than half, the race will remain in limbo until at least Nov. 18.

      The same goes for Alaska's gubernatorial race, where Democratic candidate Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins holds a substantial lead but may not be able to reach 50% either.

      Democrats just hit 253 House seats (and counting). How big could their majority get?

      Before Election Day, America's leading political prognosticators converged around a consensus forecast for the U.S. House: Democrats were going to win back control, but their new majority was likely to max out at around 235 to 240 seats. Even the most bullish among them put the odds of a 255-plus-seat majority at 10%.

      How big could their new majority get? Right now, the AP shows Democratic candidates leading by narrow margins in seven outstanding House races — and trailing by similar margins in another 16.

      Those numbers suggest Democrats could reach at least 260 seats by the time the dust settles. If they do, it will be their largest majority since the early 1990s.

      Why is the Mississippi race so close?

      The nail-biter Senate race in Mississippi remains too close to call in this historically red state.

      Democratic candidate Scott Colom has 49% of the vote, while Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith has 48.9% of the vote — just 1,138 votes separate the two out of more than 948,000 cast so far.

      County clerks have 10 days to certify the election.

      If Colom wins, he will become the first Democratic candidate to be elected to the Senate from Mississippi since 1982, and the first Black US senator elected in the state since 1875.

      The potential flip mirrors the blue wave that has swept the midterms, which many see as a reflection of the Trump administration and the president's low approval ratings, propelled by issues including the Iran war and rising prices.

      But in Mississippi, there are other factors at play. Aaron Hitefield, an assistant professor of political science at Mississippi State University, told Time magazine that Hyde-Smith's campaign lacked luster.

      Hyde-Smith won reelection in 2020 by 10 points compared with Mississippi's other Republican Senator, Roger Wicker, who scored a 25-point lead in his 2024 win.

      She did not "do the groundwork necessary to run a successful campaign," Hitefield said, adding: "Even if she does win, she didn't do, really, what she needed to win with any sort of safety. "

      Which races are yet to be called?

      While Democrats have taken control of both the House and Senate, there are a number of races yet to be called that will determine the size of the Democrats' majority.

      Senate races

      In the Senate, Democrats have 54 seats, while Republicans have 44, according to the latest figures from the Associated Press. Two races have yet to be called: Mississippi and Alaska.

      In Mississippi, Democratic candidate Scott Colom has 49% of the vote, while Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith has 48.9% of the vote — just 1,138 votes out of more than 948,000 that have already been counted separate the two. County clerks have 10 days to certify the election.

      In Alaska, Democratic candidate Mary Peltola has 48.2% of the vote, while Republican Dan Sullivan has 47.9%, according to AP. A number of votes come from rural areas, and the state, which runs a ranked-choice voting system, will accept ballots mailed from outside the U.S. (and postmarked by Election Day) until Nov. 18.

      House races

      In the House, Democrats currently hold 240 seats while Republicans have 147. There are 48 yet to be called — including New York's 17th and Colorado's 4th districts.

      Senate race remains on knife-edge in Mississippi

      The Senate race in Mississippi has turned into a nail-biter, with the contest still too close to call and 96% of the expected vote counted.

      According to the latest figures from the Associated Press, Democratic candidate Scott Colom has 49% of the vote, while Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith has 48.9% of the vote.

      The race is still uncalled because officials are waiting on two batches of ballots, with one of those containing affidavit ballots, largely filed by people who did not show up at the polls with voter ID or had been marked as inactive on voter rolls.

      The state has 10 days — in this case, until Nov. 13 — to certify the election.

      However, regardless of how close the count is, there is no automatic legal recourse for a vote count. Mississippi is the only state that doesn't have a law allowing for recounts of close statewide races, and candidates would need a court order to get a recount, which would likely happen after Nov. 13.

    Midterms tend to be backlash elections, and the sitting president's party almost always loses seats when voters return to the polls to judge his performance in office.

    This year, Democrats comprehensively routed their Republican rivals, regaining control of the U.S. House, U.S. Senate and statehouses across the country. Along the way, they shattered a series of decades-long Republican winning streaks and notched several historic firsts.

    In the House, Democrats could end up flipping 40+ seats once the count is fully completed. That could lead to a majority of about 255, the largest since 2008, and possibly since 1992.

    Democrats have already flipped seven seats in the Senate, and that could reach nine once Alaska and Mississippi are called. That hasn't happened for either party since 2014. The last time a party flipped more than nine Senate seats in a midterm election was 1958.

    The nail-biter Senate race in Mississippi remains too close to call in this historically red state.

    Democratic candidate Scott Colom has 49% of the vote, while Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith has 48.9% of the vote — just 1,138 votes separate the two out of more than 948,000 cast so far.

    County clerks have 10 days to certify the election.

    If Colom wins, he will become the first Democratic candidate to be elected to the Senate from Mississippi since 1982, and the first Black US senator elected in the state since 1875.

    The potential flip mirrors the blue wave that has swept the midterms, which many see as a reflection of the Trump administration and the president's low approval ratings, propelled by issues including the Iran war and rising prices.

    But in Mississippi, there are other factors at play. Aaron Hitefield, an assistant professor of political science at Mississippi State University, told Time magazine that Hyde-Smith's campaign lacked luster.

    Hyde-Smith won reelection in 2020 by 10 points compared with Mississippi's other Republican Senator, Roger Wicker, who scored a 25-point lead in his 2024 win.

    She did not "do the groundwork necessary to run a successful campaign," Hitefield said, adding: "Even if she does win, she didn't do, really, what she needed to win with any sort of safety. "

    While Democrats have taken control of both the House and Senate, there are a number of races yet to be called that will determine the size of the Democrats' majority.

    In Mississippi, Democratic candidate Scott Colom has 49% of the vote, while Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith has 48.9% of the vote — just 1,138 votes out of more than 948,000 that have already been counted separate the two. County clerks have 10 days to certify the election.

    In Alaska, Democratic candidate Mary Peltola has 48.2% of the vote, while Republican Dan Sullivan has 47.9%, according to AP. A number of votes come from rural areas, and the state, which runs a ranked-choice voting system, will accept ballots mailed from outside the U.S. (and postmarked by Election Day) until Nov. 18.

    In the House, Democrats currently hold 240 seats while Republicans have 147. There are 48 yet to be called — including New York's 17th and Colorado's 4th districts.

    The Senate race in Mississippi has turned into a nail-biter, with the contest still too close to call and 96% of the expected vote counted.

    According to the latest figures from the Associated Press, Democratic candidate Scott Colom has 49% of the vote, while Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith has 48.9% of the vote.

    The race is still uncalled because officials are waiting on two batches of ballots, with one of those containing affidavit ballots, largely filed by people who did not show up at the polls with voter ID or had been marked as inactive on voter rolls.

    The state has 10 days — in this case, until Nov. 13 — to certify the election.

    However, regardless of how close the count is, there is no automatic legal recourse for a vote count. Mississippi is the only state that doesn't have a law allowing for recounts of close statewide races, and candidates would need a court order to get a recount, which would likely happen after Nov. 13.
