New Melrose Triangle Renderings Show West Hollywood Makeover

TheTools · 13.11.2026 17:13 · source · permalink

There’s a new look for West Hollywood’s infamous hole in the ground — the Melrose Triangle. Whether we’ll ever see it built is another story.

The Charles Company released another set of renderings for its long-delayed project at 9060 Santa Monica Boulevard. The infamous property at the intersection of Santa Monica Boulevard, Melrose Avenue and Almont Drive.

The new images were prepared by Corbel Architects and shared with the City’s Planning Commission Design Review Subcommittee. It gives us a better idea of what the developer has in mind.

The latest plan has 282 apartments, including 66 affordable units for seniors, along with roughly 100,000 square feet of commercial space and 528 parking spaces. That’s a shift from its earleir plan for more office space. The buildings will range from five to eight stories and have stores, restaurants, and a central courtyard connecting the development to the surrounding streets. The proposed landscaping is gorgeous. And there’s a lot of it. The idea is to have up to 58,000 square feet of common open space. The City only requires 2,000. There will be lots of room to enjoy outdoor space, grab something to eat or just chill and enjoy the surroundings.

The 66 affordable senior apartments will include 33 for very-low-income residents and the other 33 for moderate-income residents.

The new renderings show a big landscaped staircase leading up from Santa Monica Boulevard, outdoor dining areas and pedestrian walkways cutting through the property. Courtland Studio has also replaced SWA Group as the landscape architect.

But as WEHOonline reported back in May, getting something built has been a very long and rather expensive exercise in frustration.

The original Melrose Triangle project was approved back in 2014, and a revised version got the Planning Commission’s blessing in 2022. But we’re still waiting. The developer excavated the property years ago, leaving us with that giant hole everyone started calling Lake WeHo. And after the previous approvals expired, the City ordered the developer to fill it back in.

And who could forget the mosquito problem that came with all that standing water?

At a community meeting in May, the project’s architect, David Kim, and land use attorney Kathleen Truman, walked us through the latest proposal. About three dozen people attended, and the reaction was generally favorable, although there were questions about parking, traffic and the project’s height.

One resident described it as “one of the better or best versions I’ve seen.”

But what caught our attention was what Truman had to say about the hole.

“The entitlements have expired, and the City has requested that we fill the hole,” she told residents. “That’s one of the reasons that we are processing the building plans at risk to try and get in and get something built in this triangle, and perhaps do it quickly, so we don’t have to take all of the dirt out again.”

Think about that. After years of digging, redesigning and waiting, the developer could wind up spending a considerable amount of money filling the hole, only to excavate it all over again.

When WEHOonline spoke with the development team in May, financing hadn’t been secured.

Oliver Gabay, the Charles Company’s vice president of development, said the company was exploring financing options but needed to finalize the project first.

Truman suggested construction would take up to two and half years and could begin in 2027, assuming the approvals and permits came through.

If you’re wondering about billboards, and we know you are. The digital artwork shown in some of the earlier renderings will not be billboards. Truman cleared that up back in May. No billboards are proposed for the property.

The new renderings aren’t a construction announcement, and there’s no indication from the images alone that financing has been secured. But for a property that’s spent so many years as an open pit, it’s encouraging to see the proposal moving through another stage of the design process.
