Postpartum psychosis explained by Sanford counselor as Clancy mistrial ruling stands

TheTools · 24.09.2026 23:22 · source · permalink

FARGO, N.D. (Valley News Live) - Prosecutors are still weighing whether to retry Lindsay Clancy, the mother accused of killing her three young children, after jurors were split over her diagnosis of postpartum psychosis.

Massachusetts Superior Court Associate Justice William Sullivan declared a mistrial after days of deliberations.

“Alright, members of the jury, at this point, I am going to declare that the jury is deadlocked, and I am going to declare a mistrial,” Sullivan said.

A majority of jurors believed Clancy was in the grip of postpartum psychosis, a rare and severe condition that occurs in about one to two out of every 1,000 people after childbirth.

What postpartum psychosis looks like

Angela Blackford, an Integrative Health Counselor at Sanford Health, described symptoms of the condition.

“Delusions like firm beliefs. Beliefs that you are receiving messages through music or TV. That you or your baby are Godlike. Sometimes symptoms can also be, sometimes there’s a little bit of mania. Like feeling like you don’t need to sleep,” Blackford said.

Blackford said postpartum psychosis is less common than postpartum depression. It is also different.

“There’s like the deep sadness, there can be loss of interest in things. Loss of motivation, numbness, tearfulness, that lasts longer than a couple of weeks or so after pregnancy,” Blackford said.

Local increase in patients

Blackford said as postpartum psychosis is mentioned more often, she has seen an increase in local patients coming in.

“I have noticed a little bit of an increase in people thinking about it and talking about it. I do notice it,” Blackford said.

Symptoms can happen within the first few days to the first few weeks after childbirth. Postpartum psychosis, depression and anxiety can also happen to men.

“Like hormone fluctuations is a little bit different for them. It’s still a life change. It’s still a routine change. It’s still an autonomy change,” Blackford said.

Blackford said postpartum psychosis does not last forever and is treatable with medication, therapy or support groups.

Clancy is in a psychiatric hospital until her case is resolved.
