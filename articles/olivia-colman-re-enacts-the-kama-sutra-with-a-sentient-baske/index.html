Olivia Colman re-enacts the Kama Sutra with a sentient basket

TheTools · 15.10.2026 09:00 · source · permalink

Wicker is a mortifying adult fairy tale about a lonely fisherwoman acquiring a magical wickerwork husband

1/5

Olivia Colman’s career is unusually broad for an Academy Award-winning actress: pop over to iPlayer and the same woman who won an Oscar for playing Queen Anne can be found in Mitchell and Webb sketches with titles such as The Boy with an A--- for a Face. But even those look like eight weeks in Mother Courage at the Almeida next to this mortifying adult fairy tale, in which a lonely fisherwoman acquires a magical wickerwork husband whose benign if unsettling presence causes uproar among the nearby yokels.

Adapted from a short story by Ursula Wills-Jones, Wicker stars Colman as the nameless fisherwoman and Alexander Skarsgård is her woven beau, fashioned by a travelling basketmaker (Peter Dinklage) who takes pity on her loveless plight. Why this creation comes to life is never explained – the hand-wavy “it’s a fairy tale” excuse is given a thorough workout – but he and the fisherwoman are side by side at the local chapel’s altar soon enough. They then galumph off home for the consummation, which proves epic in its athleticism and duration.

Between his roles in The Legend of Tarzan, The Northman, Succession and Pillion, Skarsgård must now be fancied by just about everyone. But unless you have a thing for natural fibres, Wicker’s eye-candy level is low: the husband remains swathed in his woody carapace for the duration, and as far as I could tell, the only human body parts visible are eyeballs.

There is an amusing moment when the wicker husband becomes physically aroused and we hear a cartoonish creak from his lower anatomy, like the sound when Scooby-Doo steps on a floorboard. But there is only so much comic mileage in having Colman re-enact the Kama Sutra with a sentient basket, so the story eventually recentres on the villagers, led by Elizabeth Debicki’s embittered town gossip, who take umbrage that the least eligible woman among them has ended up with this dreamboat golem.

The dialogue, which runs the gamut from irksome to infuriating, is written in what might be called Cockwomble English, with strenuously jaunty expressions crowbarred into every other line. (Breasts are “bubbies”, “what did you say” becomes “pardon my a---”, and so on.) Your ear trips over this awfulness so constantly that it becomes impossible to get past Wicker’s fundamentally silly premise and relax into the film.

Meanwhile, on the rare occasions that something does threaten to resonate emotionally – such as Debicki’s own deep-seated reasons for envying Colman’s marital happiness – Wicker loses its nerve and lapses back into whoops-a-daisy, watch-your-turnips-madam mode. So along comes the unhappily married baker to drunkenly fantasise about being cuckolded by Skarsgård, or Richard E Grant swanking into frame as the town’s hapless doctor and trying to sew up a gash in someone’s foot.

We are also offered a satirical critique of the dreaded patriarchy – village brides aren’t given wedding bands but have metal collars clamped around their necks, for instance – but all of this is trowelled on so thick it just feels facile. In a number of respects, not least its title, Wicker nods to a certain bawdy British folk horror classic, but it could have done with some of that earlier film’s nerve-wracking impishness and genial menace. Fly the reels off to Summerisle and have Christopher Lee set them alight.

Screening at the London Film Festival. In UK cinemas from Friday, Oct 30.
