New research could help clinicians track emerging resistance to mutant-selective PI3K inhibitors in breast cancer | Mass General Brigham

Mass General Brigham-led study reveals mutations involved in resistance and how combination therapy may counteract them

Mutations in the PIK3CA gene, which encodes the PI3Kα enzyme, are common drivers of certain cancers, especially hormone receptor–positive metastatic breast cancers. A team led by investigators from the Mass General Brigham Cancer Institute has uncovered additional PIK3CA mutations that allow cancers to become resistant to investigational PI3Kα inhibitors. The findings could help clinicians monitor for the emergence of resistance mutations and change the course of treatment accordingly. Results are published in Cancer Discovery.

“Understanding how tumors evolve under treatment-selective pressure is critical if we want to anticipate the emergence of resistance and extend treatment benefit and overall patient well-being,” said co–senior author Dejan Juric, MD, an oncologist and director of the Henri and Belinda Termeer Center for Targeted Therapies at Mass General Brigham Cancer Institute. “Based on our findings, we anticipate that we could monitor for the emergence of additional PIK3CA alterations during the course of treatment, anticipate whether the disease is likely to progress, and potentially switch to other treatments that remain effective even in the presence of these acquired alterations.”

Juric, co-lead Mass General Brigham authors Andreas Varkaris, MD, PhD, and Ferran Fece de la Cruz, PhD, co-senior author Ryan Corcoran, MD, PhD,and their colleagues analyzed pre- and post-treatment levels of tumor DNA circulating in the blood of 32 patients with PIK3CA-mutant, hormone receptor–positive metastatic breast cancer who received the investigational PI3Kα inhibitors zovegalisib or tersolisib. These drugs bind to PI3Kα at a different location than currently approved PI3Kα inhibitors, which are less selective and can cause significant side effects.

Many secondary PIK3CA mutations that arose in patients after treatment clustered around the specific pocket where the newer drugs bind. The team further examined these novel secondary mutations in cell models and validated which ones would lead to acquired resistance. Importantly, combining low doses of approved PI3Kα inhibitors with the investigational mutant-selective PI3Kα inhibitors suppressed resistant cancer cells in lab models and produced more sustained tumor suppression than either treatment alone in mice.

Several next-generation PI3Kα inhibitors are currently under phase 2 and 3 clinical investigation for the treatment of hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer and other advanced tumors.

“Despite promising clinical results with mutant-selective PI3Kα inhibitors, the development of resistance remains a major challenge,” said Varkaris. “Our research focuses on understanding, preventing, and overcoming resistance to mutant-selective PI3Kα inhibition, with the ultimate goal of maximizing and prolonging clinical benefit for patients with cancer.”
