Married women wait for the moon to rise on Karwa Chauth because the moon is traditionally worshipped as part of the fast, which is observed for the long life, health, and prosperity of their husbands and the well-being of their families.


According to Hindu tradition, women observe a nirjala vrat, abstaining from both food and water from sunrise until moonrise. In the evening, they offer arghya (water offering) to the Moon God, Chandra Dev, pray for their husbands’ well-being and view the moon through a sieve, often followed by looking at their husbands through it. Their husbands then offer them water and, in many families, the first bite of food to break the fast.


The moon is associated with calmness, peace and emotional well-being in Hindu beliefs. Its sighting marks the completion of the day’s fast and the performance of the prescribed rituals.

Karwa Chauth is not only a ceremony of fasting, but it also has significant cultural and historical importance. It is said that the festival imitates the worship of Goddess Parvati, who fasted in order to win the love of Lord Shiva as a husband. Married women also carry out this fast with the greatest degree of devotion so that they can have a long life and a healthy existence for their husbands. It is also believed that the day brings good fortune, prosperity, and harmony to the family.


Besides praying to their husbands, women also worship Lord Shiva, Goddess Parvati, Lord Ganesha, and Karwa Mata to bless them in the life of a happy and successful marriage. The fast is also a sign of faith and tradition, keeping the family ties together, not just love and commitment.
