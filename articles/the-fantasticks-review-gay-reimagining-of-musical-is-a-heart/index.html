The Fantasticks review – gay reimagining of musical is a heartwarming delight

TheTools · 17.11.2026 03:00 · source · permalink

Hayes theater, New York City
The world’s longest running musical is brought to Broadway for the first time in a joyful new version

If you, like no doubt millions of tenderhearts the world over, are mourning the end of the Netflix series Heartstopper – a sweet and whimsical chronicling of two teenage boys in love – then all you need to do is book a trip to New York City and secure a ticket to the new production of The Fantasticks, the beloved musical now receiving its first-ever Broadway run. The show, from writers Tom Jones (not the one you’re thinking of) and Harvey Schmidt, ran for more than 40 years off-Broadway, the whole time telling the story of young lovers, Matt and Luisa, secretly pushed toward one another by their meddling parents. On Broadway, though, it has become the courtship of Matt (Maxim Chlumecky) and Lewis (Nathan Levy), two lads springy of hair and bouncy of step as they bound toward the rest of their lives together.

The parents, fathers in the original production, have been switched to mothers, flower gardener Mildred (Leslie Rodriguez Kritzer) and avid vegetable grower Bessie (Ruthie Ann Miles), perhaps to add a few more female voices to this enduringly winsome (though some might say cloying) chamber piece. Those modern tweaks might be necessary, as so much of The Fantasticks is firmly rooted in the tones, tempos and tastes of another era. The musical first premiered in 1960, and its quaint attitudes about love and marriage, about childhood and what comes after it have otherwise not been meddled with in director-choreographer Christopher Gattelli’s lively and wistful new production.

Characters still occasionally speak in dreamy and poetic rhyming couplets (some borrowed from the 1894 play Les Romanesques, on which the musical is based). The songs – bookending nostalgia anthem Try to Remember, sprightly parenting metaphor Plant a Radish, swooning lovers’ duet They Were You – are as cute and shimmering and simple as ever. Some of that may be too antiquated for younger viewers, but perhaps the gay, gender norm-bending riff will be enough to sustain their interest, or even earn their hard-won adolescent respect. Older audience members, on the other hand, should have no trouble being transported back to their own youths, perhaps even to their own memories of this cherished show. It’s a Fantasticks for the longtime fans, and a warm invitation to the uninitiated to forgive its slight corniness and succumb to its gentle charms.

Boys and girls of all ages have also been given something to appreciate in the form of Zane Phillips, the hulking Hercules who plays narrator/trickster magician El Gallo. With arms the size of Chelsea Piers pilings, Phillips adds a safe kind of sensuality to the proceedings; he’s flirty and dashing, but there’s a curl of a joke to his almost ludicrous physical presence, to the robust, barrel-chested timbre of his hearty tenor. We see a flash of mid-century cheesiness in there, as if a hunk of yesteryear has been transported to our time to connect this contemporary Fantasticks to the past. (Whether you think the original El Gallo, Jerry Orbach, was a hunk is up to you.) What’s new about Phillips’s El Gallo is the more fluid way he can interact with Matt and Lewis; the air of seduction in the characters’ moments together takes on a different sort of charge when everyone’s seemingly on the same page, orientation-wise.

Which isn’t to suggest this is a horny Fantasticks. That would be something like sacrilege. For the most part, Gattelli dutifully honors the wry but wholesome spirit of the world’s longest-running musical. Chlumecky and Levy are rosy and apple-cheeked as needed (and are both in terrific voice). Miles and Rodriguez Kritzer ham it up with verve, while also locating their worried and doting characters in something poignant and true. And as the show’s resident jesters, the old vaudevillians known as Henry and Mortimer, Kevin Del Aguila and a vibrant David Patrick Kelly make ancient comedy routines seem spry.

Jason Sherwood’s scenic design pays homage to the bare-bones, black box aesthetic of the original production, while gracefully widening the visual scope to better fit a Broadway house. He provides a scuffed-up white brick wall on to which Jen Schriever’s supple lighting is splashed in various jewel-tone hues, successfully conjuring up the pregnant possibility of moonlit dusk, or the pale and forlorn glare of late-autumn sun.

It’s all a quite fitting environment for this spare story of love realized, tested and reconfirmed, and for the show’s broader consideration of what it is to be young and hungry but naively ill-prepared for the world and its adventures. This Fantasticks strikes those ruminative chords quite well, particularly in its achingly gorgeous final tableau. Though, one must remember that the show, in its flowery language and storybook fancifulness, has never been to everyone’s liking. The line between endearing and twee is a fine one, and plenty of contemporary audience members might find that Jones and Schmidt cross it too often. Lifelong fans like myself, though, ought to be carried away, borne back into fond memories suddenly complicated by a new idea: it needn’t always be a boy and a girl who meet, and fall in love, and try to figure out just how happy ever after could ever be.

  • The Fantasticks is at the Hayes theater, New York City, until 7 February 2027
