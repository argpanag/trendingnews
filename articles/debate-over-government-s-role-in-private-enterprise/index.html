Debate over government’s role in private enterprise

TheTools · 12.10.2026 06:00 · source · permalink

This article first appeared in The Edge Malaysia Weekly on October 5, 2026 - October 11, 2026

THE government’s move to support a fundraising drive for low-cost carrier AirAsia Group Bhd (KL:AAGB) is raising questions over how far it should go to help a heavily indebted private company and whether taxpayers could ultimately be left footing the bill.

People familiar with the matter say the Ministry of Finance (MOF) has provided a letter of support to Alton Aviation Consultancy, which it appointed to help secure international debt financing for AirAsia, as lenders become more selective about funding airlines amid challenging market conditions and soaring jet fuel prices. According to the sources, Alton will receive a success fee based on a percentage of the amount raised.

AirAsia is reportedly seeking up to US$1 billion (RM4.08 billion), primarily for debt restructuring, refinancing and balance sheet consolidation, with the remainder earmarked for operational needs.

The exact nature of the government’s backing remains unclear. In particular, questions remain over whether the letter of support is legally binding and whether it could expose the government to repayment obligations if AirAsia defaults.

If it is not legally binding, there is also a question over how much weight lenders and investors would place on the letter.

The debate goes beyond AirAsia’s finances. It touches on a broader policy question: how far should the government go to protect a strategically important company when the business remains privately owned?

Proponents of government involvement point to AirAsia’s role in regional connectivity and tourism.

Shukor Yusof, founder and analyst at aviation consultancy Endau Analytics, says AirAsia’s extensive network across Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, the Philippines and Cambodia gives the airline economic importance.

“AirAsia was the first mover in Southeast Asia’s low-cost carrier segment. Compared with regional peers like Lion Air and Cebu Pacific Air, AirAsia is far ahead in terms of connectivity,” he tells The Edge.

He argues that AirAsia’s contribution to tourism, connectivity and the broader economy means the government has an interest in ensuring the airline remains operational.

AirAsia’s financial position has deteriorated sharply due to higher fuel costs and foreign exchange losses. The airline reported two consecutive quarters of losses. Net loss widened to RM527.16 million in the second quarter ended June 30, 2026 from RM154.88 million in the previous quarter. Revenue fell 14.5% quarter on quarter to RM5.09 billion.

Average fuel prices rose 66% during the quarter, while AirAsia recorded a net foreign exchange loss of RM331 million as major currencies weakened against the US dollar. The airline was particularly exposed to higher fuel prices after entering the year without fuel hedges.

As at June 30, AirAsia had net debt of RM2.27 billion. Cash stood at RM953.67 million and borrowings at RM3.13 billion, including RM803.2 million due within 12 months.

Lease liabilities were higher at RM13.3 billion, including deferred aircraft lease obligations.

An airline analyst tells The Edge that the pressure on AirAsia predates the latest spike in fuel prices and disruption from the conflict in the Middle East, noting that the airline’s current liabilities exceeded current assets by RM14.54 billion at the end of June. Trade and other payables also rose sharply, reaching RM10.26 billion from RM4.28 billion at the end of December 2025.

That RM6 billion increase in payables in six months raises questions about the airline’s ability to meet obligations to suppliers and other creditors, the analyst points out.

Critics of government support say AirAsia’s economic importance alone does not justify transferring financial risk to the public sector.

AirAsia is privately owned, raising questions over why the government should support its debt when other companies facing financial distress do not necessarily receive comparable assistance.

The airline’s operations outside Malaysia add another dimension. AirAsia has businesses in Thailand, Indonesia, the Philippines and Cambodia, prompting questions over whether Malaysian taxpayers should ultimately bear risks associated with its operations beyond the country.

That is the essence of the moral hazard debate: shareholders and private owners retain the benefits when a company performs well, but taxpayers could be asked to absorb losses when it runs into trouble.

One industry expert says public resources should focus on protecting employees and critical connectivity rather than shareholder value.

The analyst concurs, pointing to AirAsia’s dividend history as part of that debate. Major shareholders received significant dividends between financial years 2017 and 2019, including special dividends of 40 sen a share in FY2018 and 90 sen in FY2019 — the airline’s highest-ever dividend. However, no shareholder has pledged their equity stake as collateral or used their shares to secure a loan or other financial obligation.

The analyst cites Capital A Bhd’s (KL:CAPITALA) decision in 2022 not to proceed with a RM500 million Danajamin Nasional Bhd-guaranteed club facility. Capital A had objected to a condition requiring co-founders Tan Sri Tony Fernandes and Datuk Kamarudin Meranun to provide personal guarantees. 

Another issue for policymakers is whether AirAsia remains indispensable to Malaysia’s aviation market. The airline has reduced capacity on some domestic and international routes in recent months. That raises the question of whether those cuts have materially affected tourism, passenger demand or the wider economy.

Department of Statistics Malaysia data shows that Malaysia recorded 76.9 million domestic tourism visitors in the second quarter of 2026, up 3% from the previous quarter.

“If AirAsia were to substantially reduce operations or leave the market, the government would need to determine how much capacity could be replaced by other carriers,” the analyst says. Batik Air Malaysia has said it could rapidly deploy additional aircraft to meet domestic demand if necessary.

“That does not mean AirAsia’s network could be replaced without disruption, but it challenges the assumption that the carrier’s entire market share would simply disappear,” the analyst adds.

Even if policymakers conclude that AirAsia is strategically important, the next question is what the government would receive in exchange for assuming additional risk.

The analyst says the government should establish a clear objective before providing support. “Is the aim to preserve connectivity? Protect jobs? Stabilise the aviation sector? Give AirAsia time to restructure? Or fundamentally overhaul the company?”

He also says a financing package worth several billion ringgit may not address the underlying problems if AirAsia’s business model remains unchanged.

Any government support could therefore come with conditions, including stronger corporate governance, greater shareholder participation in the restructuring burden, restrictions on shareholder distributions, operational restructuring and safeguards against future losses being transferred to taxpayers.

The government would also need to assess AirAsia’s ability to repay creditors and whether the proposed support would solve the airline’s financial problems or merely defer them.

Governance is another concern. It was reported that in exchange for this government backing, the government wants two seats on AirAsia’s board.

With only two board members, the analyst questions whether the existing structure provides sufficient oversight for the scale of restructuring now facing the group.
