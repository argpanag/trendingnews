Glenn Close cries! John Malkovich weeps! But it’s Nathan Fielder everyone wants to talk to at the honorary Oscars

TheTools · 16.11.2026 18:47 · source · permalink

All of Hollywood’s biggest stars are in attendance for the Governors’ awards, but nobody gets drunk and the biggest draw is an awkward documentarian

Timothée Chalamet was seconds away from getting into trouble. It was just after 6pm in Hollywood and Lynette Howell Taylor, president of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, was trying to corral a crowd of 700-odd people into sitting down so she could begin the Governors awards ceremony. She jokingly threatened to start calling out anyone still standing by name if they didn’t sit down imminently. Chalamet was the only one left on his feet, circling the table where he was due to sit next to his girlfriend, Kylie Jenner, opposite Tom Holland and Zendaya. Howell Taylor gave him a few seconds’ grace. He looked like the popular kid testing how far he could push a headteacher.

The Governors awards had a curiously school-like atmosphere this year. Even the carpet in the Ray Dolby Ballroom was faded and striped. The social structure was much the same: closed-off and insular, with cliques, alliances, legendary enmities and intricate hierarchies of popularity and influence. There were those who seemed entirely comfortable – Chalamet, Holland, Tom Cruise, Matt Damon, Willem Dafoe, Seth Rogen – and others still angling for a better table.

Two hours earlier, I had arrived in a Toyota hybrid, almost certainly the worst car in the lot, wearing a jacket from Goodwill. At 4pm, the reception was still relatively quiet. There was a bar, a s’mores station and a playlist of default office-party songs: Timbaland, Rihanna, that sort of thing.

Among the first people I spoke to were Jordan Firstman, whose debut feature Club Kid was released in the US earlier this month, and Curry Barker, the 27-year-old film-maker behind Obsession. Firstman told me he was most keen to run into Parker Posey. Barker, dressed in a gleaming white suit, was hoping the night would finally be the one he met Christopher Nolan. Nolan’s wife was trying to arrange an introduction but Barker was hesitant. “Everyone’s talking to him, of course.” It was his first time at the Governors awards. “I like it,” he said. “I just don’t really know what it is.”

The Academy’s annual ceremony honouring careers and contributions to cinema are an unusual Hollywood gathering. Unlike the Oscars, it is not televised, and there is no suspense about who will win. Instead, the Academy assembles the people who make, finance, distribute, promote, preserve and perform in films to recognise five people whose work has helped shape the medium. This year’s recipients were Glenn Close, whose eight competitive Oscar nominations are yet to yield a win; Floyd Norman, Disney’s pioneering first Black animator; Ridley Scott, who was unable to attend; and Christine Vachon and Pamela Koffler, the independent producing partners behind Killer Films and champions of new queer cinema.

It was funny to see the raw material that would soon be repackaged as Vogue spreads and elevated to headline news: in reality, a fairly drab event. By 4.30, waiters were rolling out hors d’oeuvres, including truffles, although nobody seemed especially interested in eating them. I overhear an executive say, “I’m beating my own drum, but her films just aren’t going to make enough.” Christa, a bartender who has been working the event for 10 years, tells me she no longer gets starstruck. Some people are nice, she says; others not so much.

Nathan Fielder, for the record, was nice. He stopped to talk to practically everybody, displaying a degree of social ease that almost seemed a betrayal of his onscreen persona. His Elizabeth Holmes documentary, You Can See Everything, was one of the films people were keenest to discuss. Several approached him to say how “genius” it was. Fielder seemed graciously to accept the praise, before eventually slipping into the figure more familiar from his television work: hands clasped together, trotting away with a wobbly, timid gait.

He was frequently in the company of Lance Oppenheim, his co-director on the documentary and the filmmaker behind Primetime. Executives approached Oppenheim to tell him how much they loved his movie. “Which one?” he asked. Later Seth Rogen stood with them, wiping away a tear from beneath his glasses as they laughed.

At 5.30, Willem Dafoe arrived, gliding slowly up the stairs. David Byrne appeared at around the same time, looking sweetly awkward and a little forlorn. An executive told him, “Thank you for being you.” Edward Norton was pouring red wine. Matt Damon had an arm around his wife. John Malkovich was chatting with Robert Eggers. Rashida Jones blinked with awkwardness. Charles Melton made a particularly slick flick of the wrist to retrieve a tiny hamburger slider. Travis Knight, director of Wildwood, offered me nicotine gum, apparently relieved to have found somebody who looked as lost as he did.

Tom Cruise was obviously the most intense person in the room. He wrapped his arms around whomever he was talking to and drew them into his extraordinarily concentrated gaze, as though he were about to impart something earth-shattering. Tom Holland arrived holding Zendaya’s hand, walking right past.

At 5.43pm, I overheard security say that Kylie Jenner and Timothée Chalamet were five minutes away. When they arrived, Jenner smiled cordially at everyone who approached her, and I found myself counting how long she held the smile for: one, two, two-and-a-half. When she and Chalamet eventually took their places, the room began to settle.

Howell Taylor took the stage to introduce the evening. Her five-minute speech was so abstract and platitudinous that I struggled to follow it. She spoke about artificial intelligence and uttered phrases such as “It’s not just a celebration, it’s an endurance”. I later put the speech through Pangram, an AI-detection programme. Its verdict: “100% likely generated by AI.”

Mercifully, the evening went on. The first award went to Floyd Norman. Mahershala Ali presented the Honorary Oscar to the man who joined Disney in 1956 as its first Black animator and worked on Sleeping Beauty and One Hundred and One Dalmatians. The room rose to its feet.

Glenn Close followed. John Malkovich, her friend and co-star in Dangerous Liaisons, took to the stage to celebrate her, beginning with a joke about the mark of a great actor: dying on screen. He recalled Close’s crushed-chandelier demise while fleeing alien invaders in Mars Attacks! The chandelier, he explained, belonged to Nancy Reagan. Malkovich praised Close’s willingness to play “the more complex, darker characters”. Then he lost his footing. He began to cry as he sang her praises.

It was moving to see Close with an Oscar in her hands. She cried, too. She veered away from the teleprompter, took a breath and tried to correct herself: “Oh, I see what I meant to say.”

Matt Damon introduced Ridley Scott’s award. The two men, Damon said as a montage of clips from Scott’s films began to play, were forever sending each other books and scripts in the hope of working together again.

Finally, Todd Haynes and Julianne Moore presented the Irving G Thalberg Memorial award to the producing partners Christine Vachon and Pamela Koffler and surprise guest Neil Patrick Harris performed a number from Hedwig and the Angry Inch, the 2001 film the pair produced.

Vachon and Koffler were the first female producing team and the first independent producers to receive the Thalberg award. In their speeches, they thanked the people who were rarely credited: their lawyers, distributors and, most emphatically, their assistants.

By 8.45pm, the party had begun to resemble a school disco at which the teachers had announced that the buses were waiting. Nobody was drunk or disorderly. This was very disappointing. The evening was remarkably civilised. Kylie, Timothée and Zendaya were nowhere to be seen; they had been among the first to depart. Tom Cruise was the last man lingering. Most people were in a rush to get home. Parker Posey and Julianne Moore chatted while in line for their limousine.

It had been an unremarkable four hours. Marco, an usher who sent me down in the lift, told me how hard he and the hundreds-strong team had worked to put the event together. “We’ve been doing this all week,” he said. When I told him I hoped he got to enjoy some of the night, he laughed and looked away sheepishly. Everyone had cleared out by 9pm.
