Moroccan voter awareness challenges election boycott calls | Tarek El Bouzidi | MEO

TheTools · 17.09.2026 07:30 · source · permalink

As Morocco’s September 23 parliamentary election approaches, the political debate is moving beyond the traditional binary of participation versus boycott. Alongside calls portraying abstention as the clearest expression of anger and dissatisfaction, a broader discourse is increasingly linking the election to accountability, the choice of alternatives and a change in political representation. This shift is making political criticism itself part of the debate over the record of those in power and who should be entrusted with the next phase.

The shift takes on added significance given the framework established for the election at an early stage. In his July 2025 Throne Speech, more than a year before the vote, King Mohammed VI stressed the need to adopt and make public, before the end of that year, the overall framework governing elections to the House of Representatives. He instructed the interior minister to ensure thorough preparations and to open consultations with political parties and other stakeholders.

A year later, on July 29, King Mohammed VI placed the election within a broader political and development outlook, calling for a spirit of confidence and optimism to prevail over narratives of despair and frustration. He also expressed his hope that the election and the formation of a new government would usher in a new cycle in the country’s development process.

These directives were subsequently put into effect through the activation of a central committee to monitor the elections, in line with the royal instructions, bringing together the interior minister and prosecutor-general alongside regional and provincial committees. The committee was tasked with overseeing the various stages of the electoral process, safeguarding its integrity and fairness, addressing violations and initiating investigations or judicial proceedings where necessary, as well as liaising with political parties whenever required.

Official figures underscore the scale of the electoral race. A total of 15,801,162 voters are registered on the electoral rolls, with women accounting for 46 percent. Some 1,850 lists comprising 7,288 candidates are competing for 395 seats in the House of Representatives, equivalent to more than 18 candidates for each seat.

Across local constituencies, the number of electoral lists has risen from 1,472 in 2021 to 1,615 in 2026, while the number of candidates has increased from 5,046 to 5,505. Women account for 2,658 of the nominations, representing 36.47 percent of the total.

The previous election, meanwhile, offers a cautionary example of the limits of reading online activity as a measure of voter sentiment. In its final report on the 2021 election, the National Human Rights Council recorded around 17,000 posts or references associated with the hashtag ‘#Mansawtch,’ meaning “I’m not voting”. The council stressed, however, that the posts did not all constitute calls for an election boycott.

An analysis of the sources of the tweets found that only 10.4 percent originated in Morocco, while the largest shares came from accounts based in Germany, Belgium, Britain, the United States and elsewhere.

On the ground, meanwhile, the council recorded limited participation in some of the boycott-related activities it observed in Casablanca and Tangier, with attendance ranging from around 10 to 15 people.

By the close of polling day, turnout had reached 50.18 percent, up from 43 percent in 2016. The 2021 parliamentary election was held alongside communal and regional elections and took place under exceptional circumstances linked to the Covid-19 pandemic, while the campaign period was also shorter than usual.

The contrast between the reach of the hashtag online and the scale of mobilisation observed on the ground illustrates the limits of treating digital activity as a direct measure of the social weight of campaigns advocating abstention.

Recent political scholarship also draws a distinction between political criticism and withdrawal from public life. In his 2019 study, Dissatisfied and Critical Citizens: The Political Effect of Complaining, Polish sociologist Maciej Kowalewski examined how expressions of dissatisfaction can develop into political demands and action. Where political and institutional channels for influence remain available, he argues, discontent need not stop at complaint but can instead become a vehicle for seeking political change.

A related argument is developed by British-American political scientist Pippa Norris in her 2011 book Democratic Deficit: Critical Citizens Revisited. Norris’s concept of the “critical citizen” refers to people who place greater demands on political institutions and scrutinise their performance more closely, without this necessarily translating into withdrawal from political participation.

That distinction is increasingly relevant to the Moroccan debate, where political criticism is being articulated not only as an expression of dissatisfaction but also through questions of performance, alternatives and political responsibility.

Morocco’s increasingly connected political landscape is also reshaping how voters engage with the electoral process. The country has more than 41 million internet subscriptions, including around 38.1 million mobile internet subscriptions, according to figures from the National Telecommunications Regulatory Agency. The wider reach of digital platforms gives voters more direct access to party programmes, political statements and candidates’ records, making it easier to compare positions and scrutinise performance outside the traditional structures through which election campaigns have been conducted.

The election is also taking place under a broad electoral observation framework designed to strengthen safeguards for the integrity and transparency of the process. A total of 3,006 observers, including 783 from the National Human Rights Council and 2,023 from Moroccan associations, have been accredited, alongside delegations from 20 international and regional bodies and organisations and representatives of 10 diplomatic missions.

The Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe has said Morocco has built up substantial experience in organising elections, described preparations for the September 23 vote as advanced and welcomed recent legislative changes aimed at improving the electoral framework.

Taken together, these indicators suggest that the 2026 election cannot be reduced to a political landscape defined primarily by abstention. The 2021 experience exposed a marked gap between the visibility of boycott calls online and their limited capacity to translate into comparable mobilisation on the ground, while the current election is unfolding amid broader electoral competition, early institutional preparations and a more sustained public debate over political performance and representation.

At the centre of this debate is a more demanding conception of electoral participation, in which the ballot can serve not only as a means of choosing between competing political options, but also as an instrument of accountability, allowing voters to assess the government’s record, weigh competing programmes and determine the composition of the next legislature.
