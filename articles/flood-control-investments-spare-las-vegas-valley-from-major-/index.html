Flood-Control Investments Spare Las Vegas Valley From Major Damage After Weekend Storms

Heavy rain that soaked southern Nevada over Halloween weekend left pockets of flooding across the Las Vegas Valley. Even so, officials say decades of flood-control spending kept the region from suffering serious damage to its infrastructure.

The worst of the downpours arrived Friday afternoon, Oct. 30, and concentrated over downtown Las Vegas' Arts District and parts of Henderson. Andrew Trelease, general manager and chief engineer of the Clark County Regional Flood Control District, estimated that downtown took on roughly an inch of rain in about an hour. He described it as approximately a five-year storm. Some businesses found water inside their buildings, and flash flooding hit several neighborhoods.

Rainfall totals varied widely from place to place. A gauge near Eastern Avenue and Bonanza Road logged 0.83 inch on Friday. Estimates in parts of Boulder City, East Henderson, and North Las Vegas ranged from half an inch to a full inch.

Henderson crews carried out four flood-related rescues. One person pulled from a wash near North Pueblo Boulevard went into cardiac arrest and later died at a hospital. As of Monday, the Clark County coroner's office had not released the person's identity. Flooding and debris also temporarily closed Racetrack Road, as well as a section of Lake Mead Parkway near Lake Las Vegas Parkway that reopened later that night.

Beyond the valley, Clark County reported flood damage and debris on roads in Laughlin, Gold Butte, Moapa Valley, and an area just north of Bunkerville. The Clark County Fire Department responded to 10 vehicles caught in floodwaters over the weekend. The Nevada Department of Transportation said it had no confirmed damage to paved roads, though assessments were still underway.

The city of Las Vegas directed its cleanup toward the hardest-hit areas and toward clearing debris from drain inlets to prevent backups, noting its storm drains and basins held up well. Across the valley, eight flood-control projects are under construction, and another 12 are planned, including drainage upgrades along Charleston Boulevard.

Since its founding in the 1980s, the district has completed more than 722 miles of storm drains and channels, along with 113 detention basins, at a cost of about $2.77 billion. Trelease said that investment is paying off. Officials also acknowledged that neighborhoods still lacking flood-control facilities remain more exposed when storms like this one roll through.
