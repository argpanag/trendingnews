Even without a high fever, people with influenza A can still experience severe respiratory distress.

TheTools · 12.10.2026 17:37 · source · permalink

A patient with influenza A had only a mild fever but experienced rapidly progressing respiratory failure, requiring emergency intubation. Doctors warn that the severity of influenza does not depend entirely on body temperature, especially in the elderly and those with underlying health conditions.

Many people still consider influenza A a common illness and assess its severity based on body temperature. However, the reality at the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases shows that influenza A can progress severely, causing rapid respiratory failure in the elderly and those with underlying conditions, threatening life within just a few days of onset.

According to Master's degree holder and resident physician Le Thi Huyen from the Intensive Care Unit of the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases, patient HXT, 72 years old, has a history of hypertension for many years, is regularly taking medication, and had previously suffered a stroke.

On October 6th, the patient was admitted to the hospital with a high fever, productive cough, progressively worsening shortness of breath, and respiratory failure. A rapid test was positive for influenza A. Upon examination, doctors noted severe pneumonia and lung damage significantly affecting gas exchange capacity, requiring respiratory support with oxygen concentrations of approximately 40–50% to maintain blood oxygen levels.

Tests revealed an inflammatory response and elevated infection markers. The patient was treated for influenza A, along with treatment for the infection and intensive respiratory support.

After 3 days of treatment, the patient's fever subsided, and hemodynamics were relatively stable. However, the lung damage remained severe, the patient was unable to maintain effective respiration independently, and continued to require non-invasive mechanical ventilation with an oxygen support concentration of approximately 40%.

According to Dr. Huyen, one of the factors that makes treatment difficult is a history of stroke, especially a recent cerebral infarction, which impairs the ability to cough and expectorate phlegm. Meanwhile, pneumonia increases respiratory tract secretions. When patients cannot cough and expectorate effectively, phlegm easily accumulates, making it difficult to clear the airways, affecting ventilation and increasing the risk of respiratory complications.

According to Doctor Huyen, the patient is currently in the acute phase of the disease, with severe lung damage, and requires continued support, airway care, and close monitoring. Due to the patient's limited ability to cough up phlegm, the treatment process may be prolonged.

The second case was admitted to the Emergency Department of the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases. The patient had a history of heavy smoking for many years, suffered from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and had been regularly using nebulized medication at home for the past 3-4 months.

Approximately four days before hospitalization, the patient began coughing, expectorating white phlegm, experiencing progressively worsening shortness of breath, accompanied by fatigue and poor appetite. Notably, the patient only had a mild fever, around 38°C, with no significant signs of high fever. However, the shortness of breath worsened, prompting the family to take the patient to a nearby medical facility for examination.

Here, test results confirmed the patient had influenza A. Due to severe respiratory failure, the patient had to breathe oxygen through a mask with a reservoir bag at a flow rate of up to 15 liters/minute, and was then transferred to the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases for further treatment.

Upon admission to the Emergency Department, the patient was agitated, restless, breathing rapidly, with pale skin and mucous membranes; and there were numerous abnormal crackles in the lungs. Given the severe respiratory failure, the doctors promptly intubated the patient, provided respiratory support, and implemented intensive resuscitation measures.

However, the disease continued to progress rapidly, with the appearance of serious systemic disorders that did not improve despite intensive resuscitation.

According to doctors, in people with COPD, the airways are often inflamed and obstructed for an extended period, leading to impaired respiratory function. When infected with influenza A, the inflammation of the respiratory tract and increased mucus production can worsen the existing obstruction. At the same time, lung damage from influenza can reduce the ability to exchange gases, causing the patient to quickly fall into respiratory failure.

This case demonstrates that the severity of influenza A cannot be judged solely by the level of fever. In individuals with chronic lung disease, progressively worsening shortness of breath, especially when it progresses rapidly, can be a warning sign that the illness is becoming severe and requires immediate medical attention.

Influenza A is a respiratory infection caused by the influenza virus, primarily transmitted through respiratory droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks. Symptoms typically include fever, cough, sore throat, body aches, and fatigue. In young children, the elderly, and those with chronic illnesses, influenza can cause pneumonia, worsen pre-existing conditions, and lead to respiratory failure and other dangerous complications.

Dr. Pham Van Phuc, Deputy Director of the Intensive Care Unit at the National Hospital for Tropical Diseases, advises that people should proactively get vaccinated against influenza annually, especially those in high-risk groups. In addition, it is necessary to maintain hand hygiene, cover the mouth and nose when coughing or sneezing, keep living spaces well-ventilated, and limit close contact with people who have symptoms of the disease. People with influenza should limit contact with others to reduce the risk of infection.

When children or individuals at high risk develop symptoms suspected of being influenza, families should take them to see a doctor promptly for assessment and appropriate treatment. Do not self-medicate, especially with antibiotics, without a doctor's prescription.

Patients should be taken to a medical facility immediately if they exhibit signs such as shortness of breath, rapid breathing, cyanosis, chest pain, lethargy, unusual irritability, or a rapid deterioration of their condition. In children, signs such as difficulty breathing, chest retraction, lethargy, seizures, inability to drink, or signs of dehydration require prompt medical attention.

Doctors note that the severity of influenza cannot be assessed solely by the fever level. Patients need close monitoring of respiratory status and any unusual health changes to detect early signs of worsening condition and receive timely treatment.
