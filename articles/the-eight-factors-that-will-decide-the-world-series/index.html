The Eight Factors That Will Decide the World Series

TheTools · 23.10.2026 21:56 · source · permalink

The American League’s best team and the National League’s best team are playing each other in the World Series. Maybe it’s not the matchup that Fox executives or casual October-only fans would have preferred, but they had their turn in 2024, when the Yankees and Dodgers met in the TV Ratings Classic. Those franchises may have entered 2026 as the favorites to win their respective leagues, but Tampa Bay and Milwaukee outplayed them. The Rays and Brewers were deeper, executed better, and swept them both.

This World Series is for baseball lovers. It’s for everyone who argues that talent development and roster depth can still win out over the biggest checkbooks. It’s for fans who appreciate teams that put the ball in play (Tampa Bay), take their walks (Milwaukee), and turn elite defense and aggressive baserunning into an advantage (both). The names on the jerseys might not sell this matchup to a national audience, but the baseball should.

The 2026 Fall Classic unfolds under the threat of a lockout. The current collective bargaining agreement ends after this season, and baseball’s owners are claiming that the game has competitive balance problems driven by the increasing chasm of spending between the richest franchises and their smaller-market counterparts. But the Brewers and Rays rank 19th and 28th in payroll, respectively. Their success isn’t the result of two small-market teams getting hot for a month, either. Milwaukee ranks second in regular-season wins since 2020. Tampa Bay ranks fifth. This isn’t like 2023, when Arizona and Texas both were wild-card teams that failed to win more than 90 games. 

Here’s where the series will be won—and what could separate two teams that have spent the season finding advantages on the margins.

The Aces’ Performances: Drew Rasmussen vs. Jacob Misiorowski 

The Rays opted to give their ace, Drew Rasmussen, an extra day of rest before pitching in the World Series. Baseball fans who were hoping to see Rasmussen go head-to-head with Jacob Misiorowski will not get their wish. Instead, Nick Martinez is starting for Tampa Bay in Game 1, and Rasmussen will go in Game 2. Still, Rasmussen and Misiorowski will set the tone for the entire series. 

Misiorowski has the most dominant fastball of any MLB starter, and he overpowers hitters with it. He’s used his four-seam fastball over 60 percent of the time and has averaged 100.6 mph with that pitch. That pitch has lived at the top of the zone, and it’s been the best in the entire sport by Statcast’s run value. 

Meanwhile, Rasmussen is the poster child for the recent trend of pitchers who don't have overpowering velocity instead throwing different types of fastballs. His sinker, cutter, and four-seamer all rate as well-above-average pitches, and he throws each one 25-30 percent of the time. Because he relies on the cutter and sinker, he doesn’t generate nearly as many whiffs, but Rasmussen has thrown 21 playoff innings this month in three starts. He’s allowed three total runs and 16 baserunners.

Game 1 will be a matchup of the modern flamethrower vs. the crafty veteran (Martinez), in a wonderful battle of contrasting styles—especially since the Rays strike out at the lowest rate in the league. Like Toronto last year, the Rays’ ability to make contact, foul off great pitches, and wear down an opposing starter could be critical to their plan of forcing Milwaukee’s bullpen into the game sooner than Brewers manager Pat Murphy would like. 

Misiorowski has struggled (relative to his standards) with command in his two playoff starts. He ran a lot of deep counts against the Padres and Dodgers and failed to complete five innings in either start. San Diego jumped him for two early runs in Game 1 of the NLDS, and he’s allowed more than a baserunner per inning in each start. 

Misiorowski still has the highest baseline and ceiling of any pitcher in this matchup—his 128 stuff+ was the best of any starter in baseball by a wide margin this year. But once Rasmussen takes the ball in Game 2, the Rays will have a starting pitcher advantage in every non-Misiorowski start of the series.

Bryan Baker’s Declining Stuff 

Rays closer Bryan Baker doesn’t record outs because of his overpowering swing-and-miss stuff, but it is notable that his stuff declined during the playoffs. Baker had to pitch on three consecutive days to close out the ALCS, and he managed only one strikeout. At face value, there should be no reason to be concerned about Baker. He closed out all seven Rays playoff wins—totaling seven innings pitched—without allowing a run.

However, a look under the hood should raise some flags as the Rays try to rely on him to close out multiple World Series victories. 

Bryan Baker Stuff+

  • Regular season: 113
  • ALDS vs. Yankees: 104
  • ALCS vs. Guardians: 104 

Most of the drop-off came from Baker’s fastball. It doesn’t have the same quality of pitch shape, and the Brewers could be the team to finally get to the closer that has dominated for the Rays all season long. 

Freddy Peralta pitches against the Cleveland Guardians in Game 6

Freddy Peralta’s Improving Stuff

The playoff formula has been simple for Tampa Bay. It's won seven baseball games, and five of them were started by either Rasmussen or Freddy Peralta. Peralta was a deadline reclamation project acquisition from the Mets. At the time, Peralta had a 4.99 ERA. He then pitched to a 3.25 ERA with the Rays. 

There are a variety of reasons why Peralta’s ERA improved. A lot of it was moving in front of a much better Tampa Bay defense. He simplified his mechanics by pitching from the stretch exclusively. He upped his fastball usage to be more like the “Fastball Freddy” of old when he pitched for Milwaukee. And his indoor starts at Tropicana Field also helped to amplify the ride on his fastball at the top of the zone. 

All three of Peralta’s postseason starts have come at home. With Martinez and Rasmussen starting the first two games in Milwaukee, Peralta will presumably start Game 3 at the Trop. He’d then be in line to pitch a potential Game 7 on the road. 

As Peralta has reached back to find some extra playoff juice, his stuff has leaped off the page. It’s been a steady upward trend from his first half with the Mets and is peaking right now.

  • Regular season (Mets): 95
  • Regular season (Rays): 101
  • ALDS: 115
  • ALCS: 114

Tampa Bay’s Right Hand–Dominant Pitching vs. Milwaukee’s Offense

The Rays have only two left-handed pitchers that have thrown in a high-leverage setting in the playoffs: Ian Seymour and Cam Booser. Steven Matz threw two innings to close out their Game 3 loss in Cleveland down a run, but Seymour and Booser are the only lefties that will likely be trusted by manager Kevin Cash in a big spot. 

Assuming the Rays use Griffin Jax and Nick Martinez as their Games 3 and 4 starters, Milwaukee’s expected starting lineup will have six lefties. Brice Turang bats second, Jake Bauers fourth, Christian Yelich fifth, Garrett Mitchell sixth, plus Sal Frelick and David Hamilton at the bottom. Milwaukee had huge splits in its offensive success against righties and lefties this season. It had the league’s best offense against righties and was around league average against southpaws. 

Milwaukee’s Offensive Split by Hand

  • vs. RHP: 112 wRC+, first in baseball
  • vs. LHP: 97 wRC+, 13th in baseball

The Padres and Dodgers had multiple lefty starters and relievers to throw at this Milwaukee lineup. The Rays do not have the same luxury. Either Matz will be thrown into more high-leverage roles, or the Rays run the risk of paying the reliever familiarity tax later in the series. 

Milwaukee’s Patience Against Tampa Bay’s Changeup-Heavy Staff

One way for Tampa Bay to balance its heavily right-handed pitching staff is to throw a ton of changeups. The changeup is traditionally a weapon against opposite-handed hitters, but the Rays have expanded its job description. They’ve thrown more right-on-right changeups than any team in baseball this year, pushing an approach that could become more common across the league in seasons to come.

Changeups account for about a quarter of Tampa Bay’s pitches this postseason. Rather than relying on sliders and curveballs to get hitters to chase, the Rays use their changeups to generate whiffs and weak contact below the zone. But that approach faces a particularly difficult test against Milwaukee: No lineup in baseball chases less often than the Brewers.

That makes the bottom of the strike zone a critical battleground in this series. Can the Rays convince Milwaukee’s hitters to expand their zone or land enough changeups for strikes to keep them guessing? If the Brewers can lay off the pitches that finish below the zone, they can force Tampa Bay’s pitchers to put the ball back into the zone, where Milwaukee can do a lot more damage. 

The Fight to Establish the Running Game

One reason that old-school baseball fans will love this Rays-Brewers matchup so much is both teams’ emphasis on the running game. Both teams finished in the top five in baseball this season in total stolen bases. Instead of relying on doubles and home runs, which neither did this year, they leaned into walks and singles, and then turned them into de facto extra-base hits by taking more bases than most of the league. 

That aggressiveness on the bases has continued into October, where these two teams are averaging more than one steal per game and lead the playoff field. Tampa Bay is a perfect 11-for-11 on the basepaths, and it especially likes to go early in counts given the lack of swing-and-miss in the lineup. 

The test in this series now becomes which catcher can do a better job of blocking pitches and throwing out runners. William Contreras graded as one of the best catchers when it comes to catching runners stealing. So much of controlling the running game is still down to the pitcher’s time to the plate, but Contreras (28 percent caught stealing) has the advantage over Tampa Bay’s Liam Hicks (20 percent) in this series. 

Soft Contact vs. Infield Defenses 

Milwaukee and Tampa Bay spent the season stressing opposing infielders. They bunt for hits, move runners, and turn soft grounders into singles where other teams settle for outs. Both offenses force defenses to make difficult plays, and how those plays unfold matters considerably in this series.

Both teams are excellent defensively in the outfield. The infield is where the defensive moments will hinge. Milwaukee has two of the league’s best defenders at their positions in second baseman Brice Turang and third baseman David Hamilton, while Cooper Pratt has been closer to average at shortstop. Tampa Bay’s strength is up the middle with Taylor Walls, one of baseball’s best defensive shortstops, but Junior Caminero at third and Richie Palacios at second have been less consistent. 

That makes this a fascinating test for two of baseball’s best run-prevention machines. Each has spent the season putting pressure on opponents to field a slow roller cleanly, make a quick transfer, or deliver an accurate throw with a runner racing to first. In a series between teams this good at preventing runs, extending an inning could be enough to decide a game or two. Just ask Padres and Yankees fans wondering what could have been right now, if not for some miscues that swung games against them. 

The Home Run Trot Battle

One fun side plot to this World Series is the duel between Contreras and Caminero. It’s not just that these two hitters have carried their respective playoff offenses with clutch hits, timely homers, and elite plate coverage. The two have one-upped each other with the length of their respective home run trots. Contreras had a 35.77-second home run trot around the bases following a game-tying homer in the NLCS. Caminero then outdid him with his 38-second celebration that almost took the roof off Tropicana Field. Caminero stopped at first to pound his chest, slowed at second to put his hand to his ear, and then acknowledged Randy Arozarena’s vintage arms-crossed celebration near home plate. Caminero and Contreras show a ton of emotion on the field, and their trots have launched a ton of discourse on players showing emotion for the cool shit they do on a baseball field.
