Beaconsfield mine collapse survivor Todd Russell dead at 54

Todd Russell, part of “one of the great survival stories of Australian history” when he resurfaced after 14 days trapped underground in a collapsed Tasmanian mine in 2006, has died aged 54.

Russell died after an undisclosed physical illness, revealing earlier this month he had “nothing left to give”.

With fellow miner Brant Webb, Russell was trapped by a rockfall at a gold mine at Beaconsfield in northern Tasmania on Anzac Day 2006.

The pair were presumed dead after the body of another miner, Larry Knight, had been found, but were discovered alive after five days. Fourteen other miners escaped the rockfall. Russell and Webb spent a further nine days underground, before they walked out of the mine on May 9, 2006.

Russell had detailed his mental health battles in interviews in 2022 and 2024, and said he had sought professional help to deal with the trauma following his experience in the collapsed mine.

Earlier in November, Russell revealed on social media “years of pain, uncertainty, appointments, treatments, setbacks, and endless battles plus the emotional exhaustion of having to pick myself up time and time again, have left me with nothing left to give”.

Former Sunrise host David Koch offered his condolences on a sad day for Russell’s family.

“He is part of one of the great survival stories of Australian history, and which captured the attention of the entire world,” Koch said on Tuesday.

Koch was among a throng of media from around Australia and the world broadcasting and reporting from outside the mine during the 14-day ordeal.

He and co-host Melissa Doyle were part of a group – including then-Australian Workers’ Union national secretary Bill Shorten and vice-president Paul Howes – who would gather at the home of Russell’s mother to offer comfort to the family.

Driving to the site on the morning the miners would surface, Koch got a call from one of the rescue workers, saying Russell and Webb were coming out.

“[Russell] had asked for the door to be kept open so he could thank us for what we did with the family during that time, and that’s when he gave me his ID tag as a thank you,” Koch recalled.

“One of the most emotional moments of my entire career. I’ll absolutely never forget it.”

‘He was a shooter, I was a fisher, he liked Fords, I liked Holdens, he was country music and I love me rock ’n’ roll.’
Fellow miner Brant Webb

He also praised the rescue workers who helped bring Russell and Webb out of the mine.

“We should always be aware of the remarkable job that they did in saving two of their mates,” Koch said.

Webb promised to keep Russell’s legacy alive, telling ABC on Tuesday he would remember his friend for his “will not to give up”.

“He was a shooter, I was a fisher, he liked Fords, I liked Holdens, he was country music and I love me rock ’n’ roll, but we had kids and we had family and that was our bond,” Webb said.

“We loved our families, and we’ll give it all to our children.”

How the rescue unfolded

The survival story, and the ingenuity of the rescuers working almost a kilometre underground, captured public imagination like few events before or since.

For five days, the men were completely alone in the dark inside the steel basket of the teleloader they had been working in when an earthquake caused the tunnel to collapse.

They survived by collecting groundwater that was seeping through the rock above in their helmets, and they shared a single muesli bar that Webb happened to have in his pocket.

On the fifth day, rescuers removing rubble around the rear of the teleloader heard the men yelling “We’re here!” Word spread like wildfire that the pair had survived.

But how to get them out? It was too dangerous to blast a path to the trapped men due to hundreds of tonnes of fallen rock that covered their steel cage, and nor could the steel cage be cut without severe risk of further collapse.

So rescuers made a plan to dig a new tunnel underneath the cage – a major engineering feat that involved bringing special boring machinery from the mainland and adapting it to cut through 16 metres of solid quartz rock, estimated to be five times harder than cement.

Meanwhile, Russell and Webb ate yoghurt and chicken sandwiches passed to them via a small pilot hole drilled into their cage, talked about their families and listened to music, and used their helmets to relieve themselves.

The giant drill inched forward until miners used jackhammers to breach the final section beneath the steel cage.

Russell and Webb walked out of the mine at 5.59am to huge cheers from gathered crowds, and people lined the streets of Launceston at dawn as ambulances carrying the pair made their way to hospital.

The men attended Knight’s funeral six hours after their great escape.

Shorten said he was deeply saddened to hear of Russell’s death.

“Over the years we stayed in touch, often reflecting on the anniversary of that remarkable rescue and the courage he and Brant Webb, along with their families and workmates, showed while trapped underground for two weeks,” he said.

“Todd faced every challenge with strength and determination, and he fought bravely following his diagnosis, just as he did all those years ago while trapped underground.”

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese offered his condolences to Russell’s loved ones.

“Todd’s stoicism, resilience and good humour during and after that ordeal was the truest encapsulation of the Aussie character,” he said. “We think of his family, his mates and the entire Tasmanian community he gave so much back to.”

Tasmanian Premier Jeremy Rockliff also posted a tribute to Russell on Tuesday morning.

“Thoughts this morning are with Todd’s loved ones and everyone who had the absolute pleasure of knowing him,” Rockliff said.

Russell spent more than three decades as part of the Beaconsfield Volunteer Fire Brigade, retiring in March.

The Tasmanian Volunteer Fire Brigades Association shared its condolences on Tuesday.

“Todd will be remembered for his commitment to those around him, the friendships he forged, and the positive impact he made to the community of Beaconsfield and the broader West Tamar.

“His contribution to his community and the Beaconsfield brigade, and the respect he earned from others leave a lasting legacy that will not be forgotten.”
