Abdul el Sayed resists efforts from Haley Steven's to corner him on Israel

Michigan Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed has rejected a demand from his defeated primary rival, congresswoman Haley Stevens, that he apologize for criticism of US military aid to Israel two days before they were scheduled to campaign together in Detroit.

On Friday evening, El-Sayed rejected the demand from Stevens, whose fiancé campaigned this week for El-Sayed’s Republican opponent, former congressman Mike Rogers, at a Republican Jewish Coalition event, where he called the Democratic nominee’s criticism of Israel “100% disqualifying to be a United States senator”.

“Our campaign is and has always been focused on addressing the most critical challenges Michiganders are facing: Skyrocketing gas prices, unaffordable groceries, and the inability to see a doctor,” El-Sayed said in a statement in which he noted that the American Israel Public Affairs Committee spent heavily to defeat him in the primary.

“That focus is why our people-powered campaign defeated a historic $70M in outside spending from big corporations and AIPAC on behalf of Congresswoman Stevens. Now the stakes are even higher-defeating Trump’s hand-picked candidate, taking back the Senate, and defending our democracy,” El-Sayed continued.

“That should be the priority for all of us right now,” he added. “So while Congresswoman Stevens seems intent on relitigating a primary that ended three months ago, let’s all focus on the task at hand: that’s beating Mike Rogers, holding Trump accountable, and getting money out of politics, putting money in pockets, and passing Medicare for All.”

The candidate was responding to a lengthy statement from Stevens, in which she repeated a demand she first made on CNN, that she expected El-Sayed to effectively denounce himself for antisemitism and racism.

To assuage the fears of Black and Jewish voters who supported her in the primary, Stevens said, El-Sayed had to take down a tongue-in-cheek ad produced by his campaign to air on rightwing cable news channels that includes a clip of Tucker Carlson saying he would not vote for Rogers, and condemn what she called an antisemitic ad made by an outside group founded by commentator Cenk Uygur, which blames US aid to Israel for high food prices.

El-Sayed’s campaign already said that the ad posted online by Uygur -who was identified in Stevens’s statement as the uncle of streamer Hasan Piker – was “not affiliated in any way” with him or his candidacy.

The ad, which shows a fictional couple in a supermarket saying that they have to save money on groceries to pay “for killing Palestinians”, was not even made in support of El-Sayed, but repurposed from one originally produced in 2024 for Uygur’s own, short-lived presidential campaign.

Despite that history, Stevens argued that Uygur’s ad was inspired by El-Sayed’s rhetoric about Israel and insisted that he apologize for that too.

Uygur’s ad, Stevens wrote, “did not invent its argument; it dramatized one Dr. El-Sayed has made in his own words throughout this campaign: that Israel is the reason Michigan cannot afford schools, health care, and child care.”

“The standard here is simple,” she continued. “If those words are wrong when a Political Action Committee supporting his campaign says them, then they are equally wrong when Abdul says them, and Abdul has said them from the start. I therefore expect him to apologize for those statements as well. Abdul promises to get money out of politics. He can begin by getting Israel out of his explanation for everything Michigan cannot afford.”

The congresswoman’s statement did not go down well with many Democratic activists.

“If I was actually trying to harm Abdul’s campaign, Tommy Vietor, a former Obama staffer, and cohost of Pod Save America, posted in response to Stevens, “it would look a lot like the PR roll out we’ve seen from Haley that started with CNN, then her husband’s bizarre tirade, and now this crazy, hyper online, conspiratorial statement about a TV ad Abdul obviously hates!”

“I have no idea what the hell Haley Stevens is doing,” Michigan lawmaker Mallory McMorrow, who also ran in the Democratic primary, told CNN. “This is a moment where we have to recognize the stakes.”

McMorrow’s husband, Ray Wert, posted a link to a detailed explanation of why “a vote for Mike Rogers is NOT a vote for Jewish safety”, including that the Republican refused to denounce far-right antisemites on his own campaign, and is financially supported by “one of the country’s leading financiers of Christian nationalism”.

Leah Greenberg, a founder of the grassroots group Indivisible, was more blunt, accusing Stevens of taking part in a covert attempt to sabotage El-Sayed’s campaign. “I don’t know what the fuck is going on here”, she wrote on social media, “but if you supported Stevens and you don’t want to be associated with one of the most disgraceful, bigoted ratfucking attempts in recent political history I encourage you to let her know that this must stop immediately”.

At Florida rally, Trump praises Blacks for Trump, founded by former member of violent cult

Early in his remarks to supporters in Daytona Beach, Florida on Friday, Donald Trump recounted what he cast as very real and not at all imaginary conversations with one Black friend and one Jewish friend in Michigan who asked his advice on whether they should vote for Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers in the Senate race there.

The president began by making the wildly false, bigoted claim that “Abdul’s a jihadist”.

He then said: “I have a friend, he’s Jewish. He said, ‘What do you think I should do in Michigan?’ I said, ‘Are you stupid?’ He’s not voting for Abdul. I have another friend, he’s black. He said, ‘What do you recommend?’ I said, ‘I recommend you go Republican’. He said, ‘Okay, that’s what I’m going to do’”.

“Now if you’re Black or if you’re Jewish and you vote for Abdul, you’re not smart,” Trump said, pressing on the Republican attempt to peel Blacj and Jewish voters in Michigan away from El-Sayed. “I’m being nice. You’re not smart”

Moments later, the president spotted some supporters in the crowd he recognized.

“Oh, I have my friends over here, Blacks for Trump. I love these guys,” Trump said of the group founded by Maurice Symonette, also known as Michael the Black Man, a former member of a violent cult who posts anti-Semitic screeds and racist conspiracy theories online, and yet has been a featured member of the audience at Trump campaign events since 2016.

Symonette was known as Maurice Woodside until 1992, when the Black supremacist cult leader he followed, Yahweh ben Yahweh, was jailed for leading a conspiracy to murder 14 white people in initiation rites. Woodside was among the Miami-based Nation of Yahweh cult members charged in two of the murders, but he was acquitted.

After the trial, he reinvented himself as Michael the Black Man, a pirate radio preacher who delivered broadsides against gays and Democrats. His online sermons and writings suggest that he still sees himself as a follower of the cult leader, who died in 2007.

Symonette was interviewed before the rally on Friday by the pro-Trump network Real America’s Voice.

Top federal prosecutor in Alaska confirms civil and criminal investigations into Senate race featuring two Dan Sullivans

Speaking at a news conference in Anchorage on Friday, the top federal prosecutor in Alaska, Michael Heyman, confirmed the existence of both a criminal investigation and a civil investigation into an alleged scheme by Democrats to confuse voters by putting a candidate on the ballot for US Senate who has the same name as the Republican incumbent, senator Dan Sullivan.

“The civil lawsuit is publicly faced, and is focused on preserving the ballots,” Heyman said, referring to a Department of Justice suit filed last week, and amended on Friday, asking a ​federal judge ⁠to issue a preliminary ⁠injunction ​that would ‌block ‌Alaska from ‌certifying its Senate election result if Sullivan loses to his Democratic challenger, Mary Peltola and votes cast for ⁠another candidate named ​Daniel J. ​Sullivan ​could ​theoretically ‌change ​the outcome.

Since Alaska uses a system of ranked-choice voting, any voters who want to keep the incumbent senator Sullivan in office, have the option of ranking him and Daniel J. Sullivan first and second on their ballots, in either order, to be sure that their votes do not help elect Peltola.

Heyman would not say what potential crimes authorities are looking into, but cited reporting by the New York Times that Peltola’s campaign, and national Democrats, allegedly “orchestrated a sprawling conspiracy to recruit and prop up Daniel J. Sullivan as a sham candidate, and we are taking that extremely seriously.”

“When credible information comes out, we are committed to pursuing that information to deceive voters and we will act on it. We are acting on it intensely and swiftly,” Heyman said.

“This new information has resulted in a significant surge of law enforcement assets to Alaska,” he added. “As we speak, law enforcement around the country are taking investigative steps with respect to a criminal investigation on these matters.”

The FBI special-agent-in-charge of the bureau’s Anchorage office, Matthew Schlegel, also asked the public to aid the investigation by submitting information to a tips line.

At a campaign event in Juneau on Thursday night, Peltola denied that she knew anything about the effort to put him on the ballot. “I just want to clear the air. I knew nothing about any other candidates on the ballot except for me and the incumbent. That is all I have ever known about. I am not interested in tricking voters. I know I can beat Dan Sullivan,” she said, according to Alaska Public Media.

In response to a question from a reporter, who noted that “legal experts have really dismissed the basis for this case,” and described the civil lawsuit as “frivolous”, Heyman said he would not “litigate that case before the media”.

When another reporter asked “what types of crimes might be alleged,” Heyman said he would not go into specifics, but noted “conspiracy theories and election integrity” have been “been widely spoken about”.

While even some Democrats have described the effort to stack the ballot with Dan Sullivans as unethical, it is not clearly illegal. Alaska’s state’s supreme court has already ruled that it was legal for Daniel J. Sullivan to appear on the ballot.

California’s Democratic governor, Gavin Newsom, welcomed the federal appeals court ruling rejecting a Department of Justice demand that ballots sent to about 20,000 people the federal government suggests might not be citizens be set aside.

“Trump and his administration just lost for the third time this week in their desperate and dangerous attempts to meddle in the California election process with voting already underway and just days before Election Day,” Newsom said in a statement.

“These attacks on our democracy are serious, and the full force of our state is defending our democracy right now. We will continue to fight them on their illegal power grabs every step of the way, and deliver a free and fair election just as we’ve always done.”

California has criticized the Trump administration for suggesting, without evidence, that thousands of registered voters are not US citizens, four months after a similar claim, that nearly 16,000 noncitizens were registered to vote in Nevada, turned out to be untrue.

In the Nevada case, the Department of Homeland Security vastly reduced the number of noncitizens it claimed were registered to vote, sending state election officials a list of 185 people it believed were not citizens. Nevada officials investigated the list and found that all 185 voters were in fact citizens.

How the "billionaire tax" became a central issue in California's midterm election

One year ago, the idea of a billionaire tax in California was still in its infancy.

It wasn’t yet one of the most contentious ballot measure fights in state history – pitting Democrats against Democrats, drawing national attention from political heavyweights, and netting hundreds of millions of Silicon Valley dollars to fight it. Back then, it was a bold and desperate answer to Donald Trump’s healthcare cuts.

Trump had just signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which dictated a trillion dollars of Medicaid, Affordable Care Act and other healthcare funding be cut over the next decade to offset sweeping tax cuts. When the bill goes into effect in January, that could mean more than 3.4m Californians will lose their coverage, including millions of children.

“That’s why we were willing to do something that lots of people said was crazy,” said Dave Regan, the divisive architect of the billionaire tax and president of the healthcare union SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West (SEIU-UHW). “To us, what’s crazy is doing nothing and watching millions of people lose coverage.”

Now, with the midterms less than a week away, California voters will decide on whether to pass the ballot measure known as Proposition 40. The proposal would levy a one-time 5% tax on billionaires living in the state as of 1 January 2026, the proceeds of which would fund healthcare programs, along with some education and food assistance.

Republican Kansas Senate candidate sued by former patient

Roger Marshall, a physician Republican Senator in Kansas currently fighting an unexpectedly close reelection campaign against a Democratic challenger, has been sued by a former patient who accused him of charing illegally high interest rates on medical debt, the New York Times reported.

An investigation last month by the Times found that, during his time working as an obstetrician, Marshall had hounded more than 700 patients with lawsuits over unpaid medical bills.

The lawsuit contends that the interest rate Marshall charged, 18 percent, was so high as to violate Kansas law. Mr. Marshall’s lawyer defended the fee to the Times as “common practice” in the state.

Marshall’s Senate seat in normally deep-red Kansas has been under threat from Democratic nominee Adam Hamilton, a pastor who some polls have shown could win the race. The possibility of an unexpected Democratic pickup has drawn more attention onto the Kansas race in the final days before the midterm election.

A working-class Alaskan Native takes on a ‘multimillionaire’ Republican for seat in Congress

As Republicans fight to maintain their narrow majority in Congress, they face a unique challenge in Alaska – not from a Democrat, but from an independent commercial fisher and first-time candidate running to flip the state’s lone House seat.

Like voters across the country, Alaskans are deeply worried about affordability and the rising cost of basic necessities. Hill counts himself among the voters who have felt squeezed by high fuel prices driven up by Donald Trump’s war on Iran.

“When the spring fuel barge showed up in my home town of Naknek this spring, I went from paying $118 to fill my truck up to over $200,” Hill said. “Our gas went from $6.70 a gallon to almost $9 a gallon.”

ICE officer charged with allegedly hitting man with his vehicle in Houston

Local officials in Texas charged an Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) official this morning with aggravated assault with a deadly weapon for striking and running over a man with his vehicle.

The ICE official, Mario Alberto Morales, struck Miguel Parra Hernandez with his 2025 Jeep Grand Cherokee on 10 October in Houston.

The Harris County District Attorney, who announced the indictment this morning, alleged Morales weaponized his vehicle against Hernandez during a chase.

Morales struck and ran over both of Hernandez’s legs, leaving him “seriously injured and hospitalized to this day,” said Sean Teare, the Harris County district attorney.

Teare added that he hopes the indictment “sends a loud and clear message to every ICE officer who feels that they have a free pass to terrorize our streets: the time to stop is now. And if you don’t, you will be held accountable.”

On the morning of 10 October in Houston, ICE officials attempted to stop and arrest two men in a car, including Hernandez. According to Teare, the ICE officers were searching for a man who has seemingly been dead for nine years. The two men fled on foot, with Morales using his vehicle to chase down Hernandez, an undocumented Mexican man with no criminal history and with no deportation order.

Surveillance footage and body camera video released this morning by the Harris County district attorney’s office shows Morales speeding his vehicle towards Hernandez and striking him in a parking lot. Morales then exited the vehicle, while Hernandez can be seen on the ground yelling and crying in pain. According to local officials, Hernandez suffered multiple fractures.

In audio of a 911 call by Morales, also released this morning by county officials, the officer tells the dispatcher in a calm voice that Hernandez “hit my car”.

Morales could face between two to 20 years in prison, officials said.

This is not the first time the Harris County district attorney’s office has expressed frustration with ICE activity on the streets of Houston. Earlier this year, an ICE officer shot and killed 52-year-old Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, while on his way to work. The district attorney’s office launched an investigation into the shooting of Salgado.

Pope Leo votes in US midterms in first known political ballot for any pontiff

in Rome

Pope Leo has voted in the US midterm elections – the first time a pontiff is known to have cast a ballot in any political election at any time in history.

Leo, who has clashed with the US president, Donald Trump, over several issues, submitted his vote by post in Chicago, where he was born, the Vatican said on Friday. Leo became the first American leader of the Catholic church last year after the death of Pope Francis.

The Vatican said Leo “has exercised his right to vote as a United States citizen, whenever he was eligible to do so”, including in Peru, where he lived for years as a missionary.

“The Holy Father expresses his belief in the right and the duty of citizens to participate in democratic processes,” the statement added.

Leo, who before being elected pope was known as Robert Prevost, reportedly made his request for the mail-in ballot on 3 October, registering at an address in New Lenox, Illinois.

That was 10 days before he underwent surgery to remove a cancerous tumour from his right lung. The pontiff has gradually been resuming activities since being discharged from hospital on 17 October.

Leo and Trump have clashed over the US president’s harsh immigration polices, with the pontiff describing them as “inhuman”.

Earlier this year, Trump lashed out in response to Leo’s criticisms of the US-Israeli war on Iran, calling the pontiff “weak on crime” and “terrible on foreign policy”. The US president also said that Leo had only been elected pope because Trump himself was in the White House.

In response to the tirade, Leo said he was “not afraid” of Trump.

In his first key address to the US on the eve of the 250th anniversary of the signing of the Declaration of Independence, Leo praised the country’s history of ⁠welcoming migrants, while urging Americans to live up to the ideals put forward in the ⁠declaration, in what was seen a further rebuke to Trump.

In an interview with the US broadcaster in late July, Leo expressed “great love” for his home country and its core principles of freedom, opportunity and welcoming immigrants but qualified that he just “happens to be American”.

Leo will travel to Latin America in November for an 11-day trip covering 10 cities. Archbishop Paul Richard Gallagher, the Holy See’s foreign minister, told reporters at Rome’s foreign press association earlier this week that a papal visit to the US was expected in the “not too distant future”, adding that the outcome of the midterms might influence whether the trip goes ahead in 2027 or later.
