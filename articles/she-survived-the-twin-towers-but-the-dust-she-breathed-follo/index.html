She Survived the Twin Towers But the Dust She Breathed Followed Her All the Way to the End | GreaterGood

Marcy Borders was 28 years old and barely a month into a new position as a legal assistant with Bank of America when the world changed. Time reports she was working on the 81st floor of the North Tower of the World Trade Center when American Airlines Flight 11 struck the building just a few stories above her office.

Her supervisor told her to stay at her desk. She didn't listen. Fox News reports that she fled the building and pushed out onto a street scattered with wounded workers.

Getting down was not simple. "My stairwell was badly damaged and we had to move stairwells," she later recalled, according to The Sun. "I was convinced we were going to die."

She made it to the bottom. Outside, the North Tower collapsed before her eyes. A stranger grabbed her and pulled her into a nearby building lobby, out of the path of the falling debris.

YouTube/Wiki4All

One Frame That Became Iconic

Inside that lobby, AFP photographer Stan Honda was sheltering from the same cloud of destruction. Al Jazeera published Honda's own account of the moment: a woman walked in, completely covered in dust, and he instinctively raised his camera.

He took just one frame. A police officer immediately directed survivors up a stairwell, and the moment was gone. VOA News reports that Honda described her as "nicely dressed for work" — a suit, now buried under a thick coat of grey ash.

That single photograph, distributed worldwide by Agence France-Presse, made Marcy Borders one of the most recognized faces of the entire tragedy. The nickname "The Dust Lady" followed her everywhere. NBC News notes that three of Honda's images from that day, including the portrait of Borders, now belong to the permanent collection of the National September 11 Memorial and Museum.

YouTube/Wiki4All

A Decade Lost to Trauma

The fame brought no comfort. Borders told the New York Post that the photograph itself became a source of terror — she feared the public attention would make her a target for another attack, according to TheGrio.

She stopped working entirely. She lost custody of her two children. Fox News reports she turned to crack cocaine, telling the Post: "I started smoking crack cocaine, because I didn't want to live."

The panic was constant. Every low-flying aircraft sent her spiraling. Every man on a rooftop convinced her a shooter was taking aim. Time recounts her telling the Post: "My life spiraled out of control. I didn't do a day's work in nearly 10 years."

YouTube/Wiki4All

The Road Back

In April 2011, Borders checked herself into a rehabilitation clinic. NBC News reports that while she was there, President Obama announced the death of Osama bin Laden — news that Borders described as deeply releasing. "The treatment got me sober, but bin Laden being killed was a bonus," she said.

She got her children back. She went back to work. She began telling people, "I try to take myself from being a victim to being a survivor now. I don't want to be a victim anymore," as All That's Interesting records.

The Dust Came Back

The recovery didn't last long enough. Borders was diagnosed with stomach cancer in August 2014. She believed the toxic ash she had inhaled at Ground Zero had caused it. CBS News quotes her asking aloud: "Did this thing ignite cancer cells in me? I definitely believe it, because I haven't had any illnesses."

She faced the diagnosis without health insurance and accrued more than $190,000 in medical bills, All That's Interesting reports. She was not alone in her suspicion about a link between Ground Zero exposure and cancer. By the time of her death, roughly 3,700 survivors and first responders near the site had been diagnosed with 9/11-related cancers.

Marcy Borders died on August 24, 2015. She was 42.

What She Left Behind

Her daughter Noelle was eight years old on the morning of the attacks. TheGrio spoke with Noelle, who said she plans to publish her mother's personal journals one day. "Where The Dust Lady stops, her daughter starts," she said.

Her son Zayden and daughter Noelle continue to carry her memory forward. NBC News confirmed her brother Michael's heartbroken Facebook post in the hours after her death: "I can't believe my sister is gone."

To the world, she was The Dust Lady. To her family, she was Marcy — a mother who fought her way back from the edge, only to face one battle she could not win. As her daughter put it: "Not only is she the Dust Lady, but she is my hero. And the dust has settled, and she is free now."
