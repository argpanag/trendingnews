Why your morning coffee is under threat from El Nino

TheTools · 18.09.2026 14:59 · source · permalink

Britain’s beloved morning coffee is at risk from an unprecedented oceanic heatwave caused by El Niño.

Global supplies of tropical commodities are likely to be interrupted by the phenomenon, pushing up prices for coffee in Britain this autumn.

El Niño is a powerful natural climate phenomenon that pushes up global temperatures.

The natural warming of sea temperatures is forecast to reach more than 3C in the coming months, making this year’s weather event the largest since the 19th century, the Met Office has said.

Other products likely to rise significantly in price include chocolate and olive oil, research from the Government Office for Science (GO-Science) suggests.

The growth and harvest of the affected products, which are primarily made in just a few countries with tropical climates, will be disproportionately affected by the weather pattern.

Some researchers have even suggested El Niño could cause food prices to double, although confidence estimates in the prediction are “low” with limited evidence, according to GO-Science.

The report says: “Secondary effects of El Niño on global food supply chains have the potential to reduce yields and drive up prices, particularly in regions with already low food security.

“The UK could experience higher prices for products such as rice, and certain tropical commodities (for example: cocoa, coffee) but the precise effects remain difficult to predict.”

Products with “localised production” are likely to be disproportionately affected by the phenomenon, the scientists warn.

The report added: “Shocks to global food systems can occur through: Impacts on commodities with localised production (such as olive oil, coffee, cocoa), where global supplies are highly concentrated in certain countries.”

Several key rice producers, including India, Thailand and Vietnam, are set to face yield-reducing effects from the weather brought by El Niño.

Prices could also be driven up by disruption to production of emulsifiers, which are used in baked goods, sweets and processed meats.

Half of the UK’s food is imported, including around 80 per cent of fruit and vegetables.

This puts Britain at disproportionate risk from more severe droughts in Spain and Morocco caused by the weather pattern.

Some foodstuffs could be boosted by the heatwave, including soy beans, if yields increase in South America. This could in turn have an impact on British dairy, pig and poultry farming, the report said.

The coffee sector has already experienced years of volatility, with prices climbing sharply for arabica and robusta varieties.

Giuseppe Lavazza, the chairman of Lavazza, told consumers in July that two years of good crops from Vietnam and Brazil would be needed to “ease the situation” for the coffee market. But both countries are bracing themselves for the effects of El Niño.

Earlier this month, Dame Angela Eagle, the Environment Secretary, advised the British public to stock up on food before the impacts of the phenomenon hit the UK.

She said this year’s El Niño would lead to wetter winters and more extreme storms in the UK, which has already experienced one of the driest summers on record.

Britain ‘must take pattern seriously’

Dame Angela told The Guardian: “We’re about to experience probably the largest El Niño that has ever been seen. If you’ve got a store of a bit of food that can keep you going for a while before the emergency services can get to you, you’re going to be a lot better off than if you haven’t.” 

Forecasters have also warned that the weather pattern, which can increase the risk of drought and flooding across the world, needs to be taken seriously in Britain.

The last significant El Niño was in 2015, when the UK was hit by a series of storms. That December, hundreds of flood warnings were issued as infrastructure in York and Leeds cracked under the pressure of flooding streets.

Thousands in York had to leave their homes when the Environment Agency was forced to open the city’s floodgates.

A Defra spokesman said: “There is currently no evidence that El Niño will lead to food supply issues for the UK.

“More widely, we are working with farmers and the food and drink industry to boost domestic production, making supply chains more resilient to future shocks.”
