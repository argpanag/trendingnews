Gemma Collins built a career on humiliation… her pity-party doc lays it on thick

TheTools · 17.09.2026 20:00 · source · permalink

Read on for Ally's thoughts on Saturday Night Live: UK’s 24 writers and the latest Big Brother rip-off

SKY One’s new Gemma Collins series got off to a bad start with the arrival of some hate mail which wasn’t wasting any time with introductions.

“Are you still here, c*** face?” it began, before ditching the pleasantries and really ­letting her know what they thought.

“But who?” Gemma understandably wanted to know, could send such foul and ­dangerous material.

Sky One’s Head of Factual Entertainment? The bloke round the back of Krispy Kreme Doughnuts, in Romford?

Essex County Council?

Answers came there none.

And we’re sure as hell not about to find out on Gemma Collins: Everything. All At Once, which is one of those eight-episode pity parties where everything that can go wrong does.

Her relationship with boyfriend Rami, her family’s health, the goat that head-butted her at the local petting zoo.

It’s all a disaster and couldn’t be anything else, truth be told, because if things didn’t go tits up, she’d have no show and no career.

Episode one of Everything. All At Once does at least start on an optimistic note, though.

Animal-loving Gemma heads off for a relatively successful spell on I’m A Celebrity: South Africa cheerfully claiming “I say to Rami every year, let’s go to Chiang Mai and work for the elephant sanctuary,” because it’s not like he hasn’t put in the ground work on that one. By the time she returns, however?

Catastrophe.

All the key components of her life seem to have taken a distinct turn for the worse.

Her redoubtable mum’s health has suffered and Rami, weak and foolish man that he is, has hit the bottle big time, which means the rest of the first two shows descend into one long drunken row with lots of recriminations.

It’s not helped, of course, by the fact that Gemma is so desperate to conceive his child that, at one point, in St Moritz, she bellows into the Alpine void “Mountains. PLEASE GIVE ME A BAY-BEE.” Nearly engulfing the entire valley with an avalanche in the process.

However much mindless abuse she suffers via hate mail, though, Gemma always remains her own worst enemy, forgetting to fast for eight hours, or even eight minutes, before the first phase of her IVF hormone treatment, and surrounding herself with ­hangers-on who tell her what she wants to hear rather than the cold, hard truth.

There’s almost every variety of paranormal vampire lurking around in the background here, including some regular fraud called “Psychic Emma,” who The GC has got on speed dial when she views a new property. “Am I going to get this house?

“Yes,” replies Psychic Emma, firmly, “You’re going to have the baby there.”

An episode and half later, Gemma’s still not pregnant and the move has fallen through without any reference to not-so Psychic Emma.

It was about this moment, though, when I remembered there are always three distinct phases to any TV programme featuring Gemma Collins.

The first bit, which normally involves a pratfall (see ­Dancing On Ice), lulls you into the false idea it could be entertaining.

The second is the dawning realisation her endless ego-mania and entitlement has become too much and it’s now boring.

But the darkest and most depressing phase of the lot is the third, when you realise there’s no heartache or ­personal tragedy Gemma wouldn’t try to monetise and turn into a professional ­pantomime.

It’s at times like this that I long for the black and white innocence of Seven Up, currently coming to a beautiful conclusion on ITV’s 70 Up.

For here we have a landmark documentary series which plunges you back into a reticent Britain, of the 1960s and 70s, where the word “over-sharing” hadn’t even been invented and any enquiry about your private life was met with a polite smile or “no comment.”

It means that when a personal tragedy does overwhelm any of the participants, the effect is far more moving and powerful than anything you’ll ever see on Everything. All At Once or practically any other TV show I’ve seen in the past 25 years.

For those who weren’t lucky enough to grow up in the old Britain, I cannot recommend 70 Up enough (ITV, Tuesday, 9pm).

Or will be, in about 18 months.

Random TV irritations

Saturday Night Live: UK’s 24 writers, who haven’t got a punchline between them. Channel 4 shooting fish in a barrel with its England ’Til I Die stitch up.

The right-on absurdity of Vigil’s lesbian crime-fighting duo – Starsky & Butch – trying to stop World War III breaking out in Svalbard.

And those sad-sack Remainers who’ve turned up at the Last Night Of The Proms, every year since the Brexit vote, waving EU flags. Their eternal tragedy being, not one person even liked the EU enough to turn up at Last Night Of The Proms, with its flag, before the Brexit vote.

C4 Need To Drop Big Bro

Channel 4 never really let go of Big Brother.

It simply altered the setting and changed the name of the show.

The latest version is called Apocalypse, and it’s yet another “unique social experiment” which seeks to discover how a random bunch of 14 Anglo/Australian dickwits would cope after civilisation and order has broken down.

Very badly, is the answer. Especially one titanic waste of space called “Big Stevie” who was, almost inevitably, the first of the “survivors” to use the makeshift toilet where he didn’t hold back with either the commentary or sound effects. “It’s happening.” (Splattering noises.) “IT’S HAPPENING.”

As ghastly and useless as they all are, though, the problem for Channel 4, especially for those of us who live in the London Borough of Tower Hamlets, is that their version of the apocalypse doesn’t look much worse than a lot of modern-day Britain.

Admittedly, the local supermarket, in the abandoned US town of Riverside, has been more efficiently ransacked than our local Sainsbury’s.

But there’s a lot less graffiti, dogsh*t and litter, and C4’s armageddon has apparently wiped out all the pro-Palestinian crazies, cyclists and the entire lanyard-wearing class as well.

So the voiceover guy needn’t sound quite so sombre when he asks if viewers are “interested in taking part in a potential new series of Apocalypse”.

Because I’ll happily take the whole of next August, with an option to buy the abandoned mental asylum, if the rest of them have eaten Big Stevie by then.

The EastEnders cull began, this week, when Zoe Slater became the first soap character ever to die from a sore head.

And lo, there was much wailing, gnashing of teeth and some very bad acting. Her mum Kat went all wobbly and shouty and Big Mo drew a sharp response from Jean when she said: “They should have taken me instead.”

“Now stop that immediately.”

No, she’s got a point. They really should. It’s a start, though.

Meanwhile, among the new Big Brother contestants arriving by chauffeur-driven car, on ITV2, we had: “Full time mad bitch” Yasmin, who’s so thick she got lost on the way in, a Mad Frankie Fraser/ Derek Acorah hybrid from Liverpool called Philip, and gobby Glaswegian barmaid Millie who made the most ill-advised claim of all.

“I’m going into this like Scotland went into the World Cup.” Dave, keep the engine running.
