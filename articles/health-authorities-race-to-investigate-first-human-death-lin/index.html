Health authorities race to investigate first human death linked to rare cattle virus

A French man has become the first person to contract Shamonda virus, a disease previously only found in animals and spread through midges.

The patient, who had a weakened immune system, has since died, according to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC).

Shamonda virus was first identified in Nigeria in the 1960s and has this year been detected in farm animals all across Europe.

It was confirmed to have spread to the British Isles in September, after being found in a cattle herd on the island of Jersey. There have also been cases in Ireland.

The French patient, who was immunocompromised, died earlier this month while infected with the virus, but it is unclear if the infection contributed to his death, the ECDC said in a statement.

Investigations into whether the virus caused or contributed to the patient's death and how the infection was acquired are ongoing.

"This is an important scientific finding, as it is the first reported human infection with Shamonda virus [..] at present, there is no evidence that Shamonda virus causes widespread human disease, although further investigation is needed to understand its ability to infect people", said Dr Ben Brennan, Group Leader and Senior Lecturer, MRC-University of Glasgow Centre for Virus Research, University of Glasgow.

There was "no health risk to the general public identified at this time", the French health and agriculture ministries said in a joint statement.

In animals, Shamonda virus attacks the central nervous system and can lead to severe inflammation and haemorrhage. It can also lead to miscarriages.

Affected horses have been reported to exhibit seizures, lethargy, fever, muscle twitching, and difficulty walking.

Authorities are also still trying to ascertain whether the man himself was infected by a biting midge – the route of transmission between animals – or if he could have been infected by close contact with livestock.

It is thought he was infected in eastern France, where the virus had been circulating in midges and animals earlier in the year.

"Despite decades of circulation of the Shamonda virus in Africa and Asia, no human cases had been documented until now. This is therefore an exceptional case occurring in a severely immunocompromised patient," French health authorities said in a statement.

The UK Plant and Health Agency said some of the neurological signs of the disease in horses resemble West Nile fever, human cases of which reached a record high on mainland Europe this year.
