ANTHONY IPPOLITO | Elbow Grease

TheTools · 21.11.2026 01:17 · source · permalink

In this year’s I Play Rocky, there’s a scene that shows the contemporary audience reacting to Rocky, the 1976 sports drama now considered one of the best films ever made. Despite near impossible odds to not only convince the studios to let the then unknown Sylvester Stallone play the lead role in his own script, but to get the film completed, the audience is captivated. They’re laughing, they’re wiping away tears, they’re beaming that particularly unguarded smile so unique to that of the cinemagoer.

Looking around at the audiences of I Play Rocky watching this tale unfold 50 years later, the crowds look the exact same.

The story of Stallone, from street sleeping in 70s New York City to writing and starring in a film that would earn 10 Academy Award nominations and notch three Academy Award wins, is stuff of legend. Tasked with retelling the story of Stallone’s grit, work ethic and uncompromising self belief is the utterly brilliant Anthony Ippolito.

“It’s like singing for a musician,” Ippolito quips, grinning, himself just a few years shy of the age Stallone was when he so famously birthed the American classic. I Play Rocky follows Ippolito-as-Stallone within what has become a great piece of Hollywood lore: a near-penniless actor in New York City writes an incredible screenplay and doesn’t budge until the Hollywood studios allow him to play the lead. It’s difficult to imagine that embodying Stallone is akin to performing to a musician. It feels more analogous to walking onstage at Carnegie Hall and conducting a symphonic orchestra to an audience of one, and it’s Beethoven.

Given the circumstances, Ippolito appears relatively unintimidated. “It’s that he’s an actor. You know, sometimes they do films about famous musicians or famous politicians or journalists,” he tells me. Playing one of the planet’s most famous actors, with the man himself still around? Tough.

Ippolito is not, however, new to the game. He’s been acting since he was a child. “Like AnnaSophia Robb,” he laughs of his co-star, who plays Stallone’s wife Sasha. He adds that Robb has been “an incredibly successful actor from a young age. I wasn’t killing it as much.” Now 26, he has accumulated roles befitting a man on the edge of industry greatness.

It’s been a steady but slow rise. He had a small voice part in an episode of children’s television show Go Diego Go! in 2010. In 2013, he had a minor role in The English Teacher; two years later, he worked alongside Adam Sandler as the younger version of his character in Pixels. “He was the sweetest man,” Ippolito says of the experience. “I was younger and nervous to be on any film set. I mean, at that age, you’re just nervous all the time.” It wasn’t until 2020 that Ippolito got his first huge break, in which he starred alongside Odessa A’zion in Netflix’s cult favorite teen show Grand Army. Finally, in 2022, Ippolito played none other than fellow Italian-American, Al Pacino, in The Offer.

Ippolito straddles that particular line between rising big-screen actor and zealous cinephile. Perhaps to exist in that duality—to exist in the fan versus creative didactic—is the most imperative mode of existence when embodying Pacino and Stallone. Still, even after being able to speak with both cinematic titans, “Just being able to have a conversation with either of those individuals is, you know, [mind] blowing to me,” Ippolito admits.

Ippolito’s Stallone is vulnerable yet swaggering, sweet behind the city kid snarl, and zealously determined, and while the tried and tested formula of the feel good biopic is neither new nor revolutionary, Ippolito is so captivating his portrayal has already garnered Oscars nomination buzz. In I Play Rocky, Ippolito steps into his full maturity, much of the pre-release excitement owed to his performance alone. His casting has rightly been hailed as outstanding: Ippolito is sincerely mesmerising in his portrayal of Stallone. The film was nominated for a People’s Choice Award at TIFF, has been submitted to the Golden Globes as a comedy, and won the Virginia Film Festival Audience awards.

I ask Ippolito how much he relates to Stallone, not just within the role, but at this particular moment in his life. “There’s a lot to relate to,” he says, thoughtfully. “I mean, he [has been] an actor since he was young…we [were both born] in New York, or at least spent a good amount of time there. He’s just someone who was very passionate about the arts, and I am as well. So it was easy to kind of relate on that level, for sure.”

When Ippolito first heard actors were being discussed to play Stallone, he studied the man’s movements and filmed tapes in his grandmother’s basement, unasked and unprompted, just to get the script for the audition.

There’s a direct parallel here: the underdog’s determination, rewarded with triumph. Is that not quite… meta? “It definitely kind of is,” he agrees, but he “wasn’t really thinking about that part of it as much, at the time.”

“I was like, ‘Damn, I hope this works.’ I mean, what’s interesting about it is the current day and age of acting for actors now,” he explains. “You send in tapes and tapes, and it feels like you’re sending into an abyss, right?” Right. “Even more so when there’s no one asking for any tape to be sent.”

Out nationwide in time for Thanksgiving weekend, both the film and especially Ippolito’s performance are already garnering the same Oscar attention that lavished nominations upon the original Rocky. Getting the role was difficult enough; preparing for it was another Herculean effort.

“This one was a lot more physical,” Ippolito says when I ask him to compare the prep to anything else in his career. Leaning back on his sofa, he tells me it required him to gain “a considerable amount of weight over the course of like a year and a half or so.” And also the shoot itself was incredibly physical. The running, one-handed push-ups, boxing… just a lot of stuff that I needed to do over the course of that shoot that tested me.”

And just how involved was Stallone himself? “Peripherally involved,” he replies. Stallone is “friends with the producer Toby Emmerich, and was in touch with [the director] Peter Farrelly, especially in the early stages.”

“And he was nice enough to speak to me about a month before we started shooting,” Ippolito says. “Give some insight into what that period of time was like for him. But past that, he wasn’t very involved. He had his own stuff, he’s spending time doing his show, spending time with his family. So I’m just happy he took enough time to talk to me.” I joke that I can’t imagine not wanting to be involved in every detail of a film made about me. “That would be another nerve-wracking thing, right?” he laughs. “Like, geez!”

Why does Ippolito think I Play Rocky is coming out now, as we return to that particular All-American narrative of the underdog? “You know, we’re in a very weird world right now,” Ippolito says. “I think it’s a great time to tell, like, a human story of somebody who was judged a lot for his outward appearance… ridiculed for a facial paralysis that he suffered through birth injury. I think there’s something very human, very needed right now.”

“A wholesome story with some optimism in it because it did work out for him,” he continues. “In that he wrote this script longhand with a Bic pen into a notebook. There’s something very tangible about that.”

It is evident that Ippolito holds great respect, and at times he even sounds awed, for the subject of his portrayal. “For [Stallone] specifically… he himself is a very unique, idiosyncratic individual. He wrote a film that kind of mirrors his own story, and now that film has become such a success. When you look back at the original, it’s even more compelling. Rocky was an underdog story. I think that reaches pretty much everybody. So, if you’re asking if it reached me, if I can relate to it in some way? Absolutely.”
