The $1,000 pen that could cost Trump the midterms

TheTools · 13.10.2026 15:42 · source · permalink

Story of how Ken Paxton pocketed another man’s property continues to haunt candidate in crucial Texas senate race

John Hancock used a quill to scrawl his oversized signature on the Declaration of Independence. Presidents from Barack Obama to Lyndon Johnson have handed out pens used to sign landmark legislation. More recently, Donald Trump derides his predecessor, Joe Biden, as an “autopen president”.

Now, a pen in Texas could make electoral history with some believing it may decide the fate of a crucial Senate race, causing Mr Trump to lose control of Congress.

In August 2012, Ken Paxton, this year’s Republican candidate for Senate, was passing through a security checkpoint at a county courthouse when he picked up a pen.

The incident would morph into an unlikely political scandal that still haunts Mr Paxton, the Texas attorney general.

The trouble is that it wasn’t just any pen: it was a $1,000 Montblanc pen. And it didn’t belong to Mr Paxton: it was owned by Joe Joplin, who told The Telegraph it had been a Christmas gift from his wife.

Mr Joplin, who had been in court for a probate hearing, searched for his missing pen without success. After pondering over a cigarette, he contacted Terry Box, the county sheriff, whom he had come to know following a burglary at his house the same year.

“You won’t believe who had it,” Mr Joplin recalled the sheriff telling him, following a brief investigation. “It was an elected official.”

It was, in fact, Mr Paxton. When media got hold of the grainy CCTV footage two years later, it showed Mr Paxton bending down, picking up an object and walking off. There was the then state senator with his tie done up to his collar, a briefcase in one hand – and another man’s $1,000 pen in his pocket.

Mr Paxton quickly returned the pen, and his team has consistently maintained it was an innocent misunderstanding. When media reports first surfaced, a spokesman said it was “shuffled between two attorneys in a courthouse”, calling the story “far from newsworthy”.

At the time, Mr Joplin declined to speculate on how Mr Paxton had ended up with his pen and was “very grateful” to have it returned. Now aged 80 and retired from his legal practice, he is more talkative.

A friendly Texan with an easy manner, he burst into gales of laughter at the suggestion Mr Paxton had mistaken the pen – a handsome instrument with a snowcap finial – for his own. “This one was silver, and it was easy to spot,” he said.

Tale that won’t die

To the frustration of the Paxton team, the story has refused to die. Partly, it’s because of the comedy value – the state’s top law enforcement official stealing a pen – but also because Mr Paxton’s opponents keep hammering him on it.

The first to exploit the pen saga was Justin Nelson, a Democrat who ran against Mr Paxton for Texas attorney general in 2018. His advert featured jaunty piano music – suggestive of a vaudeville farce, beneath the dignity of a senior politician – which played alongside the security footage.

“Vote Justin Nelson for DC attorney general,” the narrator intones. “He will fight on behalf of all Texans. And he won’t steal your pen.”

Then in 2025, and again early this year, John Cornyn, the incumbent Texas senator, hammered Mr Paxton on the pen and a host of other scandals as he unsuccessfully sought to fend off a primary challenge from the conservative firebrand.

The pen was used as one of the “symbols of Ken Paxton’s depravity” in an advert that reminded viewers of the Ten Commandments: “Thou shalt not steal.”

“It’s a memorable, anecdotal story that has taken on a life of its own,” said Matt Mackowiak, a veteran Texas political operative who worked on the Cornyn campaign. “I think for people who know about it, they think it says something about Ken’s character that’s very deep and undeniable.”

Months later, James Talarico, the Democrat running against Mr Paxton and favoured to beat him in several recent polls, ran a version of Mr Nelson’s advert. It had one final twist of the knife, however: it included a clip of Mr Joplin, posing with his famous pen and endorsing Mr Talarico.

By any measure, taking a pen, even a $1,000 Montblanc one, should be the least consequential of any of Mr Paxton’s scandals over the years.

He has been accused of corruption and mortgage fraud and is only the third politician in Texas’s history to be impeached by its House of Representatives. His wife is divorcing him for adultery – or, as she put it, on “biblical grounds”.

For his part, Mr Paxton has consistently denied any wrongdoing, and he was eventually acquitted of all articles of impeachment in September 2023 following a trial by the Texas Senate. He has attributed the end of his 38-year marriage to the strains of holding office. He has never directly addressed the infidelity allegations.

But unlike corruption, which is complex, or marriages – which can be messy – some view taking someone else’s pen as a clear-cut case of right and wrong – and it helps when there’s CCTV footage they can endlessly replay online and on TV.

Rival campaigns use the incident as a shorthand for greed, opportunism and pettiness. They cannot beat Mr Paxton, a darling of the Maga movement, on pure politics in deep-red Texas, but he appears vulnerable on the question of character.

Mr Mackowiak believes that is now the deciding question of the election. When voters go to the polls in November, around 85 per cent of voters will be “rendering a judgement on Ken Paxton”, he believes.

The Paxton camp previously maintained, in an interview with The Telegraph, that their man had a “Teflon” quality and could defy his many scandals. The Cornyn campaign, by contrast, believed most voters weren’t aware of the sleaze surrounding the attorney general, and they could bring him down by saturating the airwaves with attack adverts.

A recent poll from The New York Times gives Mr Talarico a six-point lead in the race: a staggering margin, considering Mr Trump won Texas by more than 13 points in 2024.

It suggests Mr Cornyn’s team was right – albeit too late to save their candidate – and that the combination of scandal and attacks may have fatally wounded the attorney general.

If Texas falls and elects a Democrat to statewide office for the first time since 1993, Mr Trump will almost certainly lose control of the Senate.

“I’m glad that Talarico’s doing real well,” said Mr Joplin, who added that Mr Paxton was “never very credible” as a candidate and owed his success to Texas’s strong base of conservative politics.

Nowadays, he keeps his $1,000 pen safely in a drawer at home. “It still carries political significance,” he said, laughing again. “It’s crazy.”
