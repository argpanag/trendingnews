Shell found between two ancient Aussie volcano lakes provides key to understanding weather

TheTools · 23.09.2026 11:09 · source · permalink

Scientists have discovered a link between the growth of snails and extreme weather events.

Scientists have discovered an incredible tool for understanding the weather between two lakes created by a volcanic eruption 600,000 years ago.

The University of Queensland-led team selected the 26-hectare Coalstoun Lakes National Park, 230km northwest of Brisbane, as their study area because of three key reasons:

  1. It has a unique microclimate.

  2. It could be examined in detail by satellite.

  3. It's home to a large, native snail.

Honorary Professor Jamie Shulmeister explained that the volcanic lakes were "not connected to the rest of the country outside" and so these "closed areas" were the perfect study site.

The team focused their efforts on the 4.5-year life of a snail from the national park, and found a correlation between past weather events and growth. The snail is believed to have died in mid-August, 2018.

"This was an amazingly detailed study of one snail shell," Shulmeister told Yahoo News.

How changes in snails were linked to weather events

Using satellite imagery, the team studied when the lakes were dry and when they were full after heavy rain.

They then mapped every millimetre of the snail's shell using the expertise of the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation (ANSTO).

A single Biggenden banded snail found at the Coalstoun Lakes National Park was the focus of the study.

After comparing rainfall with the rings in snail shells from the area, they found a direct correlation between rain and growth.

In particular, there were spikes after Severe Tropical Cyclone Marcia in 2015 and Tropical Cyclone Debbie in 2017.

"The lakes were full immediately after the two cyclones came through, and that's when the snail was growing like mad, building its shell," Shulmeister said.

How snails could help predict future weather events

The researchers used the shell of a single deceased Biggenden banded snail. 

But they would expect to find a similar connection between growth and rain in almost any land snail species.

By studying older, or even fossilised, shells, the team expects to reconstruct past extreme weather events.

"Temperature is quite easy for us to be able to reconstruct from a variety of records, but to be actually able to say, a cyclone went through an area at a time, is really unusual," Shulmeister said.

Researchers Dr Nicholas Patton, Professor Melanie Leng and Honorary Professor Jamie Shulmeister at the study site (left to right).

"So this is a great way of looking at a very detailed record of local rainfall in an area."

Understanding the past is an important tool for predicting future weather events.

The Millennium Drought from 1996 to 2012 is considered the worst of its kind in living memory, but its possible historic events were even worse.

"Maybe in the record there are even centennial-scale droughts that you could say have pretty much gone on forever," Shulmeister said.

"And by looking at the snail records, we'll be able to say tropical cyclones were passing through once every two or three years, or no, at this time it was dry all the time."

The research was completed with Dr Quan Hua from ANSTO and Professor Melanie Leng of the British Geological Survey and the University of Nottingham, and published in the journal Holocene.
