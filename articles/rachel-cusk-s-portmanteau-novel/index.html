Rachel Cusk's Portmanteau novel

TheTools · 02.09.2026 04:50 · source · permalink

Life of M, a rumoured “biography” of a certain celebrity, is more artful than the gossip surrounding it suggests

By Lola Seaton

Rachel Cusk’s new novel, Life of M – a fictional portrait of a film star – could be seen as a professionalisation of the approach Cusk developed in her celebrated Outline trilogy (2014-18). In those novels, a self-effacing Cusk-like narrator impassively relays the personal histories volunteered by strangers and acquaintances. Thanks to her posture of “absolute passivity”, these reported soliloquies seem naturalistic, which they are not: people don’t generally unfold their life stories with mesmeric eloquence – nor thread them with the loosely thematic, abstract distillations typical of Cuskian paraphrase (“By failing he created loss, and loss was the threshold to freedom”). Not all of this lands but the gnomic compression is enjoyably stretching. The most implausible monologues occur in the final volume, Kudos, during which the narrator gives interviews in which she hardly speaks. The far-fetched reversal makes you realise that the narrator resembles an interviewer herself, whose seeming passivity is the source of her power – the power to make “recordings”, as Cusk puts it in Life of M.

The seemingly haphazard portraiture that composed the Outline books became more systematic in Cusk’s previous novel, Parade (2024), a linked series of fictionalised biographies of real artists, interwoven with scenes from the narrator’s life. The artists are all designated by the initial “G”, as though case studies of the artist-function. Life of M similarly cuts between scenes from M’s life and scenes from the narrator’s – though “scene” puts it too dynamically. The book opens with the narrator proposing to write M’s “autobiography” but what follows is a disconnected series of tableaux.
