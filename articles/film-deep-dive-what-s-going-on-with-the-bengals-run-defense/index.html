Film deep dive: What’s going on with the Bengals run defense

TheTools · 13.10.2026 18:52 · source · permalink

The Cincinnati Bengals’ run defense is in a chess match against the offenses that the Bengals are facing, and it all revolves around Dexter Lawrence.

The Bengals, looking to limit explosive passes and attempting to encourage teams to run the ball at Lawrence, have the second-highest light box rate (percentage of plays with six-or-fewer players in the box) in the NFL. Over the last three weeks, since the Pittsburgh Steelers showed the benefits of attacking the perimeter of the Bengals’ defense in the run game, opposing offensive coordinators have been entering matchups against the Bengals with specific horizontal runs in the plan that stretch the field and try to keep Lawrence out of the play.

In short, offenses are running away from No. 97.

“That’s the story,” Zac Taylor said.

This year, the Bengals’ defense has allowed the most 10+ yard runs in the NFL (21). The Dolphins’ two biggest runs on Sunday were a crack toss and a reverse to a wide receiver.

“They’re going to try to do all of that stuff,” Taylor said. “All of the things you deal with when you’re going up against a really good front that we’ve had to do on the offensive side of the ball. It’s nice to see other teams having to do that. It’s not just the easy traditional runs that are there. You have to be creative.”

The runs are really challenging the Bengals’ secondary. Going through each of the 21 10+yard runs allowed, the biggest trend is the frequency of which the Bengals’ cornerbacks and safeties are failing to knock down runs on the perimeter. What should be a five-or-so yard gain on paper turns into a crushing explosive play allowed. The Steelers and Jaguars beat the Bengals because of these plays, and the Dolphins recorded too many of them on Sunday even as the Bengals won the game, 31-24. DJ Turner, DJ Ivey, Dax Hill and Jordan Battle have all had their moments under the microscope.

“Our guys will continue to see that and recognized this is how we’re getting attacked, these are the splits that are letting us know what’s coming at us,” Taylor said. “We’ve got really smart guys up front who do a good job of communicating that. It’s starting to filter out to the whole. We’ll do a better job knocking some of those schemed up plays down.”

It’s impossible to understate the difference that Lawrence is making. Last year, the Bengals had the worst run stuff rate in the entire NFL at 10.2%. This year, with Lawrence on the team, they have the best run stuff rate in the NFL at 23%. He’s making plenty of plays himself, and he’s also setting up chances for the linebackers to get downhill and make plays in the backfield. The Bengals are accomplishing these negative plays without aggressive blitzes: Bryan Cook, Jordan Battle, Jalen Davis and Dax Hill have one combined TFL this season.

When the Bengals are in run-stopping mode, they’re very good. Just three explosive runs have been on plays allowed with a neutral or stacked box (seven+ players in the box) — two of those three were a result of a missed tackle, and the third was a well-schemed end around by the opponent.

There are positives with the Bengals’ run defense, but the negative plays allowed will be the No. 1 thing that the Bengals need to clean up coming out of the bye and leading up to a game against the Baltimore Ravens.

Going through the plays, some trends emerge.

  1. The Bengals’ secondary needs to be much better against the run, especially on crack tosses and reverses. Teams will pick on Bengals’ DBs in run defense like you might in pass coverage
  2. It’s a new ballgame when Lawrence is on the sideline, and the Run D scheme when Lawrence isn’t on the field is a specific thing that needs attention.
  3. The second-team defensive line has to be more disciplined in scramble situations
  4. Injuries play a role (banged-up safeties allowed three explosive runs in Week 3, and tackling with a patchwork safety room due to injuries hurt the Bengals in Week 4)

The 21 explosive runs allowed can be broken down into five categories: Inside runs with Lawrence on the field, outside runs with Lawrence on the field, inside runs without Lawrence on the field, outside runs without Lawrence on the field and quarterback scrambles.

Let’s go through each of those sections.

  1. Inside runs with Lawrence on the field of 10+ yards: four plays for 65 yards

Three of these four rushes can be explained away, and each of those three plays happened last week in Miami.

-A scheme shocker: Sometimes, you tip your cap. On third-and-10, the Bengals played their very light dime defense in an obvious passing situation with Boye Mafe at DT and DJ Ivey at LB. The Dolphins stunned the Bengals with a run, and it worked.

-Short-yardage aggression: On second-and-3 in the third quarter with the Dolphins on the fringe of the red zone, Barrett Carter got aggressive in a situation where you want to be aggressive. He went for an aggressive run blitz, nearly got the running back in the backfield on what would have been a drive-killing TFL. He just missed — it wasn’t even an alarming missed tackle — and the Dolphins still only picked up 10 yards on the play.

-Lawrence beat by the heat: On that same drive in the third quarter, Lawrence got blocked to the ground by the center in a one-on-one matchup. In was his seventh snap in the first seven minutes of the third quarter on a record-setting hot day.

There was one real problem play in this grouping of plays, and it should have been a run play that the Bengals blew up.

Not ready to bounce: Against the Jaguars, the Bengals stuffed what was going to be a run behind the right tackle that went nowhere. Kris Jenkins and Cashius Howell both had chances to make plays in the backfield but didn’t finish. That allowed Bhayshul Tuten to stop, collect himself and pivot toward the sideline. The problem was that Dax Hill, playing outside corner, crashed to the interior run gap and didn’t maintain leverage outside. Hill got behind the play, Tuten ran around the perimeter and all of a sudden Hill was splaying catch-up as Tuten gained 20 yards. Even though this technically counts as an inside run, it could qualify as an outside run.

The verdict: A rock solid segment of the Bengals’ run defense. This is what you pay Lawrence for.

  1. Inside runs without Lawrence on the field of 10+ yards: Two runs for 41 yards.

Similarly, these two runs can be explained away.

-Hurt safety can’t get there: The Steelers went right to an inside run in the fourth quarter in Week 3 when they saw Lawrence was off the field on Pittsburgh’s final drive of the game. There was open space up the middle for what should have been a five-to-10 yard gain. Had Kyle Dugger been healthy, he would have been able to break up the field and tackle Jaylen Warren in front of the first down line. But Dugger had a quad injury that has now kept him out of two straight games, and he stumbled as Warren broke off a big run

-Situational acceptance: With just over two minutes left in the Dolphins game, with a 13-point lead and with Shemar Stewart at defensive tackle in a light DL grouping, it’s not a killer to allow a 15-yard run that gets wrapped up at the 24-yard line.

The verdict: Better than I thought. You’d think the interior run defense without Lawrence would be more of an issue, but it’s not an area that has really hurt the Bengals this season. Still, especially with how often the Bengals play with light boxes, you wonder if teams try to exploit this more going forward. Between Week 3 and Week 5, I counted five runs of 5-to-9 yards on plays between the tackles by teams against the Bengals on plays where Lawrence wasn’t on the field.

  1. Outside runs with Lawrence in the game: Four runs for 78 yards.

Here’s where the defensive backs get exposed.

-Week 1 communication: On what turned into a 10-yard touchdown for the Bucs, DJ Turner and Bryan Cook each occupy the same run blocker as Bucky Irving runs toward an uncovered perimeter on what looked like a busted play. Also, Jalen Davis selling out on an inside run blitz didn’t help as the play unfolded.

-Insult, then injury: TJ Slaton looked misaligned on a toss by the Steelers, and Warren literally bounced off of him before hitting an uncovered run gap. Cook, who injured his ankle at the start of that game, had to run upfield to take away the first down run. But when a banged-up Cook missed his tackle, Warren sprinted for 35 yards. Safety Jordan Battle also got stuck in the wash on the play as Warren ran past him.

-Situational bounce: On a 1st and 20 play for the Jaguars, the Bengals played a safe two-high defense to take away the deep ball. It looked like the Jaguars caught them off guard with a run. Dax Hill, back in the slot for this game, got stuck on a run block. Tuten had an open lane to bounce the run outside of the tackle. PJ Jules was playing very deep down the field, so he had a lot of ground to cover before he made the stop on a 15-yard gain.

-Crack toss: Taylor said on Monday that the Bengals should have done a better job on this play in Sunday’s win over the Dolphins. First, a piece of credit to Miami for a really cool wrinkle: Because of wide receiver Malik Washington’s versatility, they put him at running back on the play and as a result got the Bengals to play nickel defense against a two tight-end set (the Dolphins had three receivers and two tight ends on the field). The personnel grouping gave the Dolphins a hat-on-hat advantage. Ivey got way too low as the running back ran past him to get into open space. Battle was playing very deep zone coverage on the snap. By the time he got to the ballcarrier, the Dolphins had an 18-yard gain. Dax Hill and Barrett Carter also got lost in the wash.

Bonus: The called-back 87-yard run by the Steelers (Metcalf holding). The play didn’t count due to the penalty, but it still had some issues. On this toss play, Warren read what he was seeing up the middle before bouncing his run all the way out wide around the right tackle. Missed tackles by Carter, Knight and a banged-up Dugger hurt the Bengals, and Battle crashed too hard to the line of scrimmage and ended several steps behind Warren in a footrace.

The verdict: Teams are going to keep running tosses, end arounds and runs designed to bounce toward the sideline when Lawrence is in the game. It’s something for the Bengals to work on. The defensive backs need to tackle better and take better angles. The Bengals will also have a decision to make when Jalen Davis is eligible to return from IR in Week 7. Do they put him back in the lineup, prioritize his tackling abilities out of the slot and shift Dax Hill back out wide, or do they stick with Ivey/Tacario Davis out wide? Do you prioritize run D or pass D at corner? Also, are there ways to be aggressive with Cook to get the Bengals’ best tackler in a better position to make plays against outside runs?

Outside runs without Lawrence in the game: Four runs for 77 yards

The spotlight remains on the secondary

-A DJ Turner tackle: A receiver end around from the Texans in Week 2 should have been stopped, but Jaylin Noel ran through Turner to pick up 11 extra yards on the play because of how long it took Turner to wrap up his tackle.

-Another injury situation from Week 3: Cook looked slower here as he battled the ankle injury and was run around for the first down.

-Backups get beat: Against the Jaguars in Week 4, PJ Jules ran downhill to try to lay a hit on Chris Rodriguez, but a last-second block from receiver Brian Thomas Jr. knocked him off of his spot. An unblocked Ivey had a bad missed tackle on the play, diving for Rodriguez’s ankles but coming up short.

-Stuck on reverse: Battle’s angle looked too narrow as he tried to get downhill and make the play, and Chris Bell ended up running further toward the sideline to break down the field. Most importantly on the play, Cook had an extremely rare missed tackle in space to allow a five-yard run turn into a 26-yard gain. Cook never misses tackles, but this one hurt the Bengals.

The verdict: Interesting that both of the explosive wide receiver carries allowed by the defense came with Lawrence off the field. Again, these plays highlight the importance of the secondary finishing tackles. Cook and Battle getting healthier on the bye week should really help.

Quarterback scrambles: Three runs that matter for 36 yards.

Four of these can be explained away due to the situation (you’ll allow a QB run over the middle of the field in the final seconds of a half with no timeouts) or how the game was called (Lawrence was blatantly held, but that not being called allowed Malik Willis to run for 39 yards on a play on Sunday).

The three notable scrambles each happened on first/second down on plays where the backup defensive line is on the field. When the Bengals have needed eyes on the QB on third down, they’ve typically done a good job. Also, when Lawrence is on the field, he plays a big part in limiting the scramble game.

Here are the three real negative scrambles allowed.

-Stroud reads game: When Slaton and Jenkins tried to execute a pass rush game, they collided and ended up in the same gap rushing over the same interior lineman. That allowed a wide open lane for Stroud to run through. The Bengals’ execution needed to be better here.

-Just one snap: Stewart played one meaningful snap against the Texans in Week 2. He did a poor job of maintaining the integrity of the pocket as he looped inside, lost the ball and then perplexingly turned his back on the QB. Stroud ran around where Stewart was supposed to be and picked up the first down.

-Good design, bad luck: On an under-center, no-play action play from Miami that saw the Dolphins move the pocket, the Bengals ended up with their pass rush attacking the exact part of the pocket that Miami was ready for on the play. The lone pass rusher in the mix was Cashius Howell, who used his standout spin move. But unfortunately for the Bengals, that split-second where Howell lost sight of the QB gave Willis the room he needed to run.

The verdict: How to handle scrambling quarterbacks will be a critical area for young guys like Howell, Jenkins and Stewart to hone in on leading up to a matchup against Lamar Jackson after the bye week.
