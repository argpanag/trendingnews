SMSFs risk losing tax-free status through pension mishandling - SMSF Adviser

TheTools · 16.11.2026 18:36 · source · permalink

Dawson said it’s important to know that death in itself won’t be a CGT event, but often it will bring about the sale of assets to pay out a lump sum or to release cash.

 “Sometimes, there are lump sums because no one was eligible to take the death benefits in the form of a pension, or perhaps they just didn’t have enough cap space,” Dawson said.

“But if the fund that is paying a retirement phase pension, sometimes the impact on the fund’s assessable income will be impacted because of that tax exemption.”

Dawson explained, for a pensioner who dies after they have reached the age of 65, it is important to look at what accounts may be operating, such as a pension account, and then determine what happens to that account upon death.

“If you worked out that the pension automatically reverted because that was part of the terms and conditions of the pension, or you have a BDBN that had the impact of making it automatically revert, then for super law, you’ve still got the same pension. You’ve just got a new owner, so you’re essentially just crossing out the original pension of one partner/spouse and putting it on the other,” she said.

“The pension itself continues for super law, and essentially, the reversionary pensioner will take over needing to draw the minimum pension.  On the tax side, we get exempt current pension income when we’re paying a retirement phase pension, so if the client already had a retirement phase pension when they were alive that ECPI just bubbles along and continues.”

Dawson added that if a client was drawing a transition to retirement income stream when they passed away, the TRIS phase would not have attracted ECPI when the member was alive.

“However, on their death, when it continues to the reversionary, death is obviously a compulsory cashing event, and the death would cause those benefits to become unrestricted, non-preserved as well,” she said.

“That will also get ECPI turned on at the time of death. Either way, when the pension reverts, you’ll normally process that in the fund to reflect that.”

She warned, however, that it is a different situation if the pension did not automatically revert, not because of the tax treatment, but from the perspective of super law.

 “On the super law side, because the original pension doesn’t automatically continue on death, that’s where we should see all the pension payments turned off. The direct debits are turned off, and the account keeps going. It’s still going to attract earnings but there’s no more pension to be paid,” she said.

“But on the tax side of things, it looks really different. It might surprise you, but the pension account itself continues to attract ECPI. So, from the date of death until you actually cash the death benefit money outside of the fund, whether or not you pay it as a lump sum, or if you use it to start a new pension, until you deal with it, it will still attract ECPI.”

Dawson said this is one of the reasons it is advisable to tell clients not to roll the pension back into accumulation on the date of death.

“Even though for super law the pension has stopped and there’s no more entitlements to pay pension payments, it’s best to leave the pension account intact in your software because you want your software to do the work and to keep allowing for that ECPI,” she said.

“For example, if there was a couple, and the husband outlived the wife and he had a single pension account in the fund, when he dies, that fund still keeps getting ECPI. Even if you sold all of the fund’s investments, triggered heaps of capital gains, and then paid out lump sums to the estate, it’s all still ECPI because the fund was all in the pension phase at the time he passed away.”
