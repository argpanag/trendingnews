Opinion: The U.S. media is getting soft on Trump in this trade war

TheTools · 09.09.2026 19:18 · source · permalink

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer walks past members of the media after doing a television interview outside the White House in Washington, Aug. 26.Jacquelyn Martin/The Associated Press

Does each side have a legitimate case to make? Watching American media coverage of the trade war with Canada, particularly of late, and particularly in television interviews, you’d get the impression that there might be justification for the attack-tariffs U.S. President Donald Trump has been bludgeoning Canada with for nearly two years.

This is what happens when the media engages in false equivalence, sometimes as a result of the pursuit of so-called “objectivity.” They present both sides of an argument as having equal weight, even though one side might have very little weight at all.

Some mainstream U.S. media outlets are not doing this. They are reporting the story fairly. But much of the coverage – and not just from the Trump genuflectors at Fox News – is giving Americans the wrong impression. They have suggested that it’s a fair fight, overlooking the core fact that this war was unilaterally started by the Trump White House – that it was the predator President’s war of choice, an economic version of his war on Iran or his buddy Vladimir Putin’s war on Ukraine.

Forgotten in a lot of the coverage is that he had signed the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement, a trade deal with Canada that he repeatedly professed to love. But upon being re-elected, he began claiming, in a staggering act of hypocrisy, that Canada has been ripping the U.S. off for years, and he’s been employing this as the rationale for his tariff assaults.

The Trump administration officials who have been sent out to condemn Canada’s case are not being confronted with this slightly inconvenient fact, along with so many others. The framing is that the U.S. is going about the business of correcting trade imbalances. For the most part, they’re getting kid-gloves media treatment, as opposed to guilty-party treatment.

U.S. trade representative Jamieson Greer and others are using the argument that Canada turned down a great deal in the most recent negotiation, one better than other American trading partners were getting. Instead of being ravaged with 50-per-cent tariffs on steel and aluminum, for example, we might have been able to get a 25-per-cent rate. We should have been grateful. We were only getting the less awful deal shoved down our throats.

It’s not often mentioned any more that to impose his tariffs, Mr. Trump has deployed justifications that have either been judged by the courts to be illegal, or are incredibly far-fetched. For recent tariffs, he’s weaponized section 338 of the nearly century-old Smoot-Hawley Act. No president has ever used it before, but as per Mr. Trump’s edicts on Tuesday, it is now providing the rationale for banning Canadian products, because it says products can be banned if a country fails to change “its discriminatory conduct.” Which country, one might ask, has been engaged in discriminatory conduct?

The U.S. side has some legitimate beefs, such as the way Ottawa has gone about interpreting the USMCA on the dairy trade. But there is nothing that merits what Prime Minister Mark Carney calls being “attacked.” Mr. Greer argues that Mr. Trump’s tariff actions are aimed at addressing the problems brought on by globalization, including job loss in American manufacturing. That’s an interesting argument. But does fixing the globalization problem include name-claiming a Great Lake and 51st-state taunts, amid other attempts at economic subjugation?

According to the polls, Canada has been winning the public relations battle on the tariff war in the U.S. so far. But it’s not enough to deter Mr. Trump from further expanding the conflict. He has sufficient support for his campaign from Congressional Republicans and majority support from his MAGA base. He has backing from his right-wing media echo chamber, and has been given an easy ride from other outlets that are intimidated by him or are simply poorly versed on the Canadian trade story and won’t take him on.

For Canada’s part, we’re not doing enough to make our case south of the border. A full-court press is needed and it is not happening. Dominic LeBlanc, the minister responsible for Canada-U.S. trade, has been doing a lot of interviews, but, though it is wise not to stoop to the level of insults coming from the Oval Office, he’s gone too far the other way. He’s too nice. He gives the U.S. media too little to work with. We need interlocutors who can convince Americans that there is only one guilty party in this war, since only Americans have the ability to change their government’s direction.

Ottawa should spend big on a lobbying and communications campaign to reverse the ugly perceptions Mr. Trump is creating in his country about our country. The American media is letting too much of his rubbish stand.
