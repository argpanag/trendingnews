How Team USA won Worlds before it started

TheTools · 27.09.2026 23:06 · source · permalink

Members Only

Numbers in the front group and an agreement to be selfless paid off in Montreal.

Matteo Jorgenson sat in pre-Worlds training camp across the border in Vermont and tapped away at a plan, the sort his team usually makes for him. Tactics, some analysis. An American winner was his goal, whether it was him or not. 

On an overcast, blustery Sunday in Montreal a few days later, his countrymen followed that plan nearly to the letter. Brandon McNulty became the first American to win the elite men's road race at the World Championships since Lance Armstrong in 1993. 

Jorgenson's summary of the plan is short enough to fit in a blast of race radio. Quinn Simmons would follow the big move. McNulty and Jorgenson would let others burn their matches chasing it. When it all came back together, one of them would go, hopefully alone. 

It’s rare for any plan to survive six and a quarter hours and 273.4 km of racing. This one did. 

This was one of the most open men’s Worlds road races in recent memory. Tadej Pogačar’s absence will do that; there were at least 15 riders with a valid claim at potential victory on Sunday morning, and three, maybe four, of them were from the US team. 

"On paper, we don't have like Del Toro, Seixas, such an outright favorite," Kevin Vermaerke said, sitting on a plastic chair in the US’s team paddock zone after his own work was done. As we spoke, there were 12.5 km left for McNulty; Vermaerke was glued to the TV, sitting quite literally on the edge of his seat, never taking his eyes off the screen as he talked through the team’s preparation. He spoke in between huge bites from a burrito. "The caliber of riders we have, Matteo, Quinn, and Brandon, are best in the world, you know? … The goal was to have the three of them in the final,” he said, watching exactly that. 

The team meeting set a specific target. "We set this goal of always having more than 10% in the lead group," said US team director Mike Creed, who also directed Ashlin Barry’s under-23 win earlier this week. "We're always more than 10% and we're going to win by numbers."

Step one: Follow the move

For much of the early goings, you’d barely have known the US was in the race. Mexico did work early; France took control, executing their obvious intention to make the race hard. Bruno Armirail rode a 60 km turn on the front for Seixas that probably deserves its own feature.

Vermaerke, his face still dusty from racing, explained why they stayed quiet: “The Montreal circuit does its own selecting, so there was no point spending bodies to control it,” he said. 

The Americans first showed themselves with a little under 90 km to go, when Larry Warbasse took up tempo to put Simmons in position ahead of the Camillien-Houde. On that lap, the race detonated. Key riders sought to anticipate, and get ahead of better climbers. Mathieu der Poel (Netherlands) went, Wout van Aert (Belgium) countered, and a lead group of six formed. Van der Poel, Van Aert, Oscar Onley (Great Britain), Primož Roglič (Slovenia), Anthon Charmig (Denmark) and Simmons.
