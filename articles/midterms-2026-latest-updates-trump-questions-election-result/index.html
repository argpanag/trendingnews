As Republicans continue to reckon with a historic rout, President Trump is now suggesting without evidence — as he did in 2020 — that voting machines were to blame for a bruising defeat.

Members of Congress returned to Capitol Hill Monday for the start of a lame-duck session after Democrats completed one of the most lopsided midterm election victories in modern U.S. history to gain control of the House and Senate, leaving their Republican rivals to reckon with a decisive rout.

In the Senate, Democrats flipped seven seats and now hold 54 to the Republican Party's 44. Two seats, in nail-biting contests in Alaska and Mississippi, remain undeclared. In Mississippi, where Democrat Scott Colom leads Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 2,500 votes, absentee ballots must arrive by Tuesday and counties must certify results by Friday.

In the House, Democrats held 254 seats to the GOP's 163 as of midday Monday, comfortably passing the majority threshold of 218, with 18 races yet to be called, according to the Associated Press. The latest estimates show that the party is on track to win its largest majority since 2008. And there are still seats it could flip, including Colorado's Fourth District, where Republican Rep. Lauren Boebert, a staunch Trump ally, trails Democrat Eileen Laubacher by about 3,200 votes with 97% of the vote counted.

At the White House, President Trump signaled he would work with Democrats during his remaining two years in office but said he wouldn't hesitate to use his veto power. Trump also acknowledged that the Iran war contributed to Republican losses, but defended his decision to pursue the conflict.  Vice President JD Vance, though, blamed the scale of GOP losses squarely on the war with Iran. "The real reason we lost is obvious to almost everyone," he wrote in a post on X. "The reason we lost is the war."

Meanwhile, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is facing intense political backlash over the conflict. On Monday, Rep. Thomas Massie, an outgoing Kentucky Republican, introduced articles of impeachment against Hegseth, and a vote could come as early as this week. But Trump continues to express confidence in Hegseth and insists he is not considering replacing his Pentagon chief at least not yet.

On election night, as Democrats were on the brink of gaining control of Congress, Trump told the New York Times that he was "absolutely willing" to accept the results.

But as Republicans grappled with the fallout from their bruising defeat, Trump reversed course, publicly questioning the legitimacy of the outcome while suggesting without evidence, as he did in 2020, that the voting machines were rigged.

      Trump questions election results, revives voting-machine conspiracy

      On election night, as Democrats were on the brink of gaining control of both the House and Senate in a historic rout, President Trump told the New York Times that he was "absolutely willing" to accept the results.

      Nearly a week later, as Republicans grappled with the fallout from their bruising defeat, Trump reversed course, publicly questioning the legitimacy of the outcome while suggesting without evidence, as he did in 2020, that the voting machines were rigged.

      "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," Trump wrote in a post on Truth Social on Monday. "How could the Polls be so wrong??? Some early voting 'dumps' were so large that it was impossible to win, no matter how many people went to vote. It's all about the machines, which are far more important than the voter!"

      The comments were reminiscent of those Trump made after his loss to Joe Biden in the 2020 presidential election, when Trump claimed that he only lost as a result of widespread ballot fraud. Courts rejected dozens of legal challenges to the results. Trump's false claims helped fuel the Jan. 6, 2021, attack on the U.S. Capitol by a mob of his supporters.

      The voting machine conspiracy, in particular, was at the center of a billion-dollar defamation lawsuit later brought by Dominion Voting Systems against Fox News, which repeatedly broadcast the claims. Fox settled with Dominion Voting Systems for $787.5 million just before the case was set to go to trial.

      Where things stand Saturday in the uncalled Senate, House and governor’s races

      Four days after Election Day, several key races remain too close to call. Democrats hold 54 Senate seats to Republicans' 44 and 254 House seats to the GOP's 162, with 19 House races still uncalled, according to the Associated Press.

      Mississippi: Democrat Scott Colom leads Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,574 votes, 49.1% to 48.8%, with 97% of the vote counted, according to the AP. Secretary of State Michael Watson said 3,139 mail ballots were still outstanding as of Friday. Those ballots count if they arrive by Nov. 10 and were postmarked by Election Day. Election officials are also still reviewing affidavit ballots, which are cast by voters whose eligibility must be confirmed. State law does not provide for a recount in this race. "We are not backing down," Hyde-Smith's campaign said Friday. "We will see this through to the very end!"

      Alaska: Democrat Mary Peltola leads Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan by 637 votes with 74% of the vote counted, according to the AP, whose count has not been updated since Wednesday morning. Tens of thousands of early, absentee and rural ballots are still to be counted, the Anchorage Daily News reported. If neither candidate tops 50%, ranked choice tabulation begins Nov. 18.

      Colorado: Democrat Eileen Laubacher leads Republican Rep. Lauren Boebert by 3,172 votes in the Fourth Congressional District, 48.8% to 47.9%, with 97% of the vote counted, according to the AP. Counties have until Nov. 13 to finish counting.

      Kansas governor: Democrat Cindy Holscher leads Republican Ty Masterson by just over 7,000 votes, KCTV reported. The margin triggered a state-required hand-count audit. Holscher has declared victory, but Masterson has not conceded. "Every Kansan deserves to know the result is right," he said.

      Voters approved nearly all ballot measures aimed at limiting transgender rights

      Five states considered ballot measures that would place new limits on transgender rights during this year's midterms. All but one of those measures passed. 

      Four states — Washington, Nebraska, Arizona and Colorado — voted in favor of banning trans athletes from participating on sports teams that match their gender identity. 

      There are some important differences from state to state, though. The proposals in Washington and Nebraska only apply to girls' and women's teams. Colorado's and Arizona's apply to both male and female teams. 

      Washington's measure also includes a stipulation that requires all K-12 students who want to play girls' or women's sports — whether they identify as trans or not — to have their sex verified by a doctor. Arizona's measure will ban schools from allowing trans minors or adults to access bathrooms, locker rooms or other private spaces designated for a sex that doesn't match the one they were assigned at birth. 

      Colorado voters also approved a separate measure that will ban gender-affirming surgeries for minors. 

      The only state where potential new restrictions on transgender rights failed was Missouri, where voters rejected a combined proposal that would have banned most types of gender-affirming care and repealed abortion rights in the state.

Colorado counties have until Nov. 13 to count ballots and upload final results. Right now, Laubacher's lead is too big to trigger an automatic recount.

      Houston mayor says police will limit contact with ICE after Texas Gov. Greg Abbott's defeat: 'Elections matter'

      The Democratic mayor of Houston announced on Friday that police in the nation's fourth-largest city would limit their cooperation with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement during traffic stops after Republican Gov. Greg Abbott, who imposed the statewide policy, lost his bid for reelection to Democrat Gina Hinojosa earlier this week.

      Mixed results for abortion-related ballot measures across the country

      The Supreme Court's 2022 decision to overturn the nationwide abortion protections established by Roe v. Wade instantly turned abortion access into a state-by-state issue. It also led to a flurry of ballot measures across the country in which voters were asked to decide whether their states should protect or restrict abortion rights. In the years since, voters have generally come down in favor of protecting abortion rights.

      That trend got another test this year due to abortion-related measures in four states. 

      In Idaho, voters rejected a measure that would have overturned their state's strict abortion restrictions and established a right to reproductive freedom and privacy, including abortion until fetal viability. The initiative's failure means Idaho will not join the ranks of red states where voters used ballot measures to expand abortion rights. 

      One of those red states is Missouri, where voters approved a measure in 2024 that struck down some of the nation's most restrictive abortion laws. On Tuesday, voters rejected a Republican-backed measure that would have repealed that 2024 amendment and allowed strict abortion restrictions to be put back into place. That same failed measure would also have created new limits on gender-affirming care in the state. 

      Voters in two other states that already have relatively permissive laws around abortion access  — Nevada and Virginia — approved measures that will enshrine abortion rights in their state constitutions, a step that will make it much harder to restrict access in the future.

    Five states considered ballot measures that would place new limits on transgender rights during this year's midterms. All but one of those measures passed. 

    Four states — Washington, Nebraska, Arizona and Colorado — voted in favor of banning trans athletes from participating on sports teams that match their gender identity. 

    There are some important differences from state to state, though. The proposals in Washington and Nebraska only apply to girls' and women's teams. Colorado's and Arizona's apply to both male and female teams. 

    Washington's measure also includes a stipulation that requires all K-12 students who want to play girls' or women's sports — whether they identify as trans or not — to have their sex verified by a doctor. Arizona's measure will ban schools from allowing trans minors or adults to access bathrooms, locker rooms or other private spaces designated for a sex that doesn't match the one they were assigned at birth. 

    Colorado voters also approved a separate measure that will ban gender-affirming surgeries for minors. 

    The only state where potential new restrictions on transgender rights failed was Missouri, where voters rejected a combined proposal that would have banned most types of gender-affirming care and repealed abortion rights in the state.

    The Supreme Court's 2022 decision to overturn the nationwide abortion protections established by Roe v. Wade instantly turned abortion access into a state-by-state issue. It also led to a flurry of ballot measures across the country in which voters were asked to decide whether their states should protect or restrict abortion rights. In the years since, voters have generally come down in favor of protecting abortion rights.

    That trend got another test this year due to abortion-related measures in four states. 

    In Idaho, voters rejected a measure that would have overturned their state's strict abortion restrictions and established a right to reproductive freedom and privacy, including abortion until fetal viability. The initiative's failure means Idaho will not join the ranks of red states where voters used ballot measures to expand abortion rights. 

    One of those red states is Missouri, where voters approved a measure in 2024 that struck down some of the nation's most restrictive abortion laws. On Tuesday, voters rejected a Republican-backed measure that would have repealed that 2024 amendment and allowed strict abortion restrictions to be put back into place. That same failed measure would also have created new limits on gender-affirming care in the state. 

    Voters in two other states that already have relatively permissive laws around abortion access  — Nevada and Virginia — approved measures that will enshrine abortion rights in their state constitutions, a step that will make it much harder to restrict access in the future.
