Way of the Warrior Kid — McG [Review] | In Review Online

TheTools · 20.11.2026 14:15 · source · permalink

McG has never been a particularly fashionable director. Even in the early message boards surrounding “vulgar auteurism” in the late 2000s, where action directors like Paul W.S. Anderson and Tony Scott were suddenly uplifted to underground stardom, his Terminator: Salvation was never seriously discussed as an underappreciated classic, but more as an object for further study. Its glossy Playstation 2-esque exteriors, like the chassis of an overpolished vintage chevy, straddled the line between boring blockbuster sheen and digital grotesquerie. His battle choreography held strong to the Michael Bay school of thought, but always with an appreciable distance, and a genuine interest in those surreal metallic surfaces only the early-aughts blockbuster could really boast. His 2017 film The Babysitter, while still south of a “good horror film,” is one of the more notably acerbic popular releases of this century.

Way of the Warrior Kid is also a hateful film, though in a far less entertaining mode than McG’s previous work. The cynicism is apparent in the most banal blurb: Chris Pratt plays a Navy SEAL, sent stateside after a mission gone wrong, where he stays with his sister (Linda Cardenelli) and her son Marc. Marc is the target of vicious bullying at school and feels completely unprepared to enter high school next year, so Jake, in automatic Karate Kid pastiche, vows to teach him how to fight. These glorified theatrical recruitment videos are ubiquitous in the direct-to-streaming markets these days, commissioned by a laundry list of ex-military producers and executives, usually starring politically dubious or perpetually unemployed stars, made for an audience that seemingly does not exist. However, met with the right amount of technical bravura, one might still find some pleasure in the grift, in films like Joseph Kosinski’s Top Gun: Maverick.

Unfortunately, Way of the Warrior Kid appears to take no pleasure at all, cynicism or not. Pratt plods along, his already threadbare character executed with paper doll depth. Here he is fully incapable of even suggesting an emotion, and instead spends the movie wearing a one-note, plastered-on frown. Similarly rote, McG’s repeat cinematographer Shane Hurlbut is operating in unforgivably automatic territory. The ecstatic, corrosive images of Terminator: Salvation are nowhere to be found, and in their place is the same vacuous clarity as any other direct-to-streaming actioner: bleak, two-dimensional landscapes of suburban monochrome, inflected with the occasional day-glo sunsets of late-’80s blockbusters sprinkled in.

But while no one really acquits themselves well here, at the fore of the blame is McG, who not only appears completely anonymous during dramatic sequences, but sadly also in any of the action set pieces. There is no physicality or kineticism, and indeed barely any movement at all in a film where sequences rarely diversify further than a shot-reverse shot for its climactic fight scenes. The Rocky-esque training montage, which makes up the majority of the film’s drama, is absurdly undramatic, while grueling drills and repetitious workout routines drag on for minutes at a time with little-to-no reprieve, like an especially sadistic Samuel Beckett play. One feels no real authoring hand in these sequences, not even a cynical one. It would be just as well if there were no one behind the camera at all.

McG has been in-and-out of Hollywood for two decades now, almost exclusively helming projects of similar, if not lower, caliber than Way of the Warrior Kid with twice as much ingenuity. In the scheme of things, a failed film is just a drop in the pond. But the totality of the failure here, the lack of even a glimmer of workmanlike prowess, blockbuster ingenuity, and an eye for action from a filmmaker who seemed to revel in it a short time ago, is worrying to say the least, on top of being an interminable viewing experience.

DIRECTOR: McG;  CAST: Chris Pratt, Jude Hill, Linda Cardellini, Levi McConaughey, Ava Torres;  DISTRIBUTOR: Apple TV;  STREAMING: November 20;  RUNTIME: 1 hr. 46 min.
