El Niño vs. Polar Vortex: A winter weather showdown

TheTools · 07.11.2026 09:47 · source · permalink

With temperatures still reaching double digits in early November, it might be tempting to think winter is taking a year off in southern Manitoba. And according to Environment and Climate Change Canada Senior Climatologist David Phillips, there is reason to expect a milder-than-normal winter.

But before putting away the snow shovels for good, Phillips has a reminder: winter is not cancelled.

Two major weather players, an unusually strong El Niño and a potentially disruptive polar vortex, are expected to influence the months ahead. While El Niño is likely to be the dominant force, the polar vortex could still deliver some memorable reminders of just how cold Manitoba winters can get.

In fact, Phillips describes the coming winter as something of a weather performance, with the two systems taking turns on centre stage.

And if the current weather is any indication, southern Manitoba is already seeing El Niño make its presence felt.

El Niño is already making its mark

El Niño is a natural climate pattern that begins thousands of kilometres away from Manitoba, in the Pacific Ocean near Ecuador and Peru, explains Phillips. It develops when ocean waters become warmer than normal and remain that way for an extended period, affecting the atmosphere above them.

For an El Niño to be officially declared, ocean temperatures must reach at least half a degree above normal and maintain that warmth for three months. This year's event has gone well beyond that threshold.

"Right now, the waters are just like hot tubs out there," Phillips says. "Instead of half a degree warmer than normal, they're approaching four degrees warmer than normal. I've never seen it this warm. Nobody has, in the history of observing these things."

Turbocharged El Niño?

Those exceptionally warm waters influence the atmosphere and change the position and speed of the jet stream, which helps determine weather patterns around the world.

Phillips says this particular El Niño has been almost turbocharged, contributing to drought in some parts of the world and flooding in others. The tropical storm activity has also followed the pattern typically associated with El Niño, with fewer storms in the Atlantic and considerably more in the Pacific.

In Canada, a strong El Niño is generally associated with a milder-than-normal winter. And southern Manitoba is already showing signs of that influence.

A November that feels more like September

Phillips points to the unusually warm conditions across Steinbach, Winkler and Portage during the fall where temperatures have recently been running approximately 10 to 12 degrees above normal.

"Highs of 14 degrees. I mean, this would be what you'd expect maybe Labour Day, not here at the end of the first week of November."

For Steinbach residents, Saturday's forecast high is around 13 degrees. The record high for November 7 is 16.5 degrees, established in 1999.

Phillips says climatologists look at previous weather patterns to help determine what might happen next. When they find past situations that resemble current conditions, they use those as analogues to help develop their forecasts.

"November is like on fire"

Looking back over approximately 80 years of Manitoba weather records, he says the second- and third-warmest falls on record occurred during strong El Niño events.

Steinbach has also experienced temperatures approximately three to four degrees above normal through September and October, with November continuing that trend.

"So you go to the bank on it," Phillips says. "The fact that you're getting this fall, that temperatures, for example, in Steinbach have been almost three to four degrees warmer than normal in September, October, and now November is like on fire."

What a previous El Niño winter looked like

One of the more recent examples of a strong El Niño was the winter of 2023-24. Phillips says it provides a useful indication of what southern Manitoba could experience this year. In Winkler, average temperatures during December, January and February were almost six to seven degrees warmer than normal.

Across Steinbach, Winkler and Portage, November through April was warmer than normal, with March being the exception.

One way to measure the severity of a Manitoba winter is to count how many days temperatures fall below minus 20 degrees.

Phillips says in a typical winter, Winkler experiences approximately 40 days below that mark. During the winter of 2023-24, there were just 14. Portage normally sees about 42 such days, but recorded only 15. Steinbach typically experiences approximately 50, but had just 18.

The even colder days were virtually nonexistent.

Phillips says southern Manitoba normally experiences somewhere between 10 and 15 days below minus 30 during a winter. During that previous strong El Niño winter, there wasn't a single one.

"It was just balmy and open."

A milder winter can bring some welcome benefits. There may be less snow to shovel, plow and push, easier winter driving, fewer concerns about heavy snow loads on roofs and lower home heating costs. Phillips estimates homeowners could save between 10 and 15 per cent on heating bills during a milder winter.

Winter recreation, however, might take a hit.

There is also another side to having less snow and colder weather.

Phillips explains lower snowfall can mean less moisture replenishing reservoirs and dugouts, adding that could leave farmers hoping for additional moisture through spring and early summer storms. A dry winter can also contribute to an earlier forest fire season and increase the risk of drought.

As Phillips points out, the winter that people are celebrating today could be the weather they are cursing a few months later.

Enter the polar vortex

While El Niño is associated with warmer conditions, another weather phenomenon has been getting considerable attention: the polar vortex.

Phillips describes it as the elephant in the room when discussing a potentially mild winter.

"All of a sudden, we start hearing about this villain called the polar vortex," he says. "I think it's kind of a Hollywood blockbuster. You've got the Godzilla of El Niño's going to fight it out with the dreaded polar vortex."

Unlike El Niño, the polar vortex is not a new phenomenon. It is a naturally occurring area of extremely cold air that sits high above the North Pole in the stratosphere, approximately 10 to 50 kilometres above the Earth's surface.

"Poking the sleeping bear"

Phillips compares it to a spinning top that normally stays in place, circulating cold air around the polar region.

When it remains strong and stable, that cold air stays largely where it belongs. But when the vortex becomes disturbed, it can stretch, change shape and shift southward, bringing colder temperatures and potentially blizzard conditions with it.

"It's like poking the sleeping bear," Phillips explains. "Then it begins to kind of wake up and begins to move and it takes its winds and its cold with it."

Scientists have already been watching signs that the polar vortex is becoming disturbed, possibly influenced by El Niño.

What's unusual is the timing. Phillips says these disturbances are more commonly associated with January and February, rather than late October and early November.

That doesn't necessarily mean southern Manitoba is in for a prolonged stretch of extreme cold. Rather, the polar vortex could make occasional appearances throughout an otherwise milder winter.

A weather ballet

So how do El Niño and the polar vortex work together?

Phillips suggests thinking of them as two different influences on the weather, rather than two systems engaged in an all-out battle. El Niño helps establish the overall personality of the winter, while the polar vortex can influence the mood on any given day.

He uses a personality comparison to explain the difference. El Niño is like a person's general character, while the polar vortex is more like waking up in a different mood one morning.

"You wake up in the morning and your mood is different," he says. "You're not necessarily going to attack the world like you normally would do. You're just going to roll over in bed and sleep another hour or two."

In other words, a mild winter overall can still have some very cold days. He describes a winter that could alternate between periods of bitter cold and stretches of sunshine and surprisingly comfortable temperatures.

There may be days when Manitobans are bundled up in parkas and balaclavas, followed by periods when a lighter jacket or even short sleeves are enough, he explains.

Phillips expects El Niño to remain the dominant influence, while the polar vortex could create shorter-lived cold spells lasting perhaps a day or two, or occasionally up to a week.

Winter is not cancelled

Despite the prospect of a milder winter, Phillips stresses that Manitobans should not become complacent. A strong El Niño does not eliminate the possibility of extreme cold, snowstorms or dangerous wind chills.

"A drier and milder winter doesn't mean a total absence of those frigid moments, those blizzard moments, which are legendary in southern Manitoba."

He expects there will be something for everyone this winter, including people who enjoy the traditional cold and snow.

And while a mild winter might sound appealing, there can be consequences when the weather doesn't follow its usual seasonal patterns.

Warmer conditions may allow certain insects, pests and diseases to survive that might otherwise be affected by a colder winter, Phillips points out. Less moisture can also create challenges for agriculture, while changing weather patterns can bring unexpected flooding or drought concerns.

Phillips says the climate has become increasingly unpredictable, with records being broken and traditional weather expectations not always holding true. The best scenario, he adds, would be a return to normal weather, without too many surprises or challenges. But that isn't necessarily what southern Manitoba should expect this winter.

His advice is to keep an eye on the sky and pay attention to the forecasts, recognizing that conditions could change considerably from one week to the next. 

For now, southern Manitoba can enjoy the November sunshine and unusually warm temperatures. Just don't put away the winter boots.

As Phillips points out, even with El Niño calling the shots, the polar vortex could still have a few surprises up its sleeve.
