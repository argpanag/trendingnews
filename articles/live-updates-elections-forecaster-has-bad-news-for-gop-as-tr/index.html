Trump says he told DOJ to file Alaska Senate lawsuit

President Donald Trump said Friday he told the Department of Justice to preemptively file a lawsuit seeking to block certification of Alaska’s US Senate election if Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan loses.

“Well, I’m the one that said ‘do it,’” Trump told reporters in Florida when asked about the lawsuit.

The lawsuit, filed on Friday, alleges that a candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan could confuse voters. It asks a court to potentially delay the certification of the election if the incumbent is defeated.

The race is a critical contest that could decide the balance of power in the Senate. The GOP incumbent is also facing Democratic former Rep. Mary Peltola. Democrats need to net four seats in November, and Alaska is one of several GOP-held states they’re hoping to flip.

“They have two guys running, Dan Sullivan,” Trump said. “All of a sudden, he decides to run for the Senate, and his name is Dan Sullivan. One is Daniel S., one is Daniel J. Daniel J. is the fake.”

Daniel J. Sullivan is a registered Republican, but state election officials listed him on the ballot without a party affiliation. Sen. Sullivan is labeled on the ballot as “Registered Republican” and “incumbent.”

Son of Darline Graham's GOP primary rival signals he'll vote for her Democratic opponent

The son of Republican Rep. Ralph Norman of South Carolina said he will not be voting for Sen. Darline Graham for US Senate.

Warren Norman stopped short of endorsing, but said he’d likely vote for, Democrat Annie Andrews. The pediatrician has been gaining ground in the typically reliably Republican state, forcing GOP groups to spend millions shoring up Graham’s campaign.

The younger Norman said his decision was “not personal” but added “there is no true conservative to vote for in this election.”

Norman’s father lost a bruising special GOP runoff election to Graham in August. He said he will vote for Graham and support the GOP ticket, but in a podcast interview this week he added that his family would not vote for Graham.

The lingering discontent from the special primary led some supporters of losing GOP candidates to start the “Write-In Ralph” campaign, encouraging Republican voters to write in Rep. Ralph Norman’s name, instead of voting for Graham in the general election. Norman is not affiliated with it but his wife is a member of the group’s Facebook page.

Andrews wrote on social media she is “grateful to have the support of the Norman family.”

Ramaswamy says he "respectfully" disagrees with GOP governor's criticism of incendiary ad

Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy rejected GOP criticism of his controversial ad featuring his opponent Amy Acton’s estranged mother, telling reporters Friday at a campaign event he “respectfully” disagreed with Gov. Mike DeWine’s calls for him to take it down.

CNN and other reporters scrambled to get Ramaswamy to ask questions as he got on a GOP bus bound that is traveling across the state today in the home stretch of the midterm campaign.

“Our ad had nothing, nothing to do with childhood abuse, sexual abuse. We condemn that as forcefully as anyone,” he said. “Our ad was about a consistent track record of Amy Acton telling lies, including during this campaign, and I do think that there has been very little to no coverage of the lies she has told about me, about my family, about my mother.”

The ad features Acton’s mother calling her a liar. Acton’s mother lost custody of Acton and her brother when Acton was 12 years old. Her stepfather was indicted by a grand jury on charges of sexually abusing her.

When pushed by CNN on if he doubts Acton’s story that she was abused by her stepfather, Ramaswamy deflected, noting he had never met or spoken to Acton’s mother and repeating his previous attack on Acton’s credibility.

“I think that in the standard of political advertising that they have used and that the media regularly accepts, I think it is absolutely acceptable for voters to hear and make a decision for themselves,” he said.

How Abdul El-Sayed has been responding to attacks on his Muslim faith

President Donald Trump once again attacked Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed’s Muslim faith this morning and repeated false claims about the Democrat’s ties and loyalties.

Trump plans to vote early in Florida on Saturday

President Donald Trump said Friday he plans to vote early in Florida on Saturday.

“I think we’re voting tomorrow,” he told reporters as he left the White House for a rally in the Sunshine State.

A White House official confirmed Trump and first lady Melania Trump will vote early in Palm Beach County on Saturday.

The president, who has widely criticized mail-in voting, recently encouraged voters to cast their ballots using “every appropriate tool.”

“If your state has early voting, do not wait. Go out and vote,” Trump said in a video posted to Truth Social earlier this month. “Vote early, vote by mail, or vote in person on Election Day.”

Election Day is November 3, but more than 21 million ballots have already been cast across the country, as of Friday, according to data compiled by CNN, the Associated Press and the data firm Catalist.

Trump again defends incendiary ad in Ohio governor's race, regardless of abuse allegations

Reporters on Friday repeatedly pressed President Donald Trump on whether he was still supportive of an ad in the Ohio governor’s race which featured the estranged mother of Democratic nominee Amy Acton, who has said she was sexually abused by her mother’s husband.

And Trump repeatedly defended it – no matter the abuse.

“No, it’s a mother speaking badly against Amy Acton,” Trump said. “To me, it’s very easy. The mother – if it were my mother speaking badly about me, you’d be making it the number one story.”

GOP nominee Vivek Ramaswamy’s campaign has said it is putting $4 million into the ad, in which Acton’s mother calls her daughter a liar and urges voters not to support her. Acton has said she has not seen her mother since she was about 12 years old, when she says the man her mother married sexually abused her. He faced criminal charges and was “declared a fugitive,” Acton has said, and her mother lost custody of Acton and her brother.

That context has sparked many Republicans to condemn it. But when reporters pressed Trump on the mother staying married to the man charged with abusing her daughter, Trump responded: “You don’t know that. You don’t know anything.”

“Her mother stood by, didn’t stand by — her mother went on television and said that the daughter who’s running for governor of Ohio is a liar and a bad person, and that’s all I know,” Trump said.

“I don’t know anything about stepfathers from many, many years ago. I know that recently a mother got on television and said that her daughter should not be governor of Ohio,” Trump continued. “This has nothing to do with a stepfather or anything else.”

DOJ sues to block Alaska Senate results if GOP incumbent loses in race with 2 Dan Sullivans

The Trump administration has preemptively filed a lawsuit seeking to block certification of Alaska’s US Senate election if the Republican incumbent Sen. Dan Sullivan loses in the state, which could decide the balance of power in the Senate.

The lawsuit represents the latest attempt of the Trump Justice Department to insert itself in the administration of elections, running up against the minimal role the Constitution gives the Executive Branch to unilaterally get involved in running the vote. It alleges that the presence of a candidate named Daniel J. Sullivan could confuse voters and wants a court to potentially delay the certification of the election if Sen. Sullivan is defeated next month.

The lawsuit is based on a “strange and unusual” legal theory, said Derek Muller, an election law professor at Notre Dame Law School. It’s also unclear, he said, on a practical level, how a court could even intervene in the way the Justice Department proposes.

The battle for control of the Senate could come down to Alaska, where there has been little reliable polling but where Dan S. Sullivan, the incumbent senator, and Democrat Mary Peltola have been in a closely contested race. Democrats need to net four seats in November to win the Senate, and Alaska is one of several Republican-held states that Democrats are hoping to flip.

New polls confirm GOP gerrymander in Texas might not help much

Texas Republicans kicked off the redistricting wars last year by brazenly trying to redraw the state’s congressional map so they could win five new seats and, hopefully, keep their House majority.

New polling confirms they might regret how they went about it — by assuming they could keep the Latino voters that Trump won over in the 2024 election.

New polls from the Washington Post and the Schar School at George Mason University in two of the five districts show Republicans struggling to win them. In Texas’s 34th District, the GOP trails Democratic Rep. Vicente Gonzalez among likely voters, 50%-43%. The 35th District is a virtual tie, with Republican Carlos De La Cruz at 48% and Democrat Johnny Garcia at 47%.

Both districts were redrawn to be ones Trump would have won by 10 points in 2024. But they’re also heavily Hispanic, and polls have long shown Democrats swinging hard against Trump.

The polls show that, in both districts, at least 55% of likely voters said Trump’s deportations of undocumented immigrants had gone “too far.”

The latter result — in the 35th Congressional District — is particularly remarkable. Democrats hadn’t invested much in the district, but it’s looking more like a toss-up now.

Of the other three districts Republicans targeted, they’ve already pulled ad funding from Democratic Rep. Henry Cuellar’s 28th Congressional District, apparently assuming a loss. And forecasters say Texas’ 9th District, which was drawn to favor Trump by 20 points, could be competitive.

The GOP appears to be on the most solid footing in the 32nd District. But it’s possible they could gain only one to three seats from their gambit.

Apparent AI-generated ad depicts Democratic House candidate dancing with drag queens

Republicans in North Carolina appear to be using artificial intelligence to lean into the culture wars — publishing an ad depicting a fictitious scene of a Democratic House candidate barn-dancing with drag queens.

The ad, which was paid for by the National Republican Congressional Committee and approved by North Carolina state Sen. Jennifer Balkcom, the Republican nominee for the state’s 11th Congressional District, shows Democratic candidate Jamie Ager dancing with drag queens in a barn as children look on.

The ad seems to reference a Breitbart report about an event at Ager’s farm seven years ago, and its tagline riffs off a phrase that was ultimately successfully deployed against Kamala Harris during her 2024 presidential race: “Jamie Ager talks like you and me, but he’s dancing with they/them.”

The new Republican ad also repurposes images from an Ager ad that depicted people barn dancing but embellishes it with drag queens and a pride flag.

In a statement, Ager’s campaign manager Grayson Barnette said Ager’s opponent was attacking him with “100% fake, AI-generated ads.”

Balkcom and Ager are competing to replace Republican Rep. Chuck Edwards, who dropped his reelection bid after an ethics panel released a report finding “substantial evidence” the congressman repeatedly made unwanted advances toward two female staffers. The congressman denied that his behavior was “intended as a sexual or romantic advance,” according to the report.

Democrats have said they are confident they’ll win the district owing to a last minute boost of enthusiasm.

Michigan and Ohio gas prices spike as cost-of-living worries remain top of mind for voters

Refinery trouble continues to cause stunning increases in gasoline prices ahead of the midterm elections in Michigan, Ohio and neighboring states.

The average price of gas in Michigan surged by another 16 cents on Friday to $4.77 a gallon, according to AAA. That means Michigan drivers have experienced a rare 33-cent increase in the span of just two days.

Gas prices spiked even more in Ohio, where the state average has surged by 39 cents over that span to $4.37 a gallon, according to AAA.

Voters have expressed frustration with high energy costs and rising living expenses as they weigh their ballots ahead of Election Day.

Other states in the region have also suffered significant increases over the past two days: Illinois (16 cents), Wisconsin (17 cents) and Indiana (18 cents).

Analysts have pointed the finger at an ExxonMobil refinery in Joliet, Illinois. The facility has experienced an unscheduled outage at one of its gasoline-producing units, according to Andy Lipow, president of Lipow Oil Associates.

Exxon confirmed the ongoing problem in a statement on Thursday but did not provide details on what happened nor when it is expected to get fixed.

Gregory Brew, senior analyst at Eurasia Group, said on X that the refinery trouble at Joliet shows why it’s “dangerous to skip maintenance season and run refineries at 97%” of their capacity for months on end.

Cook Political Report moves 19 House races in Democrats' favor

Elections forecasting agency The Cook Political Report moved 19 House races toward the Democrats in an update this morning that said the chances of a Republican House majority “have all but evaporated.”

The elections forecaster said Democrats are now expected to flip between 12 and 22 seats in the House.

“Several of these ratings changes — particularly in Texas and North Carolina — are tied to Republicans’ underperformance at the top of the ticket and the resulting delta between Republican and Democratic voter enthusiasm statewide,” The Cook Political Report said.

The forecaster moved six races from “toss up” to “lean Democrat” and two races from “lean Republican” to “toss up.”

Some young men are moving away from Trump and Republicans

Polls show a stunning shift happening with young men, who were a key part of President Trump’s coalition in 2024, ahead of the November midterm elections. CNN's Steve Contorno speaks with young men about the issues driving the shift in Michigan, where a critical Senate race is playing out.

Nearly twice as many men under 30 say they’re more likely to vote for a Democratic candidate than a Republican in November, according to a recent Pew Research poll of registered voters, a stunning shift after a roughly even split their vote in 2024.

To understand the political whiplash, CNN spoke with two dozen young men around Lansing, Michigan, where a Big Ten campus, the state capital and two General Motors plants put college students, young professionals and blue-collar workers into close proximity. The region has drawn intense attention from statewide candidates for governor and Senate and is home to a highly competitive House race.

Their frustrations ranged from the war with Iran to Trump’s embrace of artificial intelligence and to the proliferation of data centers. Some remain ambivalent to politics; a few weren’t ready to give Democrats a pass. But nearly all described mounting anxiety about the cost of getting by and fears that the American dream was moving out of reach.
