Can Canadian agriculture expect anything from the federal investment summit? - Manitoba Co-operator

TheTools · 12.09.2026 04:32 · source · permalink

Ag isn’t likely to headline the Toronto investment summit, but it could still benefit from spending on infrastructure and other sectors

By Jonah Grignon

Reporter

4 hours ago

7 min read

Agriculture does not appear to be top priority at the federal government’s upcoming investment summit, but the sector could still reap dividends.

“It doesn’t sound like agriculture will be on the agenda,” said Tyler McCann, managing director of the Canadian Agri-Food Policy Institute.

“There are some events happening on the sidelines of it where agriculture and food will be more prominent and will be discussed.”

The summit runs September 14 to 15 in Toronto with aim of accelerating the government’s plan mobilize $1 trillion in investments over the next five years.

Why It Matters

The agriculture and agri-food sector may need significant support and infrastructure investment as the effects of Canada’s trade war with the U.S. unfold.

Agriculture isn’t one of the events’ headliners. A recent report from RBC ahead of the summit identified five key sectors the government could capitalize on. Agriculture didn’t make the list — nor did it see much mention in the report.

Reporting from the Globe and Mail also suggests there are no direct investments in agriculture proposed for the summit, though it does mention projects with downstream potential like an expansion to the Port of Churchill.

Beef cattle

Serge Buy of the Agri-Food Innovation Council (AIC) said he is confident agriculture will attract investment despite the seeming lack of attention.

“You can have data centers, you can have natural resources, you can have AI up to the yahoos,” Buy said. “That’s all good. You still need to feed people.”

Buy said he has been encouraged recently by government officials recognizing agriculture’s importance, including Dominic Barton, chair of Invest in Canada, a federal agency that promotes foreign direct investment in Canada.

“If you would have talked to me about three months ago, I would have been a little bit less excited,” he said. “I do think that recent gestures and recent entreaties by the government have indicated more openness in terms of agriculture and food.”

Canada has talked plenty about opening new markets abroad, but Buy said it won’t be able to access those markets without investing in infrastructure. If the government takes those steps now, it could pave the way for diversified trade.

“Are we at the top of everybody’s playlist?” Said Buy. “Probably not. Are we close second or third? I think we are in some people’s playlist.”

“The proof will be in the pudding, but I wouldn’t be surprised if some of the announcements do include agriculture and food,” he continued. “It will be a lot of infrastructure but look to other potential projects in terms of potential of the creation of large greenhouses.”

Disconnect between large and small

Investments in agriculture could help the rest of the economy, but investments elsewhere could also help ag said J.P. Gervais, executive vice-president of ag production at Farm Credit Canada.

“Infrastructure matters not only for ag, but it matters for the entire (economy),” he said. “For example, investment in defense has been proven to trickle down to different sectors, right? If you think of data imagery, those are type of technologies that are connected to ag.”

“I think the first step is to bring awareness, or bring ag and food into the portfolio of those in that investable universe.”

McCann said one of the current challenges for the agriculture sector in pursuing investments is the disconnect between larger and smaller projects.

There is a preference for smaller projects, both among politicians and within the agriculture and food sector, McCann said. They tend to be prioritized ahead of the hundreds of millions or billions of dollars needed for projects like food processing facilities.

These large investments happen, they have been scarce, he said.

“There’s a principle that what we should be trying to do in agriculture and food is take small companies, support their investments to become medium-sized companies, take medium-sized companies, support their investments to become large companies, and take large companies and support their investments to become global multinational leaders.”

“We don’t do big well,” he said. “We don’t do ambitious well in agriculture and food, and hopefully the investment summit puts more of a focus on that.”

Fragmentation in agriculture

One challenge agriculture could face compared other sectors to is the sheer number of players across the country, from farms to processors. So many businesses can potentially mean divided attention.

“The united path is not clearly seen,” said Buy. “The good news in that investment summit is that nobody will care; They’ll make decisions about what makes dollar sense.”

McCann said he didn’t feel fragmentation would be a barrier to securing investment.

“If you look at the companies that are big, companies that are going to do big deals, it isn’t a very long list,” he said.

“That’s true on the farm side as it is on the food processing side. You know, we have (many) farms in the country, but 7.5 per cent of them big represent about 75 per cent of farm revenues.”

Canada has thousands of food processing companies across the country, most have fewer than five employees. Only around 50 are big enough to make significant investments, McCann said.

Agriculture’s need for a co-ordinated approach

McCann said one thing holding agriculture back from investments is the lack of a set of standardized goals for projects across the sector.

“We don’t know what our top 10 trade infrastructure priorities are in the sector,” he said.

He said he is surprised Canada’s agriculture ministers have not worked to create a coordinated investment strategy.

“If we do that, then we get to have a better understanding of where the sector’s priorities align with where these other investment opportunities are, and where they don’t align.”

McCann reiterated Canada has a tendency to focus on small projects, but should consider larger, more tranformational projects “that can really move the needle.”

He said the task now will be to find investment leadership in Canada, something he felt the National Food Security Strategy fell short on.

“We need to do a better job to make it easier to get these plants and projects built in in Canada. And that is that is long, slow, hard work, but is absolutely necessary if we actually want to see substantial investments in ag and food in Canada.”

What investments does agriculture really need?

It will be important to look at gaps in the sector and identify those as areas for investment, said Gervais.

He said these could include:

  • Venture capital for agriculture and food tech
  • De-risking technology adoption for producers
  • Digitizing records of powerful agricultural data
  • water management technology, like irrigation
  • General infrastructure to bring Canadian produce to markets like Asia
  • Value-add to drive demand for Canadian product

The time for trade investment

Buy said as Canada faces down a new wave of U.S. tariffs, this may be the time for Canada to recognize its potential for diversified investments in agriculture.

“Hopefully, we’ll talk about the fantastic potential we have to do a number of things, from getting natural resources and agriculture and food products to markets,” he said.

This could mean Canada finds a place in the push for global food security, as conflicts disrupt some countries’ supply chains.

“Those countries are looking at where they can get those products from elsewhere, and Canada has never been much of an option,” he said. “The challenge and the potential for Canada is looking at capitalizing on the need for stable production for food.”

Despite this potential, Buy said Canada may struggle to attract foreign investment as other markets see the disruptions caused by the U.S.

“That is my only fear, is that at one point we may not be seen as a stable market or source because of the instability created elsewhere,” he said. “Nobody was going to invest in Greenland while Trump was talking about annexing Greenland.”
