'An incredible warmth': Dame Valerie Adams, Sir Wayne Smith lead tributes to Phil Gifford

TheTools · 01.10.2026 01:57 · source · permalink

Subscribe to listen

Dame Valerie Adams and Sir Wayne Smith have paid tribute to Phil Gifford after the legendary Kiwi sports journalist, broadcaster and writer died on Tuesday night.

Across his more than 50 years working in media, Gifford was a man of many hats, hosting several radio shows, rel="" title="https://www.nzherald.co.nz/sport/phil-gifford-dies-aged-79-after-more-than-50-years-in-sports-journalism/4KLUVQH26VGX3POQN6M5I3R4HQ/">working as a sports journalist, columnist and author, under his own name and that of his satirical alter-ego, Loosehead Len. He died aged 79.

Among his work, the Officer of the New Zealand Order of Merit worked with four-time Olympic medallist shot putter Adams on her 2012 book Valerie, The Autobiography, and with renowned rugby coach Smith – who led the Black Ferns to a World Cup title on home soil in 2022 – on his 2023 book Smithy: Endless Winters & The Spring of ’22.

Smith told the Herald he was a fan of Gifford’s before getting to know the writer, looking forward to the 8 O’clock sports newspaper and reading Gifford’s columns when he was a kid in Putāruru growing up playing rugby.

“Used to make my weekend,” Smith said.

“Straight away I had this vision of who Phil Gifford was and how he loved the game, but how he was humorous as well. Then over the years I got to meet him, obviously as a player and then a coach. He’d be the kindest, probably gentlest, funniest reporter I’ve ever met. And through the years of contact we became good friends.

“He pestered me for about three years to write the book that we wrote together and I wasn’t keen to do it. But he would ring me every month. ‘How you going?’ We’d have a chat. He’d tell me a story or two from back in the day and then he would ask, ‘Are you ready to do the book yet?’ And I’d go, ‘No, I don’t want to do a book’.

“But he was relentless about it and so eventually we did. We spent 30 days in a row together, three hours a day. It was supposed to be me telling the stories, but he always had good stories to fill in the spaces as well. He’s a great man.”

Smith, who is now coaching the Kobe Steelers, shared his condolences for Gifford’s wife, Jan, and said he was devastated to receive the news and that it had been difficult for him being in Japan.

He said his phone was going off all night and he wondered what it was, before reading the news when he got up to get ready for work around 4.30am (Japan time) today.

“To the extent of his humour and his ability to stay in contact with you without being a journalist, I think was a key thing for me. It was just a ‘G’day, how you going Smithy?’, that sort of thing over the years. We’d have a good chat,” Smith said.

“I really feel for Jan, it’s terrible news. The days that I spent with him when we were writing the book, Jan was there every day. I just absolutely loved it because I heard more and more stories about life and about other sports as well, you know. It wasn’t just rugby. We went in a quiz in Waihi once with my son and Kim Nankivell, and [former Chiefs player] Alex’s dad, and we were never going to lose the quiz with Phil on our side.

“He had a great memory, great understanding of all sports. He’s just really special to me and special to [my wife] Trish, the way he connected with us. We’re going to really miss him.”

Adams shared similar sentiments in an interview with the Mike Hosking Breakfast show on Newstalk ZB, saying she and Gifford built an “amazing relationship”.

“The first memory is actually walking into his home in Stillwater. At the time, his wife was very unwell, so they were embarking on their own journey there, but [I] walked in and being greeted by amazing tapa cloth in the hallway, so many Polynesian artefacts in their house and just an incredible warmth within the home,” she said.

“When he invited me into his little man cave to start the book, I just felt really at ease, and that just shows the kind of man he was. Not only him but also his wife. I came into their life starting this journey of having my book written, but also our relationship just blossomed to the point where, when I was living in Switzerland, they came to visit me, [they’d] come to see me compete at the Commonwealth Games, multiple competitions, helped with my kids.

“Living in Christchurch during Covid, they made me feel at home, so I was like their Tongan child that never existed but just popped up.”

Herald sports columnist Paul Lewis said few sportswriters had such an impact over such a long period as Gifford.

“He began his career at the Herald before heading elsewhere. I am old enough to remember the small crowds that gathered in Auckland’s Vulcan Lane on Saturday nights, sports nuts waiting for the (now defunct) 8 O’clock sports newspaper to read the latest match reviews from that day.

“Gifford was a big part of that with his Loosehead Len cartoon strip. It probably wouldn’t be run these days with its, um, raw humour but back then it was required reading. He was a multi-talented man – print, radio, cartoons and the author of 26 books.”

He said he recalled sitting next to Gifford at the 1987 Rugby World Cup semi-final between the All Blacks and Wales in Brisbane. “Welsh lock Huw Richards attempted to discipline Gary Whetton with a few blows. Buck Shelford arrived and introduced Richards to the third-best sleeping pill known to man.

“The referee waited until Richards regained consciousness and then sent him off, somehow overlooking Shelford. All Black-hating [Sunday] Times rugby writer Stephen Jones, seething and red in the face, walked over to us, booming a mock headline in our general direction: ‘Kiwis’ day of shame’. Gifford shot back one of those comebacks you always wish you’d said: ‘Welshman Woken Up To Be Sent Off’.

“Rest in peace, Phil.”

Speaking on Ryan Bridge TODAY, media specialist Carmen Parahi said Gifford was a warm, kind and generous man, and gave her the time of day early in her journalism career.

“We clicked immediately because we started to talk about rugby players in Hawke’s Bay. My grandfather was a Māori All Black. I mentioned that to Phil, and we just talked and talked and talked about all the fabulous Hawke’s Bay Māori All Blacks and All Blacks players.

“What I loved about him was his generosity. He looked at sports from a representation perspective. He did fabulous stories of Māori and Pacific sportspeople and the good things that they did.

“I just wanted to mihi to his whānau because it will be a sad day for them, and people who knew Phil.”
