Micah Nori returns to Minnesota to compete against, laugh with old Timberwolves’ friends

TheTools · 16.11.2026 11:58 · source · permalink

Micah Nori frequently finds himself chuckling at his own words during Portland team or coaches meetings.

It may be from referring to himself as “the weatherman” simply reporting the news or informing his players during a film session that he wasn’t the one who made the mess, they were – so they need to watch it.

“I’ll laugh,” Nori said, “because I’m like, ‘Yeah, that’s something Finchy would say.’ ”

Coaches “steal” things from every coach at every stop along their career journey. A 28-year veteran of the NBA, Nori is no exception.

But, specifically, he’s a product of Minnesota.

Nori spent his last five years as an assistant coach with the Timberwolves under his good friend Chris Finch before taking his first-ever head coaching gig this summer with Portland. The early results mimic what Nori helped achieve in Minnesota – a lot of winning.

The Blazers are 9-3 entering Monday’s matchup in Minneapolis. It’s the first of two games in two nights featuring Nori and his now former team and staff with which he has so closely bonded – the one that helped shape the team Portland is becoming, by shaping the coach Nori became.

Constant belief

Nori has a younger staff flush with rising coaches taking on elevated responsibilities this season in Portland. He’s adamant on giving each of them a voice within the operation.

Nori noted if it’s just the head coach talking all the time, your voice drowns into one resembling the mumbling of Charlie Brown’s fictional teacher.

He empowers them to contribute thoughts and ideas. He insists that so long as they get their work done, they can do so from wherever they please to better achieve a work-life balance. He operates with “no paranoia,” meaning he trusts his staff implicitly and allows them to operate in whatever way they best see fit.

He believes in his assistants, much like Finch believed in him. Nori described Finch as not only a “player’s coach,” but also a “coach’s coach.” He takes the advice of his assistants. He allows everyone to coach. He believes in their abilities and potential and always searches for growth opportunities for them.

It was Finch who wouldn’t allow Wolves assistant Max Lefèvre to turn down the chance to take over as head coach of the EuroLeague’s Paris Basketball this summer.

It was Finch who opened Nori’s eyes to the coach’s true potential.

Nori admitted becoming an NBA head coach felt as though it was “a long ways away” when he came to Minnesota in 2021. Frankly, he was content being a lead assistant, if that ultimately proved to be his professional ceiling. He didn’t even have an agent until Finch introduced him to one when the Wolves’ staff coached all-star weekend.

“I credit Finchy a lot for … basically saying, ‘Micah, you can do this,’ ” Nori said.

He could be a head coach. He could run his own program. Wolves basketball boss Tim Connelly vocally expressed the same sentiment on multiple occasions.

“(Tim) said, ‘If we do well, there’s no reason you shouldn’t be a head coach,’ ” Finch said. “He put it out there, maybe even before I did.”

Their reasons were concrete. No. 1 was Nori’s experience. He’d done everything there is to do on an NBA staff, which Finch felt showed up in Nori’s basketball acumen. He could absorb any level of information and quickly boil it down into a digestible format.

And, perhaps more importantly for Finch, was Nori’s “off the charts” emotional intelligence.

“There’s a lot to this business where that’s even more important than the basketball piece,” Finch said. “With a leader, you have to have emotional intelligence. The way he was able to connect with people in the building and hold players accountable in his own way (which was) natural and non-threatening, I think those are the most important pieces of the job. And I thought he had them.”

Nori admits having to step into the acting lead role when Finch got hurt during the 2024 playoffs (Finch was the head coach in every other aspect, but Nori was tasked with stalking the sidelines during games) opened his eyes to the idea that he could do the job on a full-time basis.

Finch quickly became emotionally invested in each of Nori’s head coaching search cycles. Each time Nori was passed over only became more motivational ammunition. Finch watched as each interview process sharpened Nori’s self belief and understanding of how to present himself.

Empowering assistants involves adding responsibilities to their plate whenever they’re ready, as well as encouraging them to leave the nest when the time is right.

Nori is loving his inaugural season as an NBA bench boss. Portland is abuzz. He acknowledged the winning certainly helps, but so does the management philosophy imparted on him by his friend.

“I’m also enjoying it because I see that, the way Finchy did it, it empowers people, it makes people feel a part of it, it gives people ownership, it makes people excited to come to work,” Nori said. “I think, in that regard, it’s been very rewarding for me to see all of that.”

A familiar approach

Schematically, Portland and Minnesota are awfully similar. A big reason why is because of the rosters. Nori noted the strengths and skillsets of your players tell you what to run.

Portland features a massive interior rim protector, long athletes on the wing and a dynamic point guard who’s most comfortable operating out of the pick and roll.

Sound familiar?

Many of Portland’s preparation processes, top to bottom, also resemble how Minnesota operates.

Nori mimics Finch’s efforts to clearly define roles, speak to players in “headlines” that make it easily known what your message is and why you feel that way and confront people with “brutal truths” in a face-to-face manner to erase ambiguity and put minds at ease.

“I‘ve probably taken as much as anybody from (Finch) and tried to implement it,” Nori said, “because I’ve seen how it works.”

In his first meeting with his new staff in August, Nori gifted each member a copy of Ernest Hemingway’s “The Old Man and the Sea” to read, something Finch had Nori and his staff do years prior.

Finch has found a few of the stirring similarities in approach to be funny. He’s joked with Nori about them.

“But I’m also extremely flattered that he would take a lot of the things,” Finch said. “And it’s not the basketball piece as much as it might be the cultural piece, the staff organizational piece or the messaging, all that kind of stuff. Those are things maybe he didn’t put a lot of time into thinking about before he got here (to Minnesota).”

Finch also knows some of the things Nori has implemented in Portland are wrinkles he pushed for with the Wolves that never came to fruition.

“So it’s kind of cool to see him put those touches on what he’s doing, as well,” Finch said. “That’s the fun of it. You get to try out the things you always wanted to, where maybe you weren’t allowed to before.”

‘I miss you’

Good luck making it through a Nori media availability without the coach cracking at least one joke, and likely more – there are few funnier.

Nori’s lasting legacy here in Minnesota remains the televised halftime interviews he delivered, in which he’d weave wacky phrases fed to him as a form of, well, fun.

While it made him a fan favorite, it was fair to wonder if the comedic relief would hinder the 52-year-old’s ability to ultimately land an NBA coaching gig. He didn’t care. Nothing would stop Nori from being Nori.

Even as the bench boss in Portland, Nori refuses to shy away from humor.

“I’m at a point in my life where that’s the only advantage of getting this later in your career, at 50-plus years old as opposed to being a 35-year old head coach and all this and that – I can do it my way, because there’s no fear,” Nori said. “There’s literally no fear of being fired. If they came in tomorrow and said, ‘Micah, you’re done,’ I’d be like, ‘Alright, cool,’ quite honestly. Because I don’t need to do this. I love it, I enjoy the hell out of it, and I’ve got a lot of people that are very, very supportive. But I don’t have to be like, ‘Ah, let me do this,’ because I’m thinking about my next job.

“At the end of the day, I care, but I don’t care if I get fired. I’m not worried about it. … So that’s what allows me to be the person that I am, it allows me to coach how I want.”

So he’s going to laugh, he’s going to have fun and, if early returns are any indication, he’s going to win.

It’s a refreshing demeanor in a profession that oftentimes treats bouncing a ball as far too serious of an endeavor. It’s why Nori is so missed in Minnesota. Wolves assistant coach Pablo Prigioni said he notices Nori’s absence in the gym “every day.”

“Micah was the type of guy that as soon as you’re stepping into this building, he was like, ‘Pablo!’” Prigioni said. “ It was so fun to be around him all these years. I’ve told him multiple times, ‘I miss you, I miss you, I miss you.’”

The coaching staff in Minnesota is the one Nori grew closest to in his career. His family didn’t relocate to Minnesota with him when he joined the staff, as both of his kids were still in high school.

So he spent a lot of time around his coworkers, even more so than usual considering how long the Wolves’ highly successful seasons lasted in recent years.

On the court, Finch said Minnesota misses Nori’s “incredible ability” to simplify messages down for all to consume. Off of it, his “big personality” is impossible to replace.

“It doesn’t mean we don’t have a great staff, it’s just a different dynamic when these guys leave,” Finch said. “His humor just kept everything light. I miss it because he could always make me laugh when I didn’t feel like it. He kind of had a unique relationship with every single person in that regard.”

Nori makes a point to stay in touch with all of them. He texts many of his former teammates – from the coaching staff to front office members such as Connelly, Matt Lloyd, Dell Demps, Steve Senior and more – regularly. The bonds built in his five years here are iron clad. All of them are celebrating Nori’s early-season success.

“I think it’s vice-versa, just because of the relationships that we’ve had – I cheer for the Timberwolves 78 nights out of the year, and I really believe they feel the same way about the Blazers,” Nori said. “I know that they’re watching us, and they’re literally pulling for me and us, which is really cool.”

He and Finch still speak on the phone three to four times a week, if not more. Sometimes it’s to chat ball, other times it’s just to laugh.

This week, great friends become opponents, if only for two days.

Finch admitted it will be “a bit surreal” seeing Nori standing on the opposite end of the floor, complaining about a call that went in Minnesota’s favor. Nori is looking forward to the reunion, while making sure to “keep the main thing the main thing” with his preparation.

This is a serious endeavor, after all … but only to a certain degree.

“My goal,” Finch joked, “is to make him call a rage timeout.”
