Jehovah's Witness rules on blood transfusions have changed, but it's too late for my brother

TheTools · 07.11.2026 20:44 · source · permalink

Isaac Valdez had an epileptic seizure and his condition deteriorated rapidly

  • Published
    7 November 2026, 01:27 GMT

In August this year, 26-year-old Isaac Valdez had an epileptic seizure while browsing in his local library, causing him to convulse and strike his head against the ground.

He lost consciousness. Later, in hospital, it transpired Isaac had hit his head with such force it had led to a brain haemorrhage.

Treatment was seriously complicated by the fact that, as a Jehovah's Witness, Isaac carried a "No Blood" card, an indication that he would not accept treatment involving any form of blood transfusion because of his religious beliefs.

As a result, a procedure to drain fluid from Isaac's brain did not take place.

But his family, from Waxahachie, Texas, were encouraged by the fact that he regained consciousness and even started to talk again. He was able to confirm that he stood by his "no blood" beliefs.

The family's relief at his apparent improvement was short-lived. Isaac slipped into a coma and his condition worsened rapidly.

A neurosurgeon told Isaac's family the only way to save his life was an emergency operation, but that it was one that required blood transfusion.

His family took the decision to abide by Issac's wishes as a lifelong Witness not to accept such a procedure.

Isaac - who his twin brother Jacob Valdez describes as an intelligent man who loved volunteering and helping the elderly - died on 26 August 2026 when doctors turned off his life support.

Jacob's "no blood" card which is like the one carried in Isaac's wallet

Just 23 days later, the Jehovah's Witnesses' Governing Body announced a seismic change in teaching that would, in effect, have given Isaac the religious green light to have the life-saving treatment he had refused.

The change suddenly means the vast majority of medical procedures using donated blood are no longer prohibited to followers. Now, decisions on whether to accept such treatments are to be left to individuals.

Jacob, already dealing with the death of his twin, was angered at the Jehovah's Witnesses' leadership for not coming to their conclusion sooner. He told BBC News he believes Isaac could well still be alive if they had.

Jacob (right) believes his twin Isaac could still be alive if the change in teaching had come sooner

The original rule on blood transfusions dates back to 1945, towards the very end of a war during which the use of blood transfusions had been increasingly widespread. The leaders of Jehovah's Witnesses announced that their believers were not permitted to accept transfusions.

This was based on their interpretation of Bible verses from both the Old and New Testaments prohibiting the consumption of blood, including one from the Book of Acts that instructs believers to abstain from blood, from food offered to idols, and from things that had been strangled.

So, why has the Jehovah's Witnesses' Governing Body, based in New York state, now decided to change the rules, 81 years after introducing them?

It comes after years of pressure from both within and outside the faith, but that is not how leaders of the faith describe the decision.

"The change is basically the application of Bible principles to modern medicine and its methods," says the Jehovah's Witnesses international spokesperson, Paul Gillies, who described it as both a fresh look at scripture and at medical advances.

Jehovah's Witnesses international spokesperson Paul Gillies says the ban on transfusion of whole blood remains but blood components can now be used

We meet Gillies at the Jehovah's Witnesses UK headquarters set in 85 acres of Essex countryside. It is a vast modern complex where hundreds of volunteer followers of the faith live and work without pay.

He insists the basic teaching has not changed and that the transfusion of whole blood is still prohibited. However, for decades, most transfusions have not used whole blood, instead using the specific components of blood that are needed by the patient.

The change is being justified by permitting the use of blood components, like plasma and red blood cells and platelets, in the huge numbers of medical procedures that were previously banned.

Gillies says the Bible references "abstaining from blood" but not "various components that could be extracted from blood".

He says the Governing Body felt it their duty to avoid scriptural "overreach" and only apply what was specifically described in the Bible.

But that explanation has stunned many and led them to ask if it was not a huge overreach 81 years ago to ban blood transfusions in the first place, when transfusions are not specifically mentioned in the bible.

Others are then also asking why it has taken so long to come to this landmark decision, given that transfusions that do not use whole blood have been the norm for decades.

These are questions Jacob asked as soon as the ban was lifted, just days after a memorial service for Isaac.

Jacob, who had already left the Jehovah's Witness faith he was brought up in, texted prominent figures from Isaac's congregation in Texas after Isaac's death. The messages are full of angst, and painful to read.

He asked them to consider "the part you played in encouraging so many people to sacrifice their life for something that was never God's will".

Most did not reply, but one "elder" responded saying he could not understand why Jacob was so angry and fixated on the policy shift.

This all further infuriated Jacob, who particularly wants the faith's decision-makers to be accountable for all the lives lost.

"I want to ask them if they could apologise directly, I would like not to have a deflected answer, just straightforward apology," he says.

It is extremely difficult to say how many Jehovah's Witnesses, among the estimated nine million followers of the faith worldwide, died each year having refused treatment involving blood transfusions. But research in one medical journal in 2016, The Obstetrical & Gynecological Survey, put the figure at around 1,100.

"We're sympathetic to their feelings, and if individuals have lost family members and are concerned about some of these questions, they deserve to be heard compassionately," says Gillies.

But there was little clarity about why the change did not come earlier.

"Why has it taken this time? Well, I guess because it takes time, it doesn't necessarily mean that there is some magic change that's taken place," says Gillies.

And when asked, Gillies deflects calls for the leadership to apologise, saying that many lives had, in fact, been saved because faithful Jehovah's Witnesses had not been exposed to blood contamination scandals.

He also insists that even after the new edict, some Jehovah's Witnesses could still decide they did not want to accept a transfusion, and that may have been the case even if the edict had come years ago.

Indeed, the BBC spoke with Alex Ball, 58, a Jehovah's Witness from West Sussex, who says in 2012 a doctor told him he would not survive the chemotherapy he needed to treat his blood cancer unless he also had a transfusion.

"I wanted to adhere as closely to the scriptures as possible when I was sick, and I think I did that well."

With help from what is called "a Jehovah's Witness Hospital Liaison Committee", Alex found an American consultant who could perform a treatment to boost Alex's blood cells without the need for any products from someone else. His UK consultant agreed to treat him using the new method and it worked.

More than a decade later, Alex says he would make the same decision again, despite the change in blood policy that would have permitted the transfusions he had been told he needed.

It is not just receiving blood which has been an issue.

Elisha Cummings, 50, a Jehovah's Witness from Trinidad and Tobago, was unable to donate blood to her aunt, who died, because of the rules.

Elisha Cummings was advised by an elder not to donate blood to her aunt Eva who died

They also cost her her marriage too, with her husband of 10 years leaving the faith and divorcing her after it came to light that he had had a blood transfusion as a child, something that was looked down upon in her Witness community.

"Since the blood policy change, I have not been myself," she says. "It left me in a sort of depressed mode."

"How many houses did I go to trying to convince people about the blood doctrine? And you're coming now and it's something totally different," Elisha says.

Elisha says her beliefs as a whole have been shaken, yet she feels reluctant to leave the faith, partly because it is what provides her with her community and she fears isolation.

In the past, those who disagreed with "blood doctrine" were ostracised or felt like they needed to leave the faith. Will they now be able to come back?

"Any individual that wants to come back to the faith is warmly welcomed," says Gillies, speaking of those who had left the faith because they had accepted a transfusion for themselves or a relative.

"If they would like to become one of Jehovah's Witnesses again and would like to speak to the elders, then they're very welcome to do so."
