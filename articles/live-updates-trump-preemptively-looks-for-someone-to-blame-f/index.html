Updates

Vance says El-Sayed 'obsessed' with conflicts in Middle East rather than Michigan

Vice President JD Vance revived his ongoing feud with Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed as he rallied in Michigan Thursday, casting the candidate as “obsessed” with conflicts in the Middle East rather than issues in the state he is running in.

Framing the midterm election as “a battle between those who loath the United States of America and those who love it,” Vance said Michigan has “become ground zero in that battle.”

“It is a battle between grievance and gratitude, between politicians obsessed with foreign causes and leaders who remember that their first duty is to fight for the people right here at home,” the vice president said as he touted his support for Republican Mike Rogers.

Vance and El-Sayed have traded barbs throughout the campaign cycle, as Republicans have looked to use the progressive, and at times controversial, candidate as a Democratic boogeyman both in Michigan and other battleground races.

Some background: President Donald Trump carried Monroe County, where Vance spoke, by 27 points in 2024.

Democratic Senate candidates show fundraising strength in Q3

Today is the deadline for third quarter reports to the Federal Election Commission, covering fundraising and spending activity between July and September, and announcements and filings are steadily coming in for some key races.

Georgia Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff was another Democratic Senate candidate announcing a huge third quarter, raising $33 million according to a release from his campaign. His GOP challenger, Rep. Mike Collins, has not yet announced or filed a Q3 report.

Meanwhile, in North Carolina, former Gov. Roy Cooper announced raising $19.5 million in Q3, as he appears in a strong position to win his battleground Senate race against former RNC Chair Michael Whatley, who has not yet disclosed his totals.

Filings, which are due by midnight, will shed detailed light on fundraising, spending, and cash on hand entering October.

Meet the Democratic donor funding last-minute Senate ads in deep-red states

A Florida lawyer and his family are plowing millions into a new super PAC to boost Democrats’ chances of flipping four GOP-held Senate seats.

The last-minute gambit by Steve Pajcic, a former Florida state lawmaker and co-founder of a personal injury firm, underscores the optimism among some Democrats that the midterms could deliver big wins for their party.

Pajcic said he will spend at least $7 million on ads to shape US Senate races in Florida, Kansas, Mississippi and South Carolina. Ads began appearing this week, including one targeting Mississippi Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in a state President Donald Trump won by nearly 23 points two years ago.

Scott Colom, the Democratic nominee in Mississippi, has drawn growing interest from big-name figures in his party. Officials in Hyde-Smith’s campaign have said they are confident she will prevail in this deep-red state.

In an interview with CNN on Thursday, Pajcic said he hopes Democrats could win 55 or 56 seats in November and “contribute to a wave of history.”

Pajcic said he could add more to the budget as he and his team evaluate races.

The New York Times first reported on the spending by Pajcic’s super PAC, New Deal for All.

Pajcic said his family’s money can go further in states such as Mississippi with lower-cost media markets. He’s steering well clear of another ruby-red state, Texas, which has seen huge spending to influence the outcome of the battle between Republican Ken Paxton and Democrat James Talarico.

“I would never put any money in Texas, because, Jiminy Cricket, I would be just a drop in the bucket,” the 80-year-old said. “I’m not one of those billionaires, you know. I’m just somebody who happens to have a little extra money, and I think this is a very good cause.”

Trump tries to lay blame for potential Texas Senate loss on Sen. John Cornyn

President Donald Trump on Thursday preemptively blamed outgoing Sen. John Cornyn, whom he spurned in the primary, and GOP strategist Karl Rove for a potential Senate loss in the Lone Star State.

“If Ken Paxton loses, it will be because of WEAK and INEFFECTIVE Political Hacks like John Cornyn and Karl Rove, who have ineffectively opposed me for my entire political career,” Trump said a Truth Social post.

His latest comments underscore GOP anxiety about the Senate race, with Republicans pouring millions of dollars into the reliably red state to boost state Attorney General Ken Paxton, who’s been neck-and-neck with Democrat James Talarico in recent polling.

Trump used some of the strongest words he’s had for Cornyn, whose name resurfaced in recent weeks in leaked audio of Paxton. The president claimed that Cornyn and Rove are “doing everything possible to make it hard” for Paxton to win and asked “all of those who supported Senator John Cornyn to UNITE, and immediately switch your support to Ken Paxton.”

The president said Cornyn and Rove “should be ashamed of themselves,” adding, “They are hurting Texas and, more importantly, hurting the United States of America!”

CNN has reached out to Cornyn.

Earlier Thursday, Trump said he’d return to Texas, if necessary, for Paxton, whom he called “controversial.” He’s already made three trips to the state within one month to bolster his chosen candidate.

Trump has said he has “no regrets” about backing Paxton over Cornyn, but the pair’s relationship has been fraught, with Trump calling the GOP nominee “a pain in the ass sometimes” and making comments on his appearance, while Paxton has privately questioned the president’s political capital.

Protester argues El-Sayed’s message won’t resonate in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula

As voters filed in for Michigan Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed’s second campaign stop of the day, a small group of Mike Rogers supporters gathered outside the venue to voice their displeasure with El-Sayed’s visit to the state’s Upper Peninsula.

Donning a “Mike Rogers” t-shirt, Pam Trask told CNN she believes El-Sayed is “unfit to serve” in the Senate and said she didn’t think his message would resonate with voters in Iron Mountain, Michigan, where he campaigned today with progressive Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez.

“If you come to the UP, you realize that we are salt of the earth people,” she added. “We work hard, and we don’t want them taking our money and our land to spread the wealth as they do in socialism, communism.”

Inside the town hall venue, Stacey Peterson said she voted early for El-Sayed and argued that his message could still appeal to residents in the reliably red part of the state.

“I think that they’re not listening to him speak, because his message is very clear,” Peterson said. “He’s running to get money out of politics, money back in our pocket and Medicare for all. And those are three things that we desperately need.”

Embattled Rep. Max Miller raises low numbers going into final weeks before midterms

Embattled GOP Rep. Max Miller of Ohio only raised around $145,000 in the last quarter and personally loaned his campaign $1 million, according to a new FEC filing published Thursday.

The filings indicate the incumbent Republican has struggled to raise funds amidst allegations of physical abuse from his ex-wife, Emily Moreno, though his personal injection of $1,000,000 into his campaign could serve as a useful buffer.

Miller has denied Moreno’s allegations. The parents are also embroiled in an ongoing custody battle over their young child.

Ohio Sen. Jon Husted, who is up this cycle, has also called on Miller to resign and drop his bid for re-election.

While the Republican senators from his home state have slammed Miller, House Speaker Mike Johnson has called Miller a “great member” of Congress and backed his reelection bid, arguing that the courts and the House Ethics Committee have yet to make a determination on the accusations against Miller.

Vance rallies in Michigan amid feud with Abdul El-Sayed

It’s probably a good thing Vice President JD Vance and Abdul El-Sayed are campaigning on opposite sides of Michigan today with a Great Lake between them.

The pair have exchanged personal barbs during this campaign cycle.

On “evil”

At a campaign rally in Michigan in August, Vance called the Democratic Senate nominee “evil,” which is not a term used between people who seem capable of having a civilized conversation.

El-Sayed responded in a statement that Trump administration policies – trade wars and real wars, among others – are evil.

Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed fired back at Vice President JD Vance's encouragement of House Republicans to use him as a boogeyman on the campaign trail, telling CNN's Manu Raju that Republicans will talk about "anything to not have to talk about" affordability concerns.

On their spouses

Vance, in August, incorrectly tried to imply that El-Sayed wants to bring Sharia law to the US, and added he would struggle to explain modern Democrats to his grandfather, “my Papaw.”

El-Sayed responded on social media wondering how Papaw would view Vance’s wife Usha, who is the child of Indian immigrants.

Vance hit back that El-Sayed should, “keep my wife’s name the hell out of your mouth, because she’s way out of your league.”

El-Sayed was asked on CNN if spouses should be left out of such attacks.

On everything

A main campaign goal of Republicans this year has been to make Democrats seem foreign, weird or radical, and El-Sayed has been their top target.

In September, Vance told House Republicans to treat El-Sayed as a bogeyman.

“If you are one of the few Republicans who’s running against a Democrat who’s not that crazy, then just talk about Abdul El-Sayed,” Vance said.

Scammers target voters with phishing emails from election officials, phony candidate merch

Scammers are targeting voters with fake warnings from election officials and websites selling phony campaign merchandise.

A new report from security firm Check Point, which was obtained by CNN, flagged these and other cybersecurity concerns before the November midterm elections.

Election officials and the FBI have previously issued warnings about similar election-related financial scams.

The Check Point report also said it uncovered more than 280 websites selling merchandise under a candidate’s name but that don’t have any official ties to the campaign.

These sites use the official campaign logos, but they aren’t affiliated with the official campaigns. In fact, these sites were connected to each other based on their domain registration. This means people might think they’re financially supporting one candidate, when they’re really paying a private company that’s cashing in from both sides.

Former President George W. Bush was among Maine Sen. Susan Collins' Q3 donors

Maine Republican Sen. Susan Collins is locked in an intense reelection campaign this year, and in the third quarter she got a boost from a notable ally: former President George W. Bush.

Bush made the maximum individual donation to Collins’ campaign, $3,500, on August 26, according to Collins’ quarterly filing with the Federal Election Commission.

The former president has deep ties to the state of Maine – his family owns a compound in Kennebunkport where they’ve vacationed for decades, with the nearby Walker’s Point promontory named for his father, former President George H.W. Bush.

Collins, meanwhile, served in the US Senate for the entirety of the Bush administration, and the two have enjoyed a warm relationship.

New Hampshire asks Supreme Court to review use of student IDs for voters

New Hampshire officials asked the US Supreme Court on Thursday to wade into a fight over a state law that bars the use of student IDs to prove a voter’s identity.

Lower federal courts allowed the state to enforce the central prohibition of that law: student IDs, on their own, no longer can be used at polls. But a federal court ruled that election workers could continue to consider student IDs as one factor along with other evidence to assess a voter’s identity.

New Hampshire Secretary of State David Scanlan, a Republican, said in the state’s emergency appeal that the “new interpretation” of the law “risks voter confusion.”

The state argues that a legal doctrine that warns federal courts against last-minute changes to election rules should decide the case in its favor.

New Hampshire voters will fill an open Senate seat in November to replace the retiring Democratic Sen. Jeanne Shaheen.

The Supreme Court is likely to decide the case quickly — potentially within a few days.

Booker's $22 million stockpile could fuel 2028 questions

New Jersey Sen. Cory Booker reported more than $22 million in cash on hand in his third quarter FEC report — one of several potential 2028 contenders whose filings could give clues about their future plans.

Booker, who unsuccessfully sought the 2020 Democratic presidential nomination, is up for reelection this year, though his race against GOP nominee Justin Murphy in reliably blue New Jersey is not expected to be particularly competitive.

As a result, Booker has been able to stockpile funds that, if he chooses to run for president again, he can transfer directly to a potential White House campaign. And $22 million represents a sizable nest egg with which to seed a future run.

Other candidates whose filings could be telling include Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff, who, even amid a high-profile reelection campaign, has established a formidable position, announcing a Q3 haul of $33 million. The latest numbers put his total raised since the start of 2025 past $100 million.

Favored to win reelection, Ossoff’s strong performance could leave him with leftover cash that can be used for a potential presidential campaign, increasingly speculated about by Democrats.

A third federal candidate whose Q3 filing will be closely scrutinized is New York Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. The progressive icon has built a fundraising operation rivaling national party committees, and has already accumulated more than $15 million in cash on hand while holding a safe Democratic House district.

This has fueled questions about her interest in higher office, whether a US Senate bid in 2028 or a run for the White House herself.

What's happening in Michigan's Senate race?

The race between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers, a former congressman who lost to Sen. Elissa Slotkin in 2024 by 0.3%, remains one of the GOP’s few offensive opportunities this cycle.

A recently released Marist poll of registered voters showed El-Sayed up seven points over Rogers: 51% to 44%. But all polls of likely voters in September painted a different picture with no clear leader in the contest, including a recent survey released on September 30 by Fox News.

The GOP spending advantage in this race is a major factor that could keep this race close up until Election Day. For the month of October, Republicans are set to spend more than $43 million while Democrats have reserved about $25 million.

Republicans here have mounted a determined effort to paint El-Sayed as outside the mainstream, running in waves of ads calling the Democrat “dangerous” and “radical.”

But frustration with President Donald Trump and concerns about the economy make the environment tough for Rogers in a state hit hard by tariffs and gas prices driven up by the Iran war.

According to the Fox News poll, 39% are extremely concerned Rogers is too closely aligned with Trump, while 36% said El-Sayed’s positions are too liberal.

Frustrated with the high cost of health care, Mary Beth Miller told CNN she is donating her one-time $90 Medicare Part B rebate check to Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed’s campaign.

Democrats are hammering Trump in ads while Republicans avoid the subject

President Donald Trump has been urging voters “to pretend that I’m on the ballot” as he tries to drum up turnout in the 2026 midterm elections.

But GOP advertisers targeting key races don’t seem to be getting the message.

An analysis of AdImpact data from the kickoff of general elections across the country post-Labor Day through this week shows that more than $200 million has been spent on campaign ads explicitly mentioning Trump.

And Democrats account for $180 million of that total, while Republicans have spent just $18 million on ads that mention the president during this key stretch.

Several of the GOP ads actually feature Republican candidates trying to create some space from Trump.

“When Trump is right, I’ll work with him. When he’s wrong, I’ll stand up for Minnesota,” says Michele Tafoya, the Republican nominee for US Senate in Minnesota, in one of her ads.

Democrats, meanwhile, have continued to voice blistering opposition. “He’s right in lockstep with Donald Trump every time,” says one an ad from a top Democratic super PAC, hammering Michigan Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers over tariffs and the war with Iran.

The spotlight of this election is on red America

The modern tale of US politics is that there is a red America and a blue America.

The battles of this midterm cycle are very focused on the red side.

President Donald Trump has focused his travel on states he won in 2024, labeling Democrats “Dumocrats” in an effort to whip up his base and save endangered Senate seats.

Democrats are campaigning in some of those same red areas with the opposite goal, hoping disappointment in Trump and the economy can help them redraw the political map.

Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez of New York is joining the Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed for a swing through the deeply red Upper Peninsula in Michigan today. It’s an area Abdul-Sayed is unlikely to win, but where he could use some votes.

Former Vice President Kamala Harris is headed to Mississippi, where the Democrat, Scott Colom, is hoping for an epic upset of Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith. If we’re talking about this race on Election Day, it will be a sign of a true blue wave.

Democrats are also honing their appeals to Republicans.

James Talarico, the Senate candidate in Texas, tried to find common ground with a woman who opposes abortion rights during a CNN town hall broadcast Wednesday.

James Talarico worked to find common ground with one Texas voter who said she votes exclusively on the issue of abortion. Talarico said that while he supports some abortion access, he said he wants to see fewer unplanned pregnancies and abortions overall, and that the issue should be viewed instead as a "public policy problem."

In Kansas, the pastor Adam Hamilton said there’s one way he can defy history to become the first Democrat elected to the Senate there in nearly 100 years.

“I’ll only get elected with Republicans voting for me,” Hamilton told ABC News in an interview that aired this week week.

Part of this is the natural ebb and flow of the American system. Backlash to Republicans, who control everything in Washington, DC, is to be expected.

Focusing across the aisle runs counter to the logic of the hyper-partisan Trump era.

But it is a necessity. Democrats need to win seats in multiple states that Trump won in 2024 in order to control the Senate.

A visit to Michigan’s Upper Peninsula, where people proudly call themselves “yoopers” and relish the outdoors, wouldn’t be complete without a stop for apples fresh from the season, a lake trout dinner or a “pasty” – a meat-stuffed pastry.

Many voters turned up hours early to line up for El-Sayed’s first town hall in Escanaba, a reliably red city on Lake Michigan.

El-Sayed made a direct appeal to disaffected Republican voters who previously voted for President Donald Trump.

After delivering his stump speech and leading the crowd in a chorus of “money out of politics, money in your pocket and Medicare for all,” El-Sayed took questions for nearly an hour.

“One of the most astounding things that I’ve come to realize as we campaign here in the general election is the number of people who tell me I voted for Donald Trump, two, three times and I’m voting for you,” he told CNN.

Rogers launches seven-figure ad buy for closing stretch of tight Michigan Senate race

Mike Rogers, the Republican nominee for US Senate in Michigan, announced plans today for a seven-figure ad buy to promote his closing message in the final weeks of the battleground race.

The campaign launched a new ad that criticizes Abdul El-Sayed, Rogers’ Democratic opponent, on tax policy, featuring residents warning that he’d drive up their utility bills. “We can’t afford Abdul,” a speaker in the ad says. Others tout Rogers’ commitment to tax cuts, saying that the Republican will put “money back in our pockets.”

Rogers’ expenditure for the closing stretch adds heft to an already massive GOP effort in the Wolverine state. In the final three weeks, Republicans are set to spend more than $29 million total on ads, including outside groups, while Democrats have about $18 million booked.

And since the start of 2025, both parties have combined to spend more than $300 million on the election – currently the fourth most expensive race of the 2026 cycle.

Pro-AI super PAC sees contributions dry up in Q3

Leading The Future, a super PAC formed to advance the development of artificial intelligence, saw its contributions continue to dry up in the third quarter of 2026.

The super PAC reported just under $200,000 in total receipts – all from interest on its existing cash, with no new individual contributions – during Q3. That follows a second quarter in which the group took in just $250,000, after raising more than $75 million since its inception in August of last year.

Industry heavyweights – including OpenAI President Greg Brockman and his wife Anna, who gave a combined $25 million, and the founders of the venture capital firm a16z, Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz, who combined to give $50 million – accounted for virtually all of the super PAC’s initial funding.

The group directed those millions to dozens of midterms races this year, backing candidates that aligned with its vision of accelerated AI progress and limited regulation.

But amid growing public backlash to artificial intelligence and the data center infrastructure required to power it, the group’s momentum appears to be flagging. The New York Times reported last month that Brockman had pulled out of a plans for an additional $25 million contribution.
