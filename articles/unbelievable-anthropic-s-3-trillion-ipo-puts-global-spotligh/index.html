“Unbelievable”: Anthropic’s $3 trillion IPO puts global spotlight on Melbourne gardening business

Anthropic has thrust a small Melbourne business into the global spotlight, with the artificial intelligence lab referencing the boutique gardening firm while listing on the NASDAQ.

The AI company launched on the NASDAQ on Thursday local time, reaching a valuation of about US$2 trillion (AU$3 trillion) in one of the largest-ever initial public offerings.

CEO Dario Amodei rang the ceremonial bell in front of a slideshow highlighting organisations and causes using Anthropic’s AI tools.

Among them was Gardener & Son, a Hawthorn-based business focused on “rewilding” suburban gardens with the help of native plants and rejuvenated soil.

The business also earned a shout-out on digital billboards across Times Square.

“It’s unbelievable,” co-founder Tyson Lundbech told SmartCompany.

After intentionally shutting its maintenance wing to focus on garden design and installation, Gardener & Son used tools like Claude to build its online presence.

That includes FindMyEVC, a free website allowing Victorians to search for their local Ecological Vegetation Class, which describes the flora common to each region before European settlement.

From there, users can “unlock” a list of suitable native plants and register their garden to assist with biodiversity mapping.

Leveraging AI to build those digital tools is “actually putting us in the garden longer,” said Lundbech.

“We would have needed a huge team to build these web applications,” he continued.

“It’s just the two of us, and it really gives us the opportunity to focus on our craft, without having a big team.”

The Gardener & Son team eventually reached out to Anthropic, sparking a back-and-forth relationship that saw the AI lab feature the business across social media.

Even so, Lundbech was taken aback by Anthropic showcasing the business during its initial public offering.

“We’re just a small Melbourne business, and it was quite incredible to see that,” he said.

The Anthropic IPO is backdropped by concerns from environmental advocates that rampant data centre development could increase pollution or suck up water in drought-prone regions.

But Lundbech said AI tools can also help Australians make better decisions about their gardens, encouraging biodiversity and soil health.

“As long as we keep going and we keep getting these plants in the ground, and we keep growing these trees and cooling our spaces, we’re heading in the right direction,” he said.

Earning a call-out from one of the most significant AI companies on Earth is not guaranteed, but Lundbech said other Australian small businesses should still consider creative ways to bring AI into their operation.

“You can use these tools in such a way that anyone can really do anything,” he said.
