The 100-year life is rewriting Australia's retirement rulebook - SMSF Adviser

TheTools · 17.11.2026 18:35 · source · permalink

The research from Challenger’s Institute for Lifetime Income shows that an Australian couple, both aged 65, now has a 43 per cent probability of at least one partner living to 95. At the same time, Australians who retire due to illness or injury do so at an average age of just 57, seven years earlier than those who retire voluntarily at 64.

The report was released to coincide with the Australian visit of academics from the Stanford Center on Longevity to discuss the New Map of Life research and its call for a new script for longer lives, with greater flexibility to move between learning, working, caring for others and retirement.

Stanford Center on Longevity Associate Director and Senior Research Scholar (Emerita), Martha Deevy, said the traditional expectations surrounding education, career and then retirement were developed for a world in which people lived much shorter lives.

“The 100-year life is here, but we’re still following a script written for a much shorter one,” Deevy said.

“For generations, we’ve organised our lives around three distinct stages: education, work and retirement. But longer lives mean that model is no longer fit for purpose, with more time to learn, change careers, care for family and contribute to our communities”

She said the New Map of Life recognises life is rarely linear which brings unexpected opportunities and challenges. Deevy added that it is therefore important to have flexibility and confidence to navigate these changes.

Dr Jonathan Kearns, Challenger Chief Economist, said the research supported Stanford’s findings and highlighted the difficulty of planning for retirement when neither its starting point nor its duration could be predicted with certainty.

“The two biggest unknowns in retirement planning are when you’ll stop working and how long you’ll live. Both can have a significant impact on how much money you’ll need,” Kearns said.

“Someone who expects to retire at 64 but is forced to leave work at 57 could face seven additional years without employment income. If they then live into their nineties, their savings may need to stretch across almost four decades.

“That’s a substantial financial challenge, particularly when inflation, investment returns and household circumstances can change considerably over such a long period. It means Australians need to plan for a range of retirement outcomes, rather than assuming their savings only need to last for an average lifespan.”

The research comes as approximately 300,000 Australians turn 65 this year, with that number expected to increase to 375,000 annually by the middle of the century.
