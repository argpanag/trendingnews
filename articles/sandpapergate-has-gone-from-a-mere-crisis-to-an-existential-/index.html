Sandpapergate has gone from a mere crisis to an existential threat

Jesus wept.

I have seen prettier train crashes, even ones with less likelihood of survivors.

The situation with Sandpapergate has now lurched from merely a crisis to an existential threat to the leading figures in the game. For those coming in late, a quick primer might be useful. It goes like this.

For decades those of us who love cricket have understood that canny bowlers could get an advantage in getting their cricket balls to move more in the air and take more wickets by doing everything from rubbing one side of it on their trousers, to rubbing Brylcreem into it, to stepping on it.

All fit broadly under the title of “gamesmanship”. But there was a line, and in 2018 in South Africa that line was crossed when it was found that with the encouragement of David Warner, and the tacit knowledge of captain Steve Smith, one of the younger players, Cameron Bancroft, had taken sandpaper onto the field and had been caught on TV cameras stuffing it down his pants.

Australia reeled in horror.

NO! Just no. While we may not know much about the intricacies of cricket, we all accepted that using sandpaper on the ball was not gamesmanship, it was CHEATING.

Everyone from the then prime minister Malcolm Turnbull down got involved and when the smoke cleared, Smith and Warner were suspended for a year, and Bancroft for nine months.

There was a strong feeling at the time that they were ‘taking one for the team’. That others, starting with the bowlers, must have known. But when in 2021 Bancroft intimated that in an interview, the four bowlers most under suspicion – skipper Pat Cummins, Josh Hazlewood, Mitchell Starc and Nathan Lyon – put out a very strong statement, denying knowledge. And that is where the matter rested until a fortnight ago when Usman Khawaja released his autobiography and wrote in reference to Cape Town, “It was all of us.” He was quickly joined by his childhood friend Warner who said, everything Khawaja said was indeed the truth.

Suddenly, all eyes were on the skipper, Cummins. Here were two of his senior players saying the bowlers, led by him, were – and there’s no other way of putting this, even if they didn’t use the word – liars.

Would Cummins defend himself, his fellow bowlers, the integrity of Australian cricket, the game itself?

Would he what!

Cummins held an open press conference and invited everyone with a microphone or typewriter to attend. He took all questions and no prisoners.

“That’s not the Usman that I know,” was his opening salvo. “It’s not the Usman that I think that has been part of a team that’s helped rebuild the Australian cricket culture over the last eight years ... Usman’s a friend, but friends aren’t casual with each other’s lives and reputations. In his book, Uzi’s been casual with my life and the lives of many others ...”

To my mind, Cummins was singularly impressive.

“Going into that press conference,” I wrote in the Herald, “it was clear to me that Cummins was going to come out blinking in the sunlight and defensive. It was inconceivable to me that Khawaja had got it completely wrong. Had he said that ‘it was all of them,’ who knew, then yes, there is room for doubt. But he didn’t. He said, ‘It was all of US.’

“But against that, Cummins could not have looked more like an honest, outraged man, nor been more emphatic. To my eyes he looked credible. It was a very strong exercise in leadership from a captain exerting his authority.”

Again, that is where the matter rested until ...

Until Wednesday evening, when Bancroft burst forth with 4000 words of his own account, naming names, backing Khawaja and Warner and saying not only everyone knew, but in the whole setup one voice was raised against it, a media manager by the name of Kate Hutchison.

In the wake of Bancroft’s purging of his very soul – for that’s what it read like, the truth that had been hidden for eight years – Australia reeled once more in hurt, in shock, confused.

Phones pinged across the country in a chorus of calamity, including one from Andrew Denton to this correspondent: “I don’t know about you, but this leaves me feeling both unsurprised and gut-punched. Apparently there are now three things you should never see being made: A sausage. The law. And the decisions of Australia’s cricket team.”

That is indeed the guts of it.

Where to from here? The whole thing brings to mind that famous quote from Edward R. Murrow: “Anyone who isn’t confused really doesn’t understand the situation.”

What is clear is that the most recent approach of Cricket Australia to hold a forum where the players of 2018 are welcome to come forward and tell their truths – in the words of its chief executive Todd Greenberg, “I think Khawaja and Cummins’s account can both be true because it’s their version of events ... their version of events is their truth” – won’t cut it. The obvious point must be made. There are no truths, just the truth.

And CA’s previous reluctance to reopen old wounds by holding an inquiry this far down the track is equally beside the point. Those old wounds are not only open again, they’re gushing out the life-blood of the game.

The only way forward is indeed to conduct a full-blown inquiry, by an independent entity, where everyone involved with the 2018 team is required to give their testimony if they still want selection. Let that entity release the unvarnished report of the independent inquiry to the public within three months of the inquiry closing.

Let us, finally, totally, get what should have happened in 2018, but clearly didn’t: THE truth.

I’d be surprised if Cummins can survive in the captaincy until such times as that inquiry is conducted – and Travis Head will likely take over – but this thing is so fast-moving and so changing in narrative every day, who knows?

There is one major player who is yet to break cover.

Where are you Steve Smith? A nation turns its lonely eyes to you.
