The energy major's stock has gained sharply over the past year, and the rally changes how its full decade looks. Put $1,000 into Chevron (NYSE:CVX) ten years ago, reinvest every dividend, and you'd now hold more than three times your money. Even so, you'd still trail the S&P 500.

Oil Crashes, Oil Spikes and a Hess-Sized Push for Scale

Chevron works across the whole energy chain. Upstream, it finds and produces oil and gas. Downstream, it refines, markets and makes chemicals. Results swing with crude prices. The 2020 COVID crash hit the whole industry hard, though Chevron kept paying its dividend. Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022 then brought a boom.

Chevron closed its Hess acquisition in 2025. That year production hit a record 3,723 MBOED, up 12%. In 2026, military action that began February 28 effectively shut the Strait of Hormuz, and Brent averaged $117 per barrel in April. Second-quarter revenue rose 51.4% to $67.2B. Over the whole decade, the quarterly dividend climbed from $1.07 to $1.78.

Timing drove almost everything here. The 5-year window starts in October 2021, just before the 2022 surge, and Chevron beat the index easily over that period. The 10-year holder sat through the 2020 collapse and still fell behind a benchmark that wasn't even credited with its dividends. Reinvested payouts did much of the work. Without them, Chevron's decade would look much weaker.

Why Chevron's Dividend Case Matters Most Right Now

Chevron's case today depends on income that holds up across the oil cycle. Investors focused on riding the current crude spike could face more risk.

Bull case: The CEO says Hess assets generate free cash flow "roughly double the incremental dividends." A 20-year Microsoft (NASDAQ:MSFT) power deal adds cash flow that doesn't depend on commodity prices. Chevron also bases its 2030 targets on oil prices below current levels.

Bear case: Today's earnings depend on a Hormuz disruption that won't last forever. The stock trades at $217.22, right near its 52-week high of $217.78.

The income case looks stronger than the price-gain case. A decade of crashes and spikes still turned $1,000 into $3,276 for patient holders who reinvested. Energy majors reward holders who stay through a full cycle.
