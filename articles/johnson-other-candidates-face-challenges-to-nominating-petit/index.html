Johnson, Other Candidates Face Challenges To Nominating Petitions For Mayor's Race

TheTools · 02.11.2026 23:38 · source · permalink

Credibility:

  • Original Reporting
  • On the Ground
  • Sources Cited

DOWNTOWN — Mayor Brandon Johnson and many of his challengers running for mayor next year are facing formal objections to the validity of their nominating petitions, which every candidate must file to gain a spot on the Feb. 23 ballot.

Other mayoral candidates who had their candidacy challenged on Monday for various reasons — not just faulty petition signatures — include outgoing Illinois comptroller Susana Mendoza, Secretary of State Alexi Giannoulias, U.S. Rep. Mike Quigley, Cook County Treasurer Maria Pappas, lobbyist John Kelly, attorney William Quinlan and several lesser-known candidates, according to a Chicago Board of Elections official.

If substantiated, the challenges could get the candidates knocked out of the mayor’s race.

Rickey Hendon, the former state senator and longtime advisor to perennial mayoral candidate Willie Wilson, filed the challenge to Johnson’s signatures Monday afternoon at the Board of Elections.

Hendon is alleging that while Johnson last month submitted more than 30,000 signatures to the elections board, most of them are illegitimate and should not be counted.

Candidates must clear a threshold of at least 12,500 valid signatures to make it on the ballot for mayor.

“We found a number of problems. One was the [City Treasurer] Melissa Conyers-Ervin’s petitions were mixed in with his. The other was a large number of photocopies, over 1,000 signatures of photocopies. The law says you have to file originals. They are not originals. They’re photocopies,” Hendon said Monday. “Then there were a number of problems with various circulators within the mayor’s filing. It was very sloppy, very messy, done unprofessionally.”

The challenge is the first against an incumbent Chicago mayor since 2007, when former Mayor Richard M. Daley’s petitions faced an unsuccessful objection, according to elections board data.

Since news of a potential challenge circulated last week, Johnson has projected confidence that he will overcome any attempt to knock him off the ballot.

“I feel absolutely confident that I’ll be able to withstand any challenges if any of those actually arise. But you know, look, we know that this system is antiquated, but I’m clear that I have more than enough signatures that are needed to secure my spot on the ballot,” Johnson said Friday, according to ABC 7.

That was echoed by the mayor’s political director, Christian Perry, in a text message Monday before the challenge was filed.

“The campaign feels confident that if and when a challenge materializes we have more than enough signatures to withstand a challenge,” Perry said.

The deadline to file petition challenges for the Feb. 23 election was 5 p.m. Monday.

The full list of challenges will not be made public on the Board of Elections website until Wednesday, after Tuesday’s midterm general election, a spokesperson said.

Michael Dorf, a longtime Chicago election attorney working for Giannoulias, told reporters on Monday that he filed challenges to Quinlan’s petitions, as well as two candidates who submitted not nearly enough signatures to get on the ballot last month: Jessica “Jesse” Maronto and John Kitover.

Quinlan’s signatures are “bad,” Dorf said. “Not registered, not genuine, many duplicates. Just not a good set.”

Meanwhile, Quinlan is challenging the candidacy of Giannoulias, Pappas and Quigley in what is his latest attempt to get them knocked off the ballot for running for two offices at the same time. Quinlan has argued doing so is now illegal due to a recent change to the municipal election calendar— although a Cook County judge dismissed a lawsuit he filed last month that also challenged the candidates’ eligibility for that reason.

“These public offices aren’t their priorities. They’re their safety nets,” Quinlan said in a statement on Monday. “Having just recently filed to run for mayor of Chicago, they’re now asking voters to retain their current jobs as a backup plan in case Chicago says ‘no.'”

When asked about Quinlan’s challenge of Giannoulias’ petitions, Dorf, his attorney, called the effort “totally frivolous.”

The nature of the challenge against Kelly’s candidacy wasn’t immediately clear, but based on his campaign’s public response Monday, it seems likely to be over his residency and whether he lives in Chicago, not the suburbs.

“Chicago has been John’s legal residence since October 2024. He bought his condo on East Bellevue in August 2024, two and a half years before this election,” his campaign said in a statement. “Kelly changed his driver’s license and voter registration to that address and voted in Chicago in the 2024 presidential election.”

In a statement, Mendoza’s campaign said they were unfazed by the challenge.

“We are fully confident in the quality of the signatures collected and that Susana has more than the amount required to make the ballot,” the statement said.

Petition challenges are a fixture of Chicago elections during every cycle, as some candidates seek to bounce potential rivals off the ballot for various reasons — a process critics have called undemocratic.

The challenges filed on Monday will be now heard by hearing officers through the elections board, which will then issue decisions in each case later this year.

Sixteen candidates filed petitions to run for mayor by last week’s deadline: Johnson, Giannoulias, Kelly, Mendoza, Pappas, Quigley, Quinlan, Wilson,Maronto, Kitover, businessman Matt Brewer, former alderman George Cardenas, conservative organizer Danielle Carter-Walters, businessman Joe Holberg, cardiologist Lisa Nee and Zoe Ma.
