China gives Japan PM frosty welcome at Apec amid fanfare for other leaders

TheTools · 19.11.2026 03:05 · source · permalink

Sanae Takaichi cuts lonely figure at airport, a move by China that is being linked to the simmering row over Taiwan

Apec’s Chinese hosts went out of their way to give world leaders a warm welcome as they arrived in Shenzhen this week. Military bands played, dozens of cheering young men and women waved colourful bouquets, and senior officials offered hugs and handshakes on the tarmac.

But there was one exception: Japan’s prime minister, Sanae Takaichi.

The red carpet was rolled out for her arrival at Shenzhen Bao’an International Airport, but Takaichi, on her first visit to China since taking office just over a year ago, had to make do with a modest guard of honour and a welcome from a relatively junior Chinese government official.

The contrast in hospitality was impossible to ignore. Donald Trump was personally greeted by Chinese leader Xi Jinping as he stepped off Air Force One, while Vladimir Putin was met by Cai Qi, a senior Communist party official and one of Xi’s closest aides.

The Australian prime minister, Anthony Albanese, beamed as he walked past rows of women in white shirts and pink skirts while soldiers from the People’s Liberation Army stood to attention, bayonets fixed. South Korea’s president, Lee Jae Myung, and the country’s first lady, Kim Hea Kyung, were given similar VIP treatment.

Takaichi, on the other hand, was met off her plane by Zhang Qiyue, China’s former ambassador to Greece, a position below those who greeted other leaders. Zhang, though, does have a connection with Japan: her father, Zhang Shu, was once China’s ambassador in Tokyo.

Many are linking China’s frosty welcome to a simmering row over Taiwan that began shortly after Takaichi made her diplomatic debut at the 2025 Apec summit in South Korea, where she and Xi vowed to build “constructive and stable” relations.

Less than a fortnight later, Takaichi triggered a diplomatic crisis when she told MPs that a Chinese attack on Taiwan could trigger the deployment of her country’s self-defence forces if the conflict posed an existential threat to Japan, whose westernmost island, Yonaguni, is just 100km from Taiwan.

Insisting that Japan could exercise its right to collective self-defence – or coming to the aid of an ally, namely the US – Takaichi said Tokyo had to plan for a crisis in the Taiwan strait.

Beijing has not ruled out the use of force to annex Taiwan – a self-governing democracy it regards as a Chinese province – under what it calls “reunification”.

China’s foreign ministry condemned her remarks at the time as “wrongful and dangerous,” while online, many accused Japan’s leader of dragging her country back into prewar militarism.

The dispute soured economic and cultural ties. Beijing warned Chinese citizens not to take holidays or study in Japan, claiming, without evidence, that they would face threats to their safety. Grassroots exchanges were called off and the Chinese releases of Japanese films postponed indefinitely.

Panda diplomacy also took a hit in January, when Xiao Xiao and Lei Lei, a pair of giant pandas that had been kept at Ueno Zoo in Tokyo, departed Japan, leaving the country without one of the animals for the first time since 1972, when Tokyo and Beijing normalised diplomatic ties.

As Xi, Trump and Putin prepared to hold historic three-way talks in Shenzhen on Thursday, Takaichi managed only a cordial handshake and a few words with Xi – another reminder that Japan-China relations have yet to come in from the cold.
