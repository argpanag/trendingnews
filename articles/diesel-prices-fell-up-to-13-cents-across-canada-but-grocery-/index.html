Diesel prices fell up to 13 cents across Canada, but grocery bills won't follow anytime soon — here's why

TheTools · 07.11.2026 09:47 · source · permalink

Roger Spicer figured he saved around $60 filling up his work truck at a truck stop in Enfield, Nova Scotia on Wednesday. Diesel in much of the eastern province dropped 13 cents overnight to 256.5 cents a litre, and drivers in other provinces saw similar cuts at the pump.

It's tempting to connect the dots. Diesel moves the food Canadians eat from farms and ports to store shelves, so cheaper fuel should translate to cheaper groceries. But food researchers say the savings won't show up at the checkout right away — and may not show up at all if fuel prices climb again.

The gap between the pump and the grocery aisle comes down to contracts, seasons and a number of pressures that have nothing to do with diesel. Here's what to watch as you plan your food budget this winter.

Why did diesel prices drop so suddenly?

The Nova Scotia Energy Board, which regulates fuel prices in the province, made an unscheduled cut on Wednesday, citing a significant shift in market prices.

In the Greater Toronto Area, diesel fell 11 cents to 235.9 cents a litre, a level not seen since early September, according to Dan McTeague of Canadians for Affordable Energy, an advocacy group focused on fuel costs.

So what's changed? Carol Montreuil, vice-president with the Canadian Fuels Association, an industry group representing Canada's fuel refiners and distributors, told CTV News that crude oil prices have fallen on expectations for an end to the wars involving Iran and Ukraine, though neither conflict has been resolved.

Why won't grocery prices fall as fast as diesel?

Will cheaper diesel mean a lower grocery bill this month? Probably not. Sylvain Charlebois, a food researcher and professor at Dalhousie University, told CTV News that the food being hauled to stores today is moving based on contracts signed months ago. Fuel prices, he said, "need to remain low for a while" before shoppers see the benefit.

Michael Mulvey, a marketing professor at the University of Ottawa, made a similar point to The Canadian Press back in October: many transportation contracts are set months in advance, and prices that rise with costs often take longer to come back down.
