Private ADHD clinics face crackdown

TheTools · 26.09.2026 01:17 · source · permalink

Private ADHD clinics face a clampdown amid warnings that some providers are diagnosing almost every patient they assess.

A major government review is expected to call for tighter oversight of independent providers following their rapid expansion as NHS waiting lists have soared.

More than half of ADHD assessments funded by the NHS are now carried out by independent providers, according to industry estimates, with costs of up to £3,000 per case.

The review, led by Prof Peter Fonagy, is expected to raise concerns about the quality and consistency of some private assessments, and call for tighter regulation.

Rates of ADHD diagnosis in England have more than doubled since 2021.

The report is expected to warn that rates show no sign of peaking and could soon result in significant overdiagnosis.

However, it will acknowledge that some of the rise reflects historic underdiagnosis among some groups, particularly women and girls.

The private sector has expanded rapidly as patients facing lengthy NHS waits have increasingly turned to alternative providers.

One GP said they had never seen a private provider reject an ADHD diagnosis. Another said just one patient among more than 1,000 they had referred had not been diagnosed with the condition, in a survey by GP magazine Pulse.

The review is expected to propose a fundamental overhaul of the way patients seeking help for ADHD, autism and mental health problems are assessed.

It will call for an end to fragmented pathways which can leave patients bouncing between separate services in search of help.

And it recommends that teams consider whether difficulties could reflect ADHD, autism, mental health problems or a combination of conditions before deciding what specialist help is needed.

Those most in need would be prioritised for specialist assessment and treatment, while others could receive support without first obtaining a diagnosis.

The report is expected to say help at school or work should not depend on being given a diagnostic label.

Prof Fonagy’s interim report warned that services organised around “single diagnostic categories” could result in “fragmented assessment and misdirected referral”.

It said a more integrated approach could provide earlier help while ensuring specialist assessments were targeted at those who most needed them.

The review has also examined the role of social media in increasing self-identification and demand for assessments, even without any change in underlying prevalence.

Its interim findings highlighted concerns about misinformation on TikTok, citing research which found 52 per cent of ADHD-related videos were inaccurate.

Another study found just 21 per cent of popular ADHD videos were considered “useful” according to clinical standards.

Researchers said greater awareness could help people who were previously overlooked, while also influencing the threshold at which people identify themselves as having ADHD.

Cost of ADHD ‘running out of control’

The rapid growth of independent provision has raised concerns about the escalating bill for the NHS.

NHS leaders have warned that the costs of meeting soaring demand for ADHD and autism services are “running out of control” and becoming unsustainable. Spending in some areas has risen as much as tenfold in a year.

The NHS Alliance has warned that the cost of ADHD and autism services is running “out of control”, with one NHS body paying between £300 and £3,000 for the same type of assessment.

Prof Fonagy’s interim report warned that private provision could increase capacity without necessarily making the system more coherent.

It said long waiting lists were driving greater use of private and Right to Choose services, potentially contributing to fragmented care and widening inequalities.

The interim review also identified the need for more evidence about diagnosis rates and “provider-level practice” among private and Right to Choose services.

Dr Liz Clarke, a Greater Manchester GP partner, said ADHD assessments should be integrated into wider mental health services.

She warned that separate pathways could miss overlapping problems, and result in inappropriate medication or psychological treatment.

“We should not have to pay twice for what should be the same assessment,” she said.

Prof Fonagy previously warned that existing services are “slow, fragmented and difficult to navigate”.

His interim report said diagnosis had increasingly become the gateway to support, even when it might not be the most appropriate first step.

Prof Fonagy said: “When support is tied tightly to diagnostic labels, demand for diagnosis will rise.”

Prof Fonagy also warned of rising distress among young people, rejecting suggestions that a “snowflake generation” was behind the increase.

However, his research explores concerns about an increasing tendency to medicalise forms of distress which may have broader “social or developmental roots”.

The review was commissioned amid wider government concern about rising economic inactivity and growing numbers of young people claiming health and disability benefits.

Recommended

Claims for Personal Independence Payment (PIP), the main disability benefit, among teenagers with ADHD and autism have more than tripled since 2019, with the greatest rises seen in the most affluent areas.

More than 51,000 16- and 17-year-olds received PIP for the conditions in 2025, compared with around 16,000 six years earlier.

The review will not make recommendations on welfare reform. However, its findings are expected to feed into Alan Milburn’s separate review of the growing number of young people not in education, employment or training.

David Hare, chief executive of the Independent Healthcare Provider Network (IHPN), said: “We recognise the need for urgent improvements in NHS ADHD and autism services, and IHPN are calling for a national framework to ensure consistent access and quality wherever patients live, underpinned by a common service specification linked to fair tariff pricing for all providers, including the NHS, while protecting patients’ legal Right to Choose.

“With 83 per cent of IHPN members delivering ADHD and Autism assessments rated Good or Outstanding by the CQC, independent providers are a vital part of ensuring patients can access high-quality services and the support they need.”
