What Leo XIV’s Cardinal Salary Decision Really Means

TheTools · 24.09.2026 14:47 · source · permalink

COMMENTARY: There will be no real return to ‘normality’ — not if ‘normal’ means ‘the way things were before Francis.’

The news that Leo XIV has decided to revoke his predecessor’s salary cuts for cardinals has had a greater symbolic impact than one might imagine.

During the COVID-19 pandemic and the economic crisis, Pope Francis cut cardinals’ salaries — the so-called “cardinals’ plate” — and the measure certainly made headlines.

Pope Leo’s decision can lead to only two conclusions in the debate: either the Pope has brought things back to normal, with the emergency period having ended, or the Pope is simply taking his own approach and reestablishing the cardinals’ privileges.

These two readings, however, probably don’t fully explain what is happening today.

Leo is not a man of radical change, and he certainly doesn’t want to position himself as a pope who has broken continuity with past popes, especially not his immediate predecessor, Francis.

He is, however, showing himself to be a pope with his own pragmatism, which allows him to “adjust” certain measures.

Francis, in fact, didn’t just cut the cardinals’ salaries. He also ordered everyone to pay market-rate rent for the apartments they received by virtue of their office, effectively conveying that he wanted to strip cardinals of their privileges and stay close to the people, even in their needs.

Although these choices made a significant impact in the media, they also revealed a desire to make Church leaders scapegoats for all evils.

The Vatican system, in fact, was not created to enrich or distribute privileges. Rather, it was created to allow everyone who works in the Vatican to live a dignified life.

Vatican salaries are not particularly high, and neither is a cardinal’s salary.

Cardinals receive around 5,000 euros a month, but this amount covers housing expenses, salaries for those who help the cardinals maintain their households, and expenses for official travel. Basically, it is a sort of per diem allowance, and not a particularly high one, given to each cardinal by virtue of his function.

The Vatican has amassed a wealth of real estate over the years, managed primarily by the Administration of the Patrimony of the Apostolic See (APSA) or the Dicastery for Evangelization, but it also has properties belonging to the Papal Household, for example.

This real estate has been leased out and used to generate revenue. It has also been rented to Vatican employees at prices that are generally below market value. This has allowed employees with modest salaries — even if tax-free — to have decent housing.

When a prelate is called to Rome, he is assigned an office apartment, free to use, because of his role. This allows the prelate to avoid additional expenses for a task he hadn’t requested but had been entrusted with.

The Vatican’s economic system is designed to help everyone live a dignified life. The Vatican supermarket, for example, was created to provide employees with a place to shop at affordable prices, and the Vatican shopping center, located inside what was once the small city-state’s train station, serves the same purpose.

Furthermore, the Vatican has no taxes. Salaries, therefore, are received net, and similarly, Vatican gas stations benefit from the absence of Italian state taxes. But only Vatican employees can benefit from these advantages, so they do not create unfair competition with the Italian state. They do, however, provide an opportunity to live better and to give meaning to their Vatican presence.

Under Pope Francis, these “conveniences” gradually disappeared. Real estate was earmarked for profit, and in some cases its management was outsourced to external companies. The Vatican supermarket, the Annona, has been outsourced to an Italian supermarket chain.

Last but not least, Francis tried to overcome the economic crisis by cutting the cardinals’ daily allowances, effectively putting a sort of target on their backs.

Leo XIV has begun to reverse this trend, starting with the salary cuts. Additionally, the Pope has abolished the freeze on seniority increases, also introduced under Pope Francis. But the salary issue goes hand in hand with another fact: the Vatican’s finances are not in such dire straits as the latest financial reports of Pope Francis’ pontificate suggested.

Debts were said to be 200 million euros, but in reality the deficit is 70 million euros. Leo XIV overruled Pope Francis’ request to invest only through the IOR (the Institute for the Works of Religion, colloquially known as the “Vatican bank”), and he closed the Donations Commission Francis established, if only because the Holy See already has departments responsible for collecting donations.

However, this return to normality involves small adjustments, not a change to the overall structure. The Holy See still relies on outsourcing — for example, the governorate has entrusted the management of procurement tenders to Consip — and APSA still aims to hoard real estate assets. In fact, Leo has decided not to change economic policy, leaving Cardinal Reinhard Marx at the helm of the Council for the Economy for a third term.

In short, Leo hasn’t launched any revolutions, and he probably won’t.

He has simply made a practical adjustment, which tells the world that, in fact, the Vatican system isn’t terrible. Rather, it must be defended and supported. Even with rules in place, there is always the possibility of corruption.

The Pope is thus sending signals. He is making small adjustments, but he has not yet taken a definitive direction, and he is probably still learning all the ins and outs of the Holy See and its administration.

Moreover, Leo still hasn’t created his own team, and it is unlikely he will replace anyone not due for replacement. Prior to his election, he served in the Curia for two years, during which he maintained cordial professional relationships with his colleagues. It’s difficult to remove someone who has always treated you well, regardless of their suitability for the position.

These choices could also mean the Pope is asking everyone who was part of Francis’ era to take responsibility, repay debts, or correct mistakes made under the previous administration.

In fact, there will be no real return to “normality” — not if “normal” means “the way things were before Francis” — nor will Leo interrupt a process already underway, whether the financial reforms we’ve discussed or the synodal reform, which is always on everyone’s lips.

Leo XIV will simply adjust his focus, overcome some populist choices, and seek to build unity in the Church.

It will not be easy.
