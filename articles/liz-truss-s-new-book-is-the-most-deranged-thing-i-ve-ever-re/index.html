Liz Truss’s new book is the most deranged thing I’ve ever read

TheTools · 27.10.2026 07:30 · source · permalink

Like an alcoholic who cannot admit they have a drinking problem, the former PM is convinced that everyone else is to blame for her failures

0/5

On the morning of Friday Sept 23, 2022, my wife and I put the television on and watched Kwasi Kwarteng, the Chancellor of the Exchequer, deliver a mini-budget that would remove him from office three weeks later, and the prime minister, Liz Truss, shortly after that. Kwarteng announced a £45bn package of tax cuts designed to jolt Britain out of “managed decline”. At the end of his statement I feared I must have nodded off for a moment, and asked my wife whether I had missed him announcing the public-spending cuts required to fund this kick-start. “He didn’t,” she said.

That was what horrified the bond markets, with fatal results for the Truss administration. But in Bank Job, her preposterous book-length attempt to justify the policy – an attempt reminiscent of the worst sort of saloon-bar bore, ranting in a corner while other patrons realise it’s time to go home – Truss never concedes she was remotely at fault. The fundamental lesson she has failed to learn is that people vote for politicians to run the country, and get annoyed when they do it badly. When they do it catastrophically badly and try to blame everyone but themselves, voters (who are not that stupid) get very angry indeed. She was, and remains, the author of her own misfortune.

“It’s the story of the power of an elite in Britain that has run the country into the ground,” she cries. “It’s a story of unelected bureaucrats who are more powerful than those meant to be in charge.” She seems particularly cross that Andrew Bailey, the Governor of the Bank of England, is, unlike her, “unsackable” – for he is her scapegoat-in-chief.

There are other culprits. Journalists (always ripe for a kicking) failed to ask “awkward questions” about “unaccountable yet powerful state institutions”, notably the Bank. “Most journalists do not understand economics, let alone monetary policy, and accepted the line they had been fed,” she says with her customary infallibility.

The line was about “unfunded tax cuts”. She makes the remarkable claim that “every tax cut is unfunded”. She was not quite four years old at the time, so must be forgiven for ignoring the 1979 Budget in which Sir Geoffrey Howe cut income tax but paid for it by almost doubling VAT. There was no such balancing act in 2022.

Therefore, others must be blamed: Andrew Bailey; the Treasury; Michael Gove; the mass media and especially the commentariat who “simply parrot a few phrases and treat economic happenings as a personnel drama”. She adds: “The Bank of England rug-pulled the elected government of the day because it didn’t agree with its policies.” The “rug-pulling” was, she proclaims, the Bank’s decision to sell £80bn of gilts the day before that disastrous mini-budget.

“No one in the incoming government was warned. I was not briefed. There was no attempt to co-ordinate the move with the fiscal announcement we had scheduled for the next day.” In fact, the Bank had announced a programme of selling gilts on Sept 1, 2022, five days before Truss kissed hands. Because of the chaos caused by her Budget – the details of which were not shared with the Bank beforehand – the gilts sale duly proposed on Sept 22 was delayed until Nov 1.

Truss does not allow these facts to get in the way. Instead, she says that a “fake establishment narrative” grew up, “ignoring the culpability of the Bank of England and its role in bringing down the Government”. What had occurred was “a constitutional scandal” in which the then-prime minister had, it seems, no power at all, but which the media and indeed many in her own party and among the public simply ignored. By Monday, Sept 26, she writes, “the political class decided the story”. Wasn’t she, by definition, leader of that class?

She also asserts that “the Governor of the Bank of England – through the Cabinet Secretary and the Treasury – put pressure on me over both the appointment of a new Permanent Secretary to the Treasury and also about reversing budget decisions”, which if remotely true would simply be another sign of the incapability of a prime minister to do the job. After all, what about the role of Liz Truss, First Lord of the Treasury, in bringing down her own administration?

This innocent bystander was a victim of events she was powerless to stop, cruelly persecuted not just by Bailey, but by “Tory traitors who were more interested in being in office than saving Britain”. The country was in a mess, she admits, because of the administration in which she had happily served for the previous decade: yet she makes no reference to her collective responsibility. Few politicians do humility, but this is parodic. She says “the Treasury and the OBR bear responsibility for Britain’s appalling economic record… most politicians are simply their puppets”. If she was a puppet, whose fault was that?

In 40 years of writing about Westminster, I have learnt that governments get the officials they deserve. Civil servants instinctively smell weakness and ruthlessly exploit it until they lose the argument, after which they behave (Mrs Thatcher was a model minister in this respect). If Truss felt the Treasury was conspiring against her, she should have worked strategically to bring it to heel: I presume on her Oxford PPE course she learnt the maxim that “civil servants advise: ministers decide”. She just wasn’t up to enforcing that code.

If she didn’t like the independence of the Bank of England, she should have brought a Bill before Parliament to reverse it before her impetuous experiment in fiscal policy. And if she felt so strongly about the OBR (which she claims is a conspiracy of Leftists), she should have abolished that too. Oddly, she appears to have spent a decade at the heart of government and spotted none of these “problems” until it was too late.

Truss is clearly deeply wounded after her term as prime minister. It can be no fun to consider that, despite a serious effort from her predecessor Boris Johnson, she is widely regarded (and not just by the people she so despises) as the worst premier in the 305-year history of that office. Yet all this book proves is that she is now like the alcoholic who cannot admit his or her drink problem: and until she does there is no hope for her.

Of all the books I have ever reviewed that purported to offer a serious thesis, this is without question the most deranged. If you are seeking a Christmas present for someone you really don’t like, give them a copy.

Bank Job is published by Free Speech Press at £8.99
