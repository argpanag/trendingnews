Hegseth reportedly moves to restrict defence ties with Canada, but Ottawa sees no change | CBC News

TheTools · 22.11.2026 02:58 · source · permalink

Classified Pentagon directive raises questions about future Canada-U.S. military co-operation

U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth has reportedly issued a classified directive restricting military co-operation between the United States and Canada — something that Canadian government and military officials were not aware of and have seen no evidence it has been put into practice.

The Washington Post reported late Friday that the directive was circulated last week in a secret memorandum. The report cited U.S. officials familiar with the decision.

Three Canadian defence officials speaking on background on Saturday said they were aware of the reports and were looking to substantiate them, as well as seeking clarification because there's been no change at the military operational level between the two countries.

A Republican senator attending the Halifax International Security Forum confirmed the existence of the memo and the restrictions but downplayed their impact, saying they have no effect on operations between the two countries.

"It does limit general flag officer meetings to those that advance [U.S. Department of War] objectives," said Sen. Mike Rounds, who sits on the armed services committee.

U.S. defence officials — uniform and civilian — must seek permission before attending social events or think-tank conferences.

The measures were drafted months ago but only signed recently, Rounds said, adding that it's unfortunate they were leaked.

The intent, however, was to put pressure on Canada.

U.S. Sen. Mike Rounds speaks to reporters in the U.S. Capitol Building on Monday in Washington, D.C. Rounds on Saturday confirmed the existence of Hegseth's memo and the restrictions, but downplayed their impact. (Finn Gomez/Getty Images)

"Look, we really want you to bring up your defence spending," Rounds said, before quickly adding the U.S. delegation at the conference had a chance to be briefed on the latest measures.

"We just had a chance to talk with our Canadian friends, and they indicated and laid out the roadmap that they're using to significantly increase their defence spending."

Democratic Sen. Chris Coons of Delaware says he was irritated by Hegseth's directive and its impact on relations with Canada.

"This is a completely unnecessary insertion of a negative tone," said Coons, who is also in Halifax and sits on the Senate foreign relations committee.

"I'm relieved this has no significant operational impact, but at the end of the day, as we've got our adversaries globally pulling together and working more closely — Iran, North Korea, China, Russia — the United States should be doing nothing to further aggravate some of the distance and division between the United States and Canada."

Defence Minister David McGuinty, who is also at the security forum, said the two countries continue to work "hand-in-glove" on various missions such as stopping the flow of illegal drugs in the Caribbean and on new policy initiatives such as integrated air and missile defence.

"It's the first time we heard of this — just yesterday," McGuinty said, referring to the directive. "It's clear there's some sort of document we haven't seen. I'm not aware of the contents of it."

Minister of National Defence David McGuinty rises during question period in the House of Commons on Parliament Hill in Ottawa on Nov. 3. McGuinty says Canada and the U.S. continue to work 'hand-in-glove' on various military missions and new policy initiatives. (Justin Tang/The Canadian Press)

There was no mention of the directive from the Americans at the security forum dinner Friday night, according to two of the confidential defence sources, one of whom wondered if the congressmen and senators were even aware of it. 

CBC News is not identifying the sources because they were not authorized to speak publicly.

In an interview with CBC's Adrienne Arsenault, McGuinty said he has not yet contacted Hegseth over the issue. He said Canadian officials are reaching out to U.S. counterparts on a host of security-related issues.

Asked if he thinks there were certain issues in Canada-U.S. relations that may have precipitated the memo, McGuinty declined to speculate, noting Ottawa has learned to take the often unexpected decisions of the Trump administration in stride.

"There have been curveballs of this kind," McGuinty said of the sudden nature of the directive. 

"We've learned to move forward."

The existence of such a directive would only add to mounting tensions between the long-standing allies over trade and defence spending. 

Canada, U.S. working on trade deal

The move comes as the Trump administration and the government of Prime Minister Mark Carney inch toward a trade deal that could see some relief in key sectors such as automotive and steel and aluminum.

The military and defence ties between Canada and the U.S. are deep, long-standing and would be difficult to restrict at an operational level without harming the security of the U.S. itself in one fashion or another, said one of the sources.

The two countries co-operate in the defence of North America through NORAD. In Europe, they are founding partners of NATO and work side by side with the other 30 members of the Western alliance. They also work together in the Indo-Pacific in joint military exercises.

A third source said it would also be difficult and counter-productive for Washington to, for example, restrict access to U.S.-made weapons systems and equipment, which Washington has been eager to sell and pressuring Canada to buy.

Officially, the scope of Hegseth's restrictions is unclear, according to the Washington Post report. Since the memo is classified, the Department of War has not identified which military programs or activities might be affected.

In a statement to CBC News prior to Rounds's comments, the Pentagon's chief spokesperson, Sean Parnell, ruled out restrictions on operational level matters, but was vague on what precise impact the memo would have.

"The Department of War is taking steps to ensure its engagements with Canada advance tangible U.S. defence objectives and ultimately support a reinvigorated defence relationship," Parnell said in a written response.

"DoW is prioritizing opportunities for bilateral cooperation that strengthens the defence of our Homelands and shared hemisphere, while supporting Canada's ability to meet NATO obligations. This guidance does not impact defence intelligence co-operation, our binational NORAD command or our co-operation through NATO."

The response left open questions about bilateral military exercises, training exchanges, defence planning and other forms of co-operation.

But even on that level, Canadian officials say they've seen no evidence thus far of any restrictions and point to the notion that the memo has been in circulation for a week.

Pentagon withdraws from Canada-U.S. defence board

Last spring, the Trump administration paused U.S. participation in the Permanent Joint Board on Defence (PJBD) with Canada. 

Halting the activity of the decades-old board, a bilateral advisory group that co-ordinates continental security and recommends defence policy to both governments, was considered largely symbolic because it met infrequently.

Canada has been routinely criticized in Washington over its lacklustre defence spending.

There has been change under Carney, and Canada has now hit the NATO target of spending two per cent of GDP.

Carney, however, has publicly stated he wants to diversify both the country's trading relationship and where it sources its military equipment. He has said it's not good for more than 70 cents of defence equipment dollars to be going to one market — the United States.
