EVs are more reliable than petrol cars in the long run

TheTools · 22.09.2026 14:36 · source · permalink

Electric vehicles (EVs) are more reliable than their petrol counterparts as they gain more miles on the clock, according to a new analysis.

A study of 47.4 million tests found that for vehicles below 60,000 miles there was little difference in performance between the two fuel types at similar ages and mileages.

But after 90,000 to 120,000 miles they diverged significantly, with electric cars 25pc less likely than petrol ones to fail their MOT.

The finding by consultancy New Automotive and industry group the British Vehicle Rental and Leasing Association (BVRLA) comes as there are growing numbers of electric cars on sale in the second-hand market.

Toby Poston, the BVRLA’s chief executive, said electric cars tended to fail less at higher mileages because they had “far fewer moving parts” and were therefore less exposed to faults or wear and tear.

He added: “There’s no combustion engine, no exhaust system and no conventional gearbox, so you remove a lot of the mechanical complexity and wear that traditionally starts to matter as a car racks up the miles.

“What is fascinating about this research is that the difference becomes clearer as mileage increases.

“That suggests the simplicity of the electric drivetrain may become an advantage over the longer term.”

He cautioned that the findings did not mean “EVs are maintenance-free” but that they “challenge the old assumption that a car with 80,000 or 100,000 miles on the clock must be approaching the end of its useful life”.

For cars of similar ages with between 90,000 and 120,000 miles on the clock, only 16.5pc of electric models failed their MOT compared with 22.1pc of petrol cars.

That meant the high-mileage electric cars were about 25pc less likely to fail, the BVRLA said.

The research was carried out by New Automotive and commissioned by the BVRLA.

It is believed to be one of the first such large-scale studies of MOT data, with research on the longevity of EVs still scarce because of the relatively short time they have been commonplace on British roads.

New Automotive’s study also found that mileage was a better predictor of performance, as opposed to age. A typical petrol car has clocked up 20,499 miles before its first MOT at three years old – compared with 27,072 for an electric.

Ben Nelmes, of New Automotive, added: “A simple comparison of MOT pass rates tells you very little because Britain’s electric cars are much younger than its petrol fleet, while comparing cars of the same age misses the fact that EVs have generally travelled further.

“Once we compare like with like, something striking happens. At lower mileages there is little between electric and petrol cars, but as the miles build the electric cars increasingly pull ahead.”

However, New Automotive stressed that its analysis could only account for roadworthiness, not battery condition.

An MOT examines a car’s brakes, tyres, suspension, steering and lights but does not measure battery health or charging performance, which can affect an EV’s performance.

Separate research by UK broker Howden Group has also previously found that drivers of electric cars were being asked to pay twice as much for insurance as those who own petrol-fuelled models.

The difference was blamed on a higher cost of repairs for electric models when something did go wrong.

Mr Poston said the BVRLA’s study was “not an argument for buying a used EV without doing your homework”.

He added: “Buyers should look at its history, condition, tyres and battery health just as they would scrutinise the engine, gearbox and service history of a petrol or diesel car.”
