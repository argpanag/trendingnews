An education property tax break coming from the provincial government for Calgarians and most other Albertans.

I have to contact Jason Nixon, the budget boss in Premier Danielle Smith’s UCP government, to check out what I’m hearing.

Is it real?

Nixon confirms the good news.

What does it mean in the real world?

Will Calgarians pay a lower amount on their education property taxes? Less or more?

“Less,” says the straight-talking Nixon, without hesitation.

He doesn’t stop there.

“Everybody in the province will pay either exactly what they were already paying under the old system, that’s in very few cities because they were already paying significantly less in taxes.

“The vast majority of Albertans, particularly in the two largest cities, will pay less tax.

“Nobody is going to pay more education property tax.

“This is a tax break, for sure.”

Nixon is quick to point out it is not anywhere near as big as the Smith government’s tax cut which is worth up to $1,500 a year for families and $750 a year for individuals.

“But it all adds up,” he says.

Got all of that?

The Smith government will collect fewer education property tax dollars, just shy of $100 million.

How this bold move affects every homeowner will be figured out in the fullness of time because calculations of individual property taxes, including issues over assessed values of homes, requires a calculus course to navigate through the mathematical mumbo-jumbo.

Nixon says the way the property tax was operating was unfair, especially in places like Calgary.

Nixon says the system was “confusing as heck.”

Somebody with a home in Calgary was paying significantly more in education property taxes than a person in another city with a home of the same value.

What a difference from a year ago.

This year, on the province’s side of the Calgary property tax bill, the education property tax, the tax hike was 21 per cent.

On the city side of the property tax bill it was less than a 2 per cent increase.

The year before, the province hiked their education property tax by more than 15 per cent.

To put it mildly, Calgary Mayor Jeromy Farkas was outraged. He cried foul loud enough so folks in every quadrant of Calgary could hear.

It wasn’t only the whopping increase that got under his skin.

To add insult to injury for Calgary taxpayers, Farkas pointed out Calgarians were whacked harder by the province’s education property taxes than other cities in Alberta.

Now Farkas will stand with Smith in Calgary at a news conference to discuss this latest move and they will not be throwing political punches. Far from it.

At the gathering of newshounds, the Smith government is expected to stress delivering tax relief for Albertans, treating all Albertans fairly and putting more money in the pockets of Alberta families.

Nixon says Farkas was right to point out the unfairness of the system.

“I heard him on that and I’m making it fair,” says the province’s budget boss.

“The mayor raised it. He’s the one who brought it to our attention. That triggered us to go look and the mayor had a point and we said: Let’s make this fair.”

This tax break is not happening so city council can hike taxes on their side of the property tax bill even more than they originally planned.

Long ago, in the era of former mayor Naheed Nenshi, city council decided to scoop up a tax break from the Alberta government.

It became known as the $52 million tax break.

Nixon is on the side of wanting the tax break passed on to Calgarians and other Albertans, both residences and businesses paying the province’s education property tax.

“I’m not a ratepayer in Calgary but you guys will want to have a conversation specifically about that.”

This story should not come as a surprise to those who try to keep themselves informed.

As loyal readers know, I put a column out a few days back and it was all about Smith offering an olive branch to Calgary and Edmonton.

The Smith government is going to be paying a lot of attention to Calgary.

Guess we are seeing the first olive.
