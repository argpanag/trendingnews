MIB’s revised broadcast rules leave IPTV, cable outside 20% cross-ownership cap, opening room for deeper media integration

TheTools · 03.09.2026 05:17 · source · permalink

New Delhi: The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting’s revised broadcasting framework could have a significant bearing on the next phase of media consolidation, particularly as television distribution increasingly shifts towards cable-broadband and IP-based networks. While the September 2 draft of the Telecommunications (Television, Radio and Associated Services) Rules, 2026 retains the 20% cross-ownership restrictions applicable to broadcasters, DTH and HITS operators, it does not extend the same ceiling to broadcaster-MSO or broadcaster-IPTV relationships.

The distinction could give large media and telecom groups greater flexibility to pursue vertical integration through cable and IPTV than through satellite distribution platforms. Under the proposed framework, a television broadcaster can continue to have significant ownership alongside an MSO, while an eligible telecom or cable entity within the same group can potentially provide IPTV, subject to the applicable authorisation and operating requirements. A similar ownership structure involving a DTH or HITS platform, however, would face the specified 20% ceiling. The revised framework therefore does not eliminate vertical integration; instead, it continues to regulate different forms of integration differently.

The September draft, which runs to 76 pages compared with 43 pages in the June 12 consultation version, follows MIB’s examination of stakeholder submissions received during the first consultation. The ministry has opened a second consultation, with comments invited until October 2. The government has described the larger exercise as an attempt to simplify and harmonise the fragmented regulatory regime for television, radio and associated services under the Telecommunications Act, 2023.

The 20% cap remains focused on DTH and HITS

The ownership provisions are particularly relevant because the September draft formally expands the definition of a television channel distribution service to include DTH, HITS and IPTV. Yet the cross-ownership provisions that follow continue to specifically address DTH and HITS rather than applying the same ceiling across all three distribution technologies.

Under the proposed rules, a television channel authorised entity, either individually or together with an MSO, cannot hold more than 20% equity in a DTH authorised entity. Conversely, a DTH applicant cannot hold more than 20% equity in a television channel or an MSO. Similar restrictions apply to HITS, with direct and indirect shareholding taken into account for specified provisions.

There is no equivalent 20% restriction expressly covering a broadcaster’s holding in an MSO or an IPTV provider. This creates an important asymmetry at a time when the distinction between content companies and distribution businesses is becoming increasingly blurred.

The provisions are also not entirely new. They largely carry forward restrictions embedded in the legacy DTH and HITS regimes rather than representing a fresh ownership clampdown introduced by the September draft. The more consequential development is that the government has chosen to retain this differentiated approach even while attempting to bring multiple broadcasting and distribution frameworks into a single regulatory architecture.

The issue has a long regulatory history. TRAI, in its recommendations on a new DTH licensing regime in 2014, observed that significant variations existed in cross-holding rules across the broadcasting and distribution ecosystem, including the absence of comparable restrictions on cable operators and stricter restrictions applicable to HITS. The regulator had argued for greater uniformity in cross-holding and control rules between broadcasters and distribution platform operators.

More than a decade later, that asymmetry remains relevant.

IPTV changes the commercial significance of the distinction

The treatment of IPTV makes the ownership question more consequential.

The June draft already recognised IPTV as a regulated service and prescribed operational conditions for IPTV providers. However, the definition of television channel distribution services in that version covered DTH and HITS, with IPTV treated separately.

The September version brings IPTV explicitly within the definition of television channel distribution services. At the same time, the ownership restrictions remain framed around DTH and HITS. This effectively creates a regulatory distinction between the classification of a distribution service and the ownership restrictions attached to it.

The revised rules also make IPTV subject to a more detailed compliance regime. Providers would have to retransmit only authorised television channels and maintain digital rights management and conditional-access functionality aimed at preventing television-channel piracy. The framework also provides for mandatory retransmission of specified public-sector channels and brings IPTV closer to conventional distribution platforms in terms of regulatory obligations.

An IPTV service can be operated through an entity holding the requisite internet service authorisation under the telecom framework or through an MSO registration under the Cable Television Networks regime. This gives integrated groups a route to combine connectivity, cable distribution, IPTV and content interests without encountering the specific 20% ownership restriction that applies when DTH or HITS is involved.

For the industry, this could influence not only ownership structures but also where future investment in distribution is directed.

Reliance illustrates why the provision matters

The implications can already be viewed through the structure of large media and telecom groups, with Reliance providing one of the clearest examples.

Reliance Industries’ FY2025-26 annual report records a 63.16% holding in JioStar India Private Limited, while DEN Networks and Hathway Cable and Datacom are also part of the group’s subsidiary structure.

The proposed ownership provisions do not, on their face, require separation between a broadcaster and an MSO simply because they are under common ownership. Nor do they impose the same 20% ceiling on an IPTV operation conducted through an eligible telecom or cable entity.

That does not mean the group structure is exempt from regulation. MSOs continue to operate under the Cable Television Networks framework, IPTV providers must satisfy the applicable authorisation requirements, and the proposed broadcasting rules contain separate provisions governing ownership changes, security clearances, transfers and operational compliance.

The significance is therefore less about any one company and more about the regulatory model it illustrates: the proposed rules place tighter ownership boundaries around certain satellite distribution platforms while leaving greater structural flexibility around cable and IP-based distribution.

Could this influence future consolidation?

Potentially. As media companies reassess the economics of traditional linear television distribution, the ownership framework could become one factor in determining how they structure acquisitions, joint ventures and new distribution businesses.

A media group seeking deeper vertical integration could have greater equity flexibility in building or acquiring an MSO or developing an IPTV operation than it would in acquiring or controlling a DTH or HITS platform.

That could become increasingly relevant as broadband networks, connected television and managed IP distribution gain importance alongside traditional DTH and cable.

The distinction also matters because the revised rules prohibit television distribution operators from entering into agreements for the exclusive distribution of authorised television channels. The provision is intended to prevent distributors from using their position to restrict access to channels, particularly where distribution and content interests overlap.

In effect, the proposed regime places limits on both ownership concentration and certain forms of distribution control, but those limits do not operate identically across every distribution technology.

Consolidation remains possible, but under continuing regulatory oversight

The ownership provisions form only one part of a much broader shift in the September draft.

The revised framework moves broadcasters and distribution operators into a continuing authorisation regime under the Telecommunications Act rather than relying on a collection of legacy permissions and guidelines. The framework covers satellite television, DTH, HITS, private FM radio, community radio, television news agencies, teleports and IPTV.

For television broadcasters, the proposed framework retains minimum net-worth requirements of ₹5 crore for the first non-news channel and ₹20 crore for the first news channel, with additional requirements for subsequent channels. DTH and HITS operators would each require a minimum net worth of ₹10 crore. Authorisation periods are proposed at 10 years for television channels, teleports and community radio, 10 years for television news agencies, 20 years for television distribution services and 15 years for private radio.

At the same time, ownership changes become more closely regulated. Shareholding, partnership and FDI changes would have to be disclosed within prescribed timelines, while changes resulting in a change of control or complete change in management would require prior written government permission.

This makes the new framework relevant to future M&A activity well beyond the 20% cross-holding question.

Compliance becomes a bigger part of the business equation

The other major change in the September draft is the introduction of a formal hierarchy for civil penalties.

A new Schedule 4 categorises breaches as non-severe, minor, moderate or major, taking into account whether violations occur for the first, second or subsequent time. The list includes failures such as delayed disclosure of shareholding or FDI changes, unauthorised appointments, failure to remove personnel denied security clearance, unauthorised channel branding, prolonged shutdowns, unregistered live broadcasts, misuse of DSNG equipment, unauthorised uplinking and carrying unauthorised channels.

The significance for broadcasters and distribution businesses is that compliance failures that might previously have been dealt with as generic breaches now sit within a more clearly structured enforcement mechanism.

The revised framework also strengthens scrutiny of personnel. Foreign personnel deployed for installation, maintenance or operation of broadcasting networks would require security clearance, while entities would have to disclose details of foreigners and non-resident Indians engaged as consultants or under other designations for more than 60 days in a year, or as regular employees.

Renewal gets a measure of procedural protection

The September draft also modifies one of the provisions that had attracted attention in the June version.

The earlier draft linked renewal to an entity not having committed more than five breaches of its authorisation conditions, including violations of programme and advertising codes. The revised version specifies that the breaches must have been adjudicated.

That distinction is significant. A complaint or regulatory allegation by itself would not be worded as sufficient to activate the five-breach threshold; the violation would first have to pass through adjudication.

The authorisation periods remain long, but renewal is not automatic. This means the proposed framework combines longer operating horizons with continuing regulatory accountability.

Radio provisions have also been recalibrated

Private radio sees a notable change from the June proposal.

Rather than requiring operators to devote one hour every day to programming on themes of national importance and social relevance, the revised draft requires up to one hour of government-mandated public-interest announcements to be carried during the day.

The change narrows the earlier formulation, which could have been interpreted as a broader government influence over programming. The revised approach is more specifically framed around public-interest announcements. The 20% local-content requirement and existing ownership concentration restrictions remain.

The revised draft also expressly brings compliance with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 within the general obligations of authorised entities and expands satellite-related provisions by specifically recognising IN-SPACe alongside the Department of Space or another designated government agency.

An old regulatory asymmetry survives a new consolidation exercise

The larger question raised by the September draft is therefore whether the government’s attempt to harmonise broadcasting regulation has also produced a genuinely technology-neutral ownership regime.

On one level, the answer is no. The rules consolidate DTH, HITS and IPTV under the broader category of television channel distribution services, but the 20% cross-ownership restrictions remain concentrated on DTH and HITS.

That means the regulatory treatment of content-distribution integration still depends, in part, on the technology and regulatory route through which distribution is provided.

TRAI had flagged this issue more than a decade ago. Its 2014 DTH recommendations noted the wide variations in cross-holding rules and argued that uniformity was needed across broadcasters and distribution platform operators.

The September draft does not fully resolve that historical asymmetry. Instead, it carries it into a new authorisation framework at a time when the commercial boundaries between broadband, cable, IPTV and television broadcasting are becoming less distinct.

For media companies, therefore, the significance of the revised rules may extend beyond compliance. The regulatory treatment of different distribution technologies could become a strategic consideration in deciding where future capital, acquisitions and vertical integration are directed.

The draft remains open to stakeholder comments until October 2 and could undergo further changes before being notified. The rules will take effect only from a date subsequently specified by the Central Government.

In short, the September revision does not dismantle India’s restrictions on media-distribution consolidation. It redraws where those restrictions apply—and, in doing so, could make cable and IPTV comparatively more attractive routes for groups seeking greater control across the television value chain.
