Local Mississippi election official says tomorrow will be pivotal for most populous county

A local election official in Mississippi’s Hinds County said tomorrow will be a “very pivotal point” for the state’s most populous county as it continues processing ballots.

Voters still have until 5 p.m. CT on Tuesday to have their absentee ballots returned as long as they are postmarked by November 3, according to Robert Graham, the president of the Hinds County board of supervisors. The county has received 64 absentee ballots since Election Day that have yet to be counted.

Some 8,000 absentee ballots have already been reported.

Hinds County, which is home to the state capital Jackson, is a key source of Democratic electoral strength. The Mississippi Senate race between incumbent GOP Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith and Democrat Scott Colom remains too close to call.

The commissioners will meet to declare their accepted and rejected provisional ballots on Wednesday morning at 9:30 a.m.

Starting at 10 a.m., the officials will begin processing the accepted affidavit ballots through the voting machines. The county doesn’t know the total number of affidavit ballots. Campaigns can make objections during this point but it won’t stop the commissioners from their counting, Graham told CNN.

“Tomorrow will be a very pivotal point,” Graham said.

Who is Jim Jordan, the combative judiciary chairman running for minority leader?

In running to be the next minority leader, Jim Jordan has pitched himself as “battle-tested on the oversight front” and vowed to aggressively counter a new Democratic majority. That sell could resonate with his GOP colleagues, who will decide Tuesday whether to elect him to the role.

Jordan has served as chair of the powerful House Judiciary committee since 2023. A fierce and longtime ally of President Donald Trump, Jordan has used his gavel and his seat on the oversight panel to probe what he has argued is the weaponization of the government against the president. He led GOP investigations into two attorneys who have prosecuted the president: former special counsel Jack Smith and Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis.

The Ohio Republican, who was first elected to Congress in 2007, has also long been an influential voice in the GOP conference. He helped found the House Freedom Caucus in 2015, leading the right-wing bloc that rankled former Speaker John Boehner before he resigned from Congress.

While the group’s members have maintained a reputation for frustrating House GOP leaders and thwarting legislation, Jordan has more recently taken a more deferential approach and aligned himself closely with Republican Speakers Kevin McCarthy and Mike Johnson.

Many of Jordan’s colleagues have long believed the former state legislator and college wrestling coach was hungry for a greater leadership role. Some saw his prolific campaigning for House Republican candidates this year as an effort to lay the groundwork for a potential minority leader run.

Jordan made a bid for the speaker’s gavel after McCarthy’s ouster in 2023. But he faced opposition from some moderates and Steve Scalise allies and failed to get the votes on the floor.

A closer look at Steve Scalise, the No. 2 House Republican running for minority leader

If House Republicans elect Steve Scalise as their leader, they’ll elevate an experienced Louisiana lawmaker who has spent over a decade in the party’s second- and third-ranking roles.

Scalise has served as majority leader since January 2023, managing the chamber’s floor calendar and acting as the second-in-command to Republican Speakers Kevin McCarthy and Mike Johnson. Before that, Scalise served as Republicans’ whip, both in the minority, from 2019 through 2022, and the majority, from 2014 through 2018.

Scalise’s run for minority leader came as little surprise to colleagues, especially after Johnson, a fellow Louisianan, told aides he would not seek the role.

Scalise launched a bid to lead the party after McCarthy was ousted as speaker in October 2023 — but he dropped out of the race after facing resistance from some of the party’s hardliners.

Scalise has overcome challenges as he has climbed the leadership ranks. He was shot and critically wounded in 2017, when a gunman opened fire at a congressional Republican baseball game practice. He underwent multiple emergency surgeries as part of a long recovery.

In 2023, Scalise announced he was diagnosed with a form of blood cancer and was undergoing treatment. His office said in 2024 he was in complete remission.

Trump and Miller have encouraged Hegseth to fight impeachment efforts for now, sources say

President Donald Trump and his new White House chief of staff Stephen Miller have privately told Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and other senior aides that their current plan is to fight against efforts to impeach the Pentagon chief and to defend him staunchly, people familiar with the matter told CNN.

Miller is personally very close to Hegseth, the sources noted, and has — like Trump — reassured him that they have his back.

The president has fielded several calls in recent days from allies on and off Capitol Hill regarding the impeachment vote being brought forth by GOP Rep. Thomas Massie. As CNN previously reported, Trump has often asked those around him for their opinions on how to handle the growing opposition, from both sides of the aisle, to his defense secretary.

However, the sources described those calls as typical Trump conversations rather than a direct lobbying effort to convince Republicans to stick with Hegseth.

While Trump and Miller have continued to support Hegseth, there are many senior advisers throughout the administration who have grown skeptical of whether the defense secretary will be able to hang onto his position for much longer, arguing his goodwill with the president will last until it becomes a serious liability.

“The president will back him to a point,” one Trump ally, a former adviser to the president, told CNN. “But he may be forced to remove him once it’s no longer tenable. There are varying views in the West Wing on how long that will be.”

A White House official, meanwhile, said in a statement that Miller and Hegseth have “built an incredibly successful partnership working together on some of the administration’s most important public safety and national security priorities.”

Questions emerge about Schumer's future as some Democrats say they want to keep options open

Several Senate Democrats say they are not committed to voting for Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer next month in leadership elections despite massive wins across the country in the midterm elections.

While many Democrats said they would consider voting for Schumer, several say they want to hear more from him on how he will lead the caucus and what his vision is for the Democratic agenda if he were to once again lead in the majority.

“We haven’t had a chance to talk as a caucus yet,” Sen. Andy Kim, a Democrat from New Jersey, told CNN. “Now we have a chance to actually lead. What do we want our committees to focus on? That’s the conversation I want to have then we can walk through how we set the leadership table.”

Sen. Michael Bennet, a Democrat from Colorado, also wouldn’t say if he’d ultimately back Schumer.

“We are going to have some time to discuss this, to debate this as a caucus, to think about how we put our best foot forward, and I think that’s what we should do,” he said.

On the Republican side, there has been clamoring for new leadership in both the House and Senate. But that came after Republicans took a drubbing in the midterms, losing Senate seats in traditionally Republican states like South Carolina, Texas and Kansas and as the House overwhelmingly flipped Democratic. Several Schumer allies pointed out that Schumer was pivotal in the Senate’s historic successes and that he’s proved he can guide Democrats to wins, a critical attribute as the party seeks to set the table ahead of the presidential race in 2028.

Several Senate Democrats who won in those seats have either not committed to Schumer or said on the trail they wouldn’t vote for him. Annie Andrews, the Democrat elected from South Carolina, said on Fox News earlier this week she wants to see “generational change” in leadership, and Adam Hamilton, the Democrat elected in Kansas, told voters throughout his campaign that he did not plan to back Schumer for leader. He told CNN’s Dana Bash Monday that he wants to see “fresh leadership” and that Schumer is seen as a more “divisive” leader whether that is the reality or not. He said he’s looking for someone that can bring Republicans and Democrats together.

Asked about the questions over Schumer’s future on Monday night, Sen. Brian Schatz, a Democrat who is running to be the next whip, told reporters that he is “for Chuck” but that he also was not Schumer’s spokesperson.

The race for leadership is a secret ballot so no one will know who voted or did not vote for Schumer next month. It only takes a majority of the caucus to win majority leader.

“I haven’t actually heard of anybody running so I think it’s actually kind of a moot point,” Arizona Democrat Sen. Ruben Gallego told CNN as he pointed out Schumer and his staff had been critical in helping Democrats secure victories around the country.

Right now there is no official challenger, but Maryland Democrat Chris Van Hollen has said he is not closing the door on a run.

Van Hollen told CNN Monday night that Democrats “shouldn’t just anoint somebody.”

Poll shows 71% of Americans — and 51% of Republicans — want Congress to end Iran war

A new poll released as Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth faces possible impeachment shows 7 in 10 Americans — and even about half of Republicans — want Congress to vote to end the Iran war.

The Reuters/Ipsos poll is the first major post-election poll to ask about the next Congress’ priorities.

The survey didn’t ask about specific ways Congress could end the war, such as cutting funding or trying to force President Donald Trump’s hand by impeaching Hegseth, nor did it delve into the circumstances of an acceptable withdrawal.

But it shows that Americans want the war brought to an end somehow. And the fact that 51% of Republicans are in that camp suggests Trump isn’t playing a great political hand right now.

The survey also showed that 62% of Americans — including 32% of Republicans — want Congress to pass legislation ending Trump’s tariffs.

But for now, the public appears skeptical of trying to impeach Trump. While 33% said it would be good for the country, 42% said it would be bad for the country.

Hegseth makes personal appeal to Trump in defiant press conference

Pete Hegseth’s first Pentagon press briefing in six months was a personal appeal to President Donald Trump and was scheduled during one of Trump’s favorite TV programs: Fox News’ talk show “Fox & Friends.”

Hegseth, who used to host the weekend edition of “Fox & Friends,” called on only representatives from right-wing media outlets, perhaps seeking relatively easy questions about the war in Iran and other US military actions.

The defense secretary also ignored shouted questions from NBC and other journalists from established news outlets. Instead, he took questions from small Trump-aligned brands such as One America News, Real America’s Voice and The Daily Wire.

Hegseth appeared to glance at a sheet of paper while picking his questioners. He depicted impeachment as a fringe effort, calling it a “circus that one congressman has tried to unleash” and just “one small thing over here that we deal with.”

“It’s nothing compared to what we’re actually engaged and dealing with, which is ensuring operationally we’re prepared all around the globe,” he added.

But his drought of press availabilities during the war — and then his defensive appearance on Tuesday — belied his depiction of impeachment as a “small thing.”

The grab bag of justifications for Hegseth’s impeachment

One of the big challenges for Democrats and GOP Rep. Thomas Massie in impeaching and trying Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is apparently going to be deciding what Hegseth’s main offenses are.

Massie’s impeachment resolution contains eight articles. They include violating the War Powers Resolution by illegally waging war in Iran, the strike on an Iranian elementary school early in the war, the administration’s extrajudicial boat strikes on supposed drug traffickers, targeting Democratic Sen. Mark Kelly of Arizona for telling officials not to follow illegal orders, “kidnapping” Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and allegedly illegal strikes in Yemen.

But Democrats who plan to support the articles haven’t always been on the same page.

Democratic Rep. James Clyburn of South Carolina has focused on Hegseth’s treatment of women and Black military personnel. Democratic Sen. Chris Coons of Delaware told CNN that impeachment is about “inadequate consultation” with Congress, the American people and US allies. And likely incoming House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries told ABC’s “This Week” on Sunday that it’s about how Hegseth “engineered this reckless and costly war of choice in Iran.”

The easy call would seem to be focusing on the highly unpopular Iran war. But it’s perhaps more difficult to argue that it was illegal — compared with any number of other military strikes that have been launched without congressional authorization in recent decades. Waging a “reckless and costly war” isn’t inherently impeachable. And it’s the president who launched the war.

Hegseth argued Tuesday morning that he was being impeached for carrying out lawful orders.

Some of Massie’s other articles might make more sense from a legal standpoint — there has been plenty of analysis on whether the boat strikes and elementary school attack were war crimes, for instance — but they are narrower and less likely to capture the public’s attention.

And almost nobody besides Massie is talking about the other articles, like Maduro’s ouster and Yemen.

Hegseth "stands behind every one" of the military operations ordered by Trump

Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth said Tuesday he stands “behind every one” of the military operations he has executed at the order of President Donald Trump and “would do it again tomorrow,” despite bipartisan criticism over his role in the deeply unpopular Iran war.

Hegseth defiantly pushed back on allegations that he violated the US Constitution by carrying out Trump’s orders to prosecute the war against Iran, arguing those military operations were legal and effective, while dismissing impeachment efforts as a “farce.”

Hegseth also attempted to deflect criticism of himself as criticism of US troops — misrepresenting the bipartisan support for impeachment.

Rather than impeachment, Hegseth argued that “the proper remedy” for what he characterized as a policy disagreement is legislation and debate. But Hegseth has demonstrated little interest in communicating with Congress to this point in his tenure — one of the chief criticisms of how he has performed in the role to date.

Hegseth says he won't engage in "finger-pointing" over GOP midterm losses

Asked whether he agrees with Vice President JD Vance’s statement that a major reason why Republicans lost in the midterms was because of the Iran war, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said he respects Vance “greatly, and I’m certainly not going to engage in any finger-pointing.”

“Thankfully, politics is not really my thing. I do the Department of War and making sure we’re prepared and ready. So a lot of people are going to look at the conflict and assess it,” Hegseth said. But he said he agreed with Trump when he said he would do it all over again, even if it was not popular with the public.

The Pentagon, State Department and White House have repeatedly offered varying explanations for why it was necessary to attack Iran, including that Tehran was rapidly expanding its ballistic missile production and preparing to attack US bases and allies in the region. The White House said in June 2025 that Iran’s nuclear program had been “obliterated” with US strikes at the time.

Asked whether he had spoken to new White House chief of Staff Stephen Miller about reports that Miller believes the war should end, Hegseth demurred, saying, “He’s busy in his new role right now, and I look forward to meeting with him soon, in particular about the ways that we’ll work together.”

Hegseth said the Pentagon’s legislative affairs team “has been very active” with regard to staving off the impeachment effort, “obviously working with Speaker (Mike) Johnson, who’s done a fantastic job, and the whip, Tom Emmer. We’ve been in touch with a lot of members.”

“Whether they love everything that’s been done, they don’t want to vote for something that isn’t real. So I’m grateful that Republicans have been taking a real look at this, and the speaker has led on it, and I appreciate that.”

Key procedural vote on Hegseth impeachment set for today

The House of Representatives is scheduled to move on a key procedural step in the effort to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth later today that will give an early indication of how many Republicans may be willing to rebuke the defense secretary.

The vote is just a first step and will determine whether the full House will then move ahead on the actual impeachment articles — or whether GOP leaders can squash the effort before members are confronted with the question.

The effort to impeach Hegseth is being led by outgoing Rep. Thomas Massie, who lost his primary earlier this year. This morning, Hegseth took to the podium at the Pentagon to defend himself and called Massie a defeated and disgruntled congressman who wasn’t conducting real oversight.

Hegseth has said repeatedly that policy disagreements over the Iran war and other actions are not the kinds of high crimes and misdemeanors that form the basis for impeachment.

A key question remains how many Republicans may back the effort and vote with Democrats. A number of Republican lawmakers have expressed frustration with Hegseth’s job performance, including James Comer, the top Republican on the House Oversight Committee, and Rep. Don Bacon, a Republican from Nebraska who is retiring at the end of the year. Retiring Sen. Thom Tillis, a Republican from North Carolina, has also said he is open to voting to remove Hegseth from office if the articles make it to the Senate for a trial.

GOP leaders must ensure that they have strong attendance today. With a narrow majority, the impact of missing members could be make or break in the votes over impeachment.

Even if Hegseth is eventually impeached in the House, GOP leadership still have a few tools at their disposal to slow down or halt a trial. GOP Speaker Mike Johnson could hold onto the articles of impeachment until the end of the year, rather than expeditiously transmitting them to the Senate. And GOP senators could also move to quickly dispose of the articles or refer them to committee, essentially slow-walking the process.

Hegseth: Impeachment effort is a "political spectacle" driven by “policy disagreements"

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth began his press conference on Tuesday morning calling GOP Rep. Thomas Massie’s impeachment push against him “a political spectacle, a personal manifesto driven by ego and resentment.”

Hegseth insisted that he had been carrying out “lawful military operations” that were “successful,” and that no previous defense secretaries had ever been impeached for doing so.

“Not once, war powers resolution or not, not under Reagan in Lebanon, not under Clinton in Kosovo, not under Obama in Iraq and Syria, not under Biden in Iraq and Syria, that’s the standard. The history is clear and it’s very straightforward,” Hegseth said.

He characterized the impeachment process against him as driven by “policy disagreements, not misdemeanors, not treason, not bribery, not the kinds of offenses the founders intended when they wrote the impeachment clause.”

Massie introduced eight articles of impeachment against Hegseth on Monday, accusing the defense secretary of waging war in Iran without congressional approval, ignoring laws about minimizing civilian casualties and directing the Defense Department to carry out extrajudicial killings.

How does impeachment work for Cabinet officials?

Proceedings can begin with an investigation by a House committee, the results of which would then be presented to the House to pass articles of impeachment. A simple majority is required for an impeachment vote on the House floor.

A trial could then be held in the Senate, where a supermajority, two-thirds of the body, would need to be reached to convict.

But the process doesn’t always have to unfold that way.

GOP Rep. Thomas Massie introduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth this week in a move that could make him the third Cabinet official in US history to be impeached.

GOP leaders will have two legislative days to schedule a vote on the measure once Massie brings up the articles.

If the impeachment vote in the House is successful, it is expected that the GOP-controlled Senate would likely attempt to move to dismiss it or refer it to committee for further consideration. Both would need majority votes.
