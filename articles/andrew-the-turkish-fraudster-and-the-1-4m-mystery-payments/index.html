Andrew, the Turkish ‘fraudster’ and the £1.4m mystery payments

TheTools · 23.11.2026 21:04 · source · permalink

As the former prince is summoned to give evidence in a fraud trial, his links to banker Selman Turk are being scrutinised once more

It has never quite been explained why Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor accepted more than £1m from a Turkish heiress. Nor why his former wife, Sarah Ferguson, received a further £225,000 and his younger daughter, Princess Eugenie, £25,000.

The mystery is hardly a sole blot on an otherwise pristine copybook. The payments, when they first emerged some four years ago, were simply added to the roster of dubious financial dealings, poor judgment calls and unwise friendships that had long cast a shadow over the former Duke of York’s reputation – and which, in the last 12 months, have seen him stripped of his royal titles, kicked out of his 30-room mansion in Windsor Great Park and cast adrift by his family.

Now, however, that mystery is set to be scrutinised afresh. A fiendishly complicated civil fraud case that has been winding its way through the High Court for five years will finally come to trial this week. Turkish banker Selman Turk is accused of embezzling some £40m from Nebahat Evyap İşbilen, the aforementioned Turkish heiress, who had hired him to help transfer her assets out of Turkey, where her husband Ilhan, a wealthy MP, is a political prisoner. More than £17m of that £40m remains unaccounted for.

Among the witnesses summoned to give evidence is none other than the King’s younger brother – the first member of the Royal family since 1870 to be sent such a summons. Andrew is not accused of wrongdoing, and has since repaid the bulk of the money. But lawyers acting on behalf of Turk believe Andrew can bolster their case by proving their version of events concerning certain payments.

“[It is] important that the court has as full an evidential picture as possible,” they said. Andrew’s testimony will be relevant to allegations of dishonesty advanced against their client, they added. Mr Justice Adam Johnson consented to the request, saying that “fairness” required that the defendant put forward his case “using the evidence he considers relevant”. He noted, rather shrewdly, that it was “highly unlikely” Andrew would wish to provide a witness statement and give evidence at trial, meaning that a summons would probably be required.

The disgraced royal may find it difficult to wriggle out of such a request, although an appearance via videolink might at least avoid the ignominy of a press scrum outside the court – a scene a man already under police investigation for other matters might be keen to avoid. But Andrew is likely to face some difficult questions.

The first payment he will undoubtedly be asked about is a sum of £750,000, transferred to the former duke in 2019. According to High Court documents, İşbilen, a member of the prominent Turkish family that owns a group of companies called the Evyap Group, claims she authorised the transfer because Turk misled her into believing the money was needed as a “gift” to thank Andrew for helping her secure a new Turkish passport after fleeing Turkey.

Her legal team maintains that the passport explanation was a dishonest ruse used by Turk to misappropriate her funds. Turk strongly denies any dishonesty, claiming İşbilen made the payment voluntarily “to create goodwill”, having been led to believe the royal could use “his connections in Turkey” to help her incarcerated husband. Had Andrew indicated that he could provide such support? If not, what was the money for and why did he accept it?

The answers lie with two men: Andrew himself and alleged fraudster Turk.

The origins of İşbilen’s relationship with Turk, a former Goldman Sachs banker, are not in dispute. The millionairess was introduced to Turk by a friend of her husband in late August 2016 as she sought help in moving more than $87m out of her homeland. She was desperate – fleeing persecution following a crackdown on the opponents of Tayyip Erdoğan, the Turkish president. Turk was billed as an expert in finance who would be able to help relocate and protect her assets. She trusted him implicitly and, in March 2017, agreed to his request for a $1m payment for his services, according to court documents.

Several months later, he helped her travel to England, via Samos and Athens. In Samos, unbeknown to her, Turk is alleged to have procured for her an “investor visa”, allowing her to make investments in the UK, by using £2m of her assets. Turk also received a $5m “loan”, which İşbilen claims to know nothing about.

Then, just nine days before İşbilen, who speaks little English, transferred £750,000 to Andrew, both she and Turk attended an awards ceremony for Pitch@Palace, a Dragons’ Den-style start-up competition and networking initiative founded by Andrew. At the event at St James’s Palace, İşbilen watched on proudly as her new friend and mentor was awarded the People’s Choice Award on behalf of his “digital bank”, Heyman AI.

Her money was soon winging its way to Andrew’s personal bank account at Coutts, allegedly at Turk’s direction. A former business associate of the banker later alleged that he had “intimated” in various conversations that the money was “all to do with ensuring Heyman AI did well at Pitch@Palace”.

Similarly, a director of one of Turk’s competitors said there was “surprise” that a banking app won the People’s Choice Award. A senior Pitch@Palace source told biographer Andrew Lownie that Turk “did an appalling pitch and I just remember thinking, ‘How did he get through?’ It was just nuts.”

Such was the scale of the win – Heyman AI was said to have netted some 10,000 votes compared to others in their hundreds – that there were fears bots had been used. Meanwhile, staff at İşbilen’s private bank Hampden & Co were later told the cash transfer was a wedding gift for Princess Beatrice, whose engagement had been announced the previous month.

A transcript of phone calls obtained by The Telegraph showed Turk telling an official from Hampden & Co on November 14, 2019, that it was “for the cost of the wedding”, adding later: “Wedding gift, so basically it’s a gift to the bride.”

In a separate call later that day, the same banker asked Andrew’s senior aide, Amanda Thirsk, to confirm whether the “unusual” payment was a gift for the cost of the wedding or a gift to Princess Beatrice. Thirsk replied: “I mean, I’m not sure it makes much difference, does it? I think it’s a gift for the wedding. What she and her family decide to do with it is really to do with them, isn’t it?”

Passport? Turkish contacts? Bung for an award? Wedding gift? Take your pick. Mr Justice Johnson will surely endeavour to find out. But how did Andrew come into the sphere of this man in the first place? And why was Turk invited into the heart of the British establishment?


The two men were introduced in May or June 2019, by Tarek Kaituni, a Libyan gun smuggler who had links to Colonel Gaddafi. The then Duke and Mr Kaituni had been friends for years. In November 2008, while he was still UK trade envoy, Andrew enjoyed a four-day holiday in Tunisia paid for by Kaituni, after which Andrew visited Col Gaddafi in Libya.

More than a decade later, just days before the Pitch@Palace event, Kaituni attended Sarah Ferguson’s 60th birthday party at Royal Lodge, the home she shared with Andrew, partially funded by none other than Selman Turk.

Turk and Kaituni met Andrew again at a private dinner at Turk’s South Kensington home in December 2019, a dinner that was also attended by İşbilen and Sarah Ferguson.

There was, according to Turk, some discussion about whether Andrew might be able to help İşbilen’s imprisoned husband. The royal is said to have offered certain information he had obtained regarding the allegations against the Turkish politician. Turk said he took İşbilen home in a taxi later that evening and she emphasised her desire to “gift something” to motivate Andrew to help her husband, despite his insistence that payment was unnecessary. She had hoped, it is alleged, that his royal connections might eventually lead to political pressure being applied on Turkey to help her husband.

The cash injection that followed the Pitch@Palace win was followed by a series of payments to the York family made through a company called Alphabet Capital. İşbilen’s lawyer, Jonathan Tickner, said there was “strong evidence” the firm was used as a “front” by Turk to make payments to those associated with Turk. One payment of £768,743, made to Alphabet in January 2020, is said to have been a loan to help Andrew buy a Swiss ski chalet.

A further £350,000 was channelled to Andrew under the reference “TK Wedding” (proof, barristers have alleged, of Kaituni’s involvement), while Sarah Ferguson received at least £225,000 from the Alphabet account.

Most of the payments sent to Ferguson in £25,000 chunks were labelled “PEG001” – a reference to her role as a brand ambassador for Pegasus Group Holdings, a Las Vegas-based renewable-energy company. Turk has claimed he reached a deal to pay Ferguson the money upfront for the work she would be doing on behalf of the American company. But records show Pegasus only ever repaid him £20,000.

The affair has been humiliating for the couple’s daughters. Princess Eugenie has said she did not know Turk or İşbilen and that the £25,000 cash transferred to her was a gift from “a longstanding family friend to assist with the cost of a surprise party for my mother”.

The friend is thought to be Kaituni, who had attended her October 2018 wedding. The gun smuggler is also said to have given Princess Beatrice an £18,000 gold and diamond necklace after he was invited by her father to her 21st birthday party at a private villa in Marbella, Spain, in August 2009. Months later, the then Duke allegedly lobbied a British company on his behalf.

İşbilen launched legal action against Turk and various offshore companies in 2021, in an attempt to recover her missing millions. Her case largely rests on the allegation that he dishonestly and systematically abused the trust that she placed in him.

She later issued contempt proceedings for an alleged breach of a court order that saw Turk sentenced to 12 months in prison in 2024 for concealing payments, including a payment of £212,676 to Ferguson.

At a previous hearing for İşbilen, Dan McCourt Fritz KC said he was baffled why Turk had sent the Yorks so much money and suggested he was simply trying to “curry favour” with the couple. Turk has denied wrongdoing, insisting that all of his transfers were authorised and for the sole purpose of protecting İşbilen’s wealth.

When he was jailed for contempt, Judge Sir Anthony Mann told the High Court there was “no doubt that there was some sort of relationship” between Turk and the then Yorks. We will discover next month, when Andrew appears in court, whether the disgraced royal can provide further clarity.
