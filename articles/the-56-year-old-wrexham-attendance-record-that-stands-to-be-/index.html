The 56-year-old Wrexham attendance record that stands to be broken this week

TheTools · 10.09.2026 17:41 · source · permalink

A crowd of more than 50,000 is expected for Wrexham's trip to West Ham United Richard Pelham/Getty Images

History beckons once again this week for Wrexham.

Friday’s trip to West Ham United, the first league meeting between the two clubs in a little over 45 years, seems certain to set a new record attendance for a match involving the Welsh side.

Providing the previous best — the 54,096 crowd that packed into Liverpool’s Anfield for an FA Cup fourth-round tie in 1970 — is eclipsed at the London Stadium, it will be yet another indication of just how far Wrexham have come since the February 2021 takeover by Rob Mac and Ryan Reynolds.

Not even the club’s eight seasons competing in Europe between 1972 and 1995, when the opposition included famous old names such as Roma, Porto and Anderlecht, can compare to the ride supporters have been on these past few years.

The players, too, have benefited, with the trip to West Ham and the largest stadium the second tier of English football has seen since the mid to late 1970s something to savour.

“You want to play in big games,” says Matty James, Wrexham’s experienced midfielder who has previously played for Bristol City at the 62,500-capacity stadium built for the 2012 Olympics.

“You want to play in the big stadiums and you want to play against the best players in the league. It is a great opportunity for us.”

The ‘sold out’ signs have become a familiar sight for Wrexham fans in the Hollywood era.

Not just at The Racecourse Ground, either, with the draw of a club given a global profile via Welcome to Wrexham often ensuring the opposition post either their biggest crowd of the season or one of the highest.

Charlton Athletic are just one of many to have tapped into the interest generated by a documentary that was renewed for another three seasons earlier this year, the Londoners even selling half-and-half scarves in their official club shop when Phil Parkinson’s side visited The Valley in October 2024.

The move upset Charlton fans, but the club’s then chief executive, Charlie Methven, was able to point subsequently to the 24,692 crowd — up a little under 10,000 on the season’s average at The Valley — including 1,650 tickets sold to international Wrexham fans (on top of the 2,647 supporters in the designated away seats) as justification.

Methven also explained to the Where’s The Money Gone? podcast that a further 3,000 to 4,000 fans had attended from the Greater London area whose interest had been piqued by the documentary, while only 11 of the 300 half-and-half scarves in the shop remained unsold.

West Ham were never going to be selling merchandise with the visitors’ name on. Even allowing for the move to the London Stadium that saw the much more earthy, if wonderfully atmospheric, Upton Park left behind, they just aren’t that sort of club.

Nor were the Hammers — with 50,000 season ticket holders signed up despite dropping out of the Premier League in May — going to receive the kind of attendance bump that meant Wrexham’s visit to Cardiff City on the opening weekend was watched by 10,000 more fans than the next weekend home game in the Welsh capital, against Sheffield United.

Already this season, the three home games played by Nuno Espirito Santo’s side in the Championship have attracted crowds of 62,264 (Charlton Athletic), 59,113 (Wolverhampton Wanderers) and 61,180 (Derby County).

Nevertheless, the prospect of a club owned by two actors from the United States and Canada taking on West Ham has proved sufficiently enticing to be broadcast live on both sides of the Atlantic.

So, what can Wrexham expect? On the pitch, a very tough test, with West Ham having gone top of the fledgling Championship table on Tuesday after a 3-2 victory at Bolton Wanderers.

Off it, this will be a very different experience from the one Wrexham supporters enjoyed when they travelled to Upton Park for the last meeting between these two clubs, a famous 1-0 FA Cup third-round replay victory for the then third-tier visitors in 1997.

Where West Ham’s old home had the stands so close to the pitch that the players couldn’t help but hear the locals’ often blunt verdicts, the London Stadium is so vast that those sitting in the upper tier behind the goal almost feel like they’re in a different postcode.

The running track means it is not much better when watching from the sides, hence why some locals have been known to applaud the frequently heard chant from the away seats, “You’ve sold your soul for this s***hole.”

Players, too, have had to get used to these unusually long distances between stand and pitch, though in Wrexham’s case this might not be too much of a problem after taking on Liverpool in Yankee Stadium, New York, as recently as late July.

Squeezing a football pitch into a space designed for baseball made for an awkward and unusual look, particularly in terms of how some seats almost hugged the touchline as others sat 60 or so yards back.

Parkinson’s side adapted well in the Bronx and were unfortunate to lose 1-0 on a night that may well help come Friday.

“There’s a little bit of distance from the pitch,” adds midfielder James. “But it’s a really good stadium to play at. It is great for the football club to travel to these (stadiums) but we need to put in a performance and come away with the three points.”

The London Stadium’s big advantage — and why the West Ham board leapt at the chance to move from Upton Park in 2016 — is its capacity.

Even in last season’s Premier League, only Manchester United’s Old Trafford hosted a bigger average attendance (73,975) than the 62,347 posted by West Ham.

Today, the Londoners’ home boasts the biggest capacity seen at this level since the 1970s, when Sunderland’s visit to Old Trafford drew a crowd of 60,585 in United’s one season down from the top flight (1974-75).

The Valley was capable of holding even more (66,000) after Charlton’s promotion from the Third Division in 1975, but this capacity was then slashed by more than two-thirds to just 20,000 following the introduction of the Safety of Sports Ground Act two years later.

Friday, therefore, will be a notable milestone in Wrexham’s history as that long-standing previous record attendance set at Liverpool in 1970 is finally broken.

Four months before that trip to Anfield, 48,315 watched a 2-0 third-round defeat at Old Trafford in the League Cup, while the 2022 FA Trophy final loss to Bromley at Wembley completes the top three of crowds for competitive fixtures, according to the comprehensive Wrexham Archive database.

The largest crowd for a European tie came against Roma in October 1984, as 36,793 turned up at the Olympic Stadium to see if Wrexham could repeat their remarkable giantkilling act of beating Porto over two legs in the previous round. As for friendlies, the 50,596 crowd that watched the 2023 pre-season defeat against Chelsea in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, remains a record.

Making history is one thing. But points remain the priority for a team that has drawn four of six league games so far. Having been held to a 1-1 draw by Burnley in midweek, midfielder James is adamant the only record that matters come Friday is extending Wrexham’s unbeaten run.

“Another relegated team and another very, very difficult game but, hopefully, we can put the performance in,” says the 35-year-old. “No pressure on us at West Ham. That’s what we have to remember.

“It’s been a tough run of games, in terms of how quickly they come. But we take that in our stride and hopefully continue the away form. Let’s hope we can get the result.”
