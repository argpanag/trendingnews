SpaceX sued by Oregon and Virginia over AI deepfake porn, Grok safety claims

TheTools · 23.11.2026 12:40 · source · permalink

In this article

Lawsuits against Elon Musk's SpaceX are stacking up, with Virginia and Oregon becoming the first states to sue the company over issues with its Grok AI image generator.

Versions of Grok and SpaceX's social network X have enabled users to generate and distribute explicit deepfakes based on real photos or videos of children and other people who did not consent, according to research by the Internet Watch Foundation, the Center for Countering Digital Hate and others.

In separate complaints submitted to state courts as of Monday morning, Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield and Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones argued that SpaceX failed to put safeguards in place to stop Grok and X users from making and sharing harmful deepfake images, especially alleged AI-generated child sexual abuse material.

The complaints also allege that SpaceX has falsely represented its AI apps and social network (Grok and X) as generally safe, which the states see as a violation of Virginia's Consumer Protection Act or Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act. The matter is particularly concerning because X allows minors 13 and older to access its content.

Rayfield and Jones both noted in their complaints that harms caused by non-consensual, sexualized images go beyond the victims depicted in the images.

"This content can cause fear, distress, and anxiety in those who are unwillingly exposed to the content," Jones wrote. Rayfield wrote that such conduct "erodes important norms regarding consent and privacy and trivializes sexual imagery, abuse, and exploitation."

In a statement emailed to CNBC by a spokesperson for SpaceXSI, and posted on the company's @Safety account on X, the company said, "When someone tries to break our safeguards and generate this content, we take appropriate measures, including actioning accounts and notifying law enforcement. Attempting to create this material is a serious crime, and the individuals who do it should be prosecuted to the fullest extent of the law. We will continue to work with the authorities via all available means to pursue these bad actors."

At an NYC Council hearing in October, a SpaceX AI policy leader, Gabriela Castillo Madrid, said the company had appropriate "safeguards" and "strict controls" in place around its AI products.

SpaceX was already facing multiple Grok- and X-related lawsuits, and government probes around the world, including by authorities in the U.K., Spain, Ireland, France and the state of California.

In private-sector lawsuits filed against SpaceX in the U.S. concerning Grok and AI-generated, explicit images, litigation is still in early stages and is tracked by The Center for Internet Technology Addiction.

When the city of Baltimore sued xAI, now owned and operated by SpaceX, the company argued that under Section 230, they were not liable for unwanted images. Section 230 is a federal law that largely shields online platforms from liability for users' content.

SpaceX also sued a user in a federal court in the Northern District of Texas for breach of contract after he allegedly used Grok to create and distribute deepfake explicit images, including depictions of minors. Lawyers for Musk's company argued that Grok should be seen as "a neutral tool, subject to user control," and that any image generation "is the result of the user's prompts and directions."

The new state suits add to legal pressure on SpaceX. Both Virginia and Oregon are seeking injunctions, civil penalties, disgorgement and legal costs, while Virginia is also seeking restitution for consumers from Musk's company.

Oregon submitted its complaint to the Multnomah County Circuit Court on Sunday evening, and Virginia submitted its complaint to the City of Richmond Circuit Court Monday morning. As of early Monday, the suits were pending the courts' acceptance.

State attorneys general have recently succeeded in compelling U.S. social media companies to change their products and pay for alleged past harms.

In August, for example, Meta settled a major federal case centered on allegations that the social media giant misrepresented the level of child mental health problems caused by its apps, Facebook and Instagram. Without admitting to wrongdoing, Meta agreed to pay states around $17 billion over a 10-year period and to make app changes that experts believe will help keep children safer online.
