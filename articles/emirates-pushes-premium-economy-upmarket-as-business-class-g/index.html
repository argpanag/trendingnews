Emirates Pushes Premium Economy Upmarket as Business Class Gets Sliced Up

Photo Credit: The new premium economy seat features a U-Dream headrest. Emirates

Skift Take

Airlines can no longer rely on passenger growth alone, so they're fighting harder for travelers who will pay more.

For years, the aircraft arms race was fought at the very front of the plane, with airlines pouring millions into first-class suites and ever-flatter business-class beds. Now the battle is moving a few rows back.

On Thursday, Emirates launched a premium economy seat with a full-height, electrically operated privacy divider, which passengers can raise and lock from a personal control pad.

Fitted to its new-generation Airbus A350 jets, the seat features electric recline with preset lounge and meal modes, a leather U-shaped headrest, deployable footrest, wireless charging and a 4K screen. 

It arrives as the airline industry enters a period of slower expansion, with global passenger revenue expected to approach $900 billion by 2027 but annual growth settling into low single digits.

Key Points

  • Airlines are pivoting from chasing passenger volume to extracting yield, with premium economy as a lucrative sweet spot that earns 1.5–3x economy revenue for only modestly more cabin space and service cost.
  • Emirates' new A350 premium economy seat — with an electric full-height privacy divider, preset recline modes, wireless charging and a 4K screen — sets a higher product benchmark aimed at justifying a premium fare, while Delta shows the payoff, with premium now at 50% of passenger revenue.
  • The premium push carries risk: as more carriers (including former holdouts like Turkish Airlines) expand premium cabins and slice business class into à la carte tiers, they may eventually compete for a limited pool of high-yield travelers, pressuring yields and raising the stakes on differentiation.

Summary

As the post-pandemic boom fades and industry growth settles into low single digits, airlines are shifting their competitive focus from the front of the cabin to the middle, treating premium economy as a key engine of profitable revenue growth. Emirates has raised the bar with a new premium economy seat on its Airbus A350 fleet featuring a full-height electric privacy divider, electric recline with lounge and meal modes, wireless charging and a 4K screen — a deliberate effort to make the step up from economy feel worth a higher fare. The economics are compelling: premium fares can generate 1.5 to 3 times standard economy revenue for only modestly more space, and premium cabins now represent roughly 24% of global passenger revenue ($188 billion). Carriers like Delta (where premium is now half of passenger revenue) and Virgin Atlantic are reconfiguring toward yield over volume, while holdouts such as Turkish Airlines are relenting, leaving only Qatar Airways and Etihad among major long-haul resisters. At the same time, business class is being sliced into à la carte tiers, sharpening the competitive comparison. The strategy carries risk: if too many carriers expand premium simultaneously, they will compete for a finite pool of willing high-yield travelers, making genuine product differentiation essential.
