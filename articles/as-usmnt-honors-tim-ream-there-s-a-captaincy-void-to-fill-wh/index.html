As USMNT honors Tim Ream, there’s a captaincy void to fill. Who will inherit the armband?

TheTools · 30.09.2026 00:10 · source · permalink

Tyler Adams wore the captain's armband vs. Peru; will it become his permanently? Rich Storry / Getty Images

ST. LOUIS — As Mauricio Pochettino stepped to the podium on Monday afternoon at Energizer Park, he couldn’t hide his smile.

There, sitting on the table in front of him, was a Caesar salad. A card in front of it read: “From Tim Ream.”

It was a nod to the first time the U.S. played under Pochettino in St. Louis, his fourth match in charge in November 2024. Ream picked out a restaurant. A large Caesar salad was served at the table.

“I think in St. Louis we ate the best Caesar salad,” Pochettino said, as the media chuckled in front of him.

The salad was a reminder of one of the biggest changes from Pochettino’s first cycle with the U.S. to his current one. Ream, who has served as captain in 23 of 32 games the U.S. has played under the Argentine manager, including four of the five World Cup games this summer (the U.S. heavily rotated against Turkey, and Weston McKennie wore the armband), will be honored on Tuesday ahead of the friendly against Chile.

The St. Louis native spent more than 14 years with the U.S., earning 86 caps and wearing the armband 32 times, sixth-most in program history. Ream, 38, started and played every minute of the 2022 World Cup, then became the oldest field player in USMNT World Cup history four years later. Tuesday night, speaking before first kick, he formally acknowledged what was long presumed by the arrangement of Tuesday’s ceremony: he has retired from international soccer.

Pochettino praised Ream as a “wonderful human being” who “performed, too.”

“He was great for us,” Pochettino said. “Always (he) is going to be an amazing part of my career because to meet a person like him, and human being, was massive for me to adapt to here, in that culture in the USA.

“I think he was humble enough. He was always defending his team, the teammates, but always with empathy for us (the coaching staff) and helping in every single decision.”

Ream joked he was like the grandfather of the team. Imagine what it might have been like for him in this camp filled with teenagers like Cavan Sullivan, Neil Pierre and Mathis Albert. That wave of youth is the reason why finding voices to replace Ream will be so critical to this next phase of the national team. At key moments of transition, a team needs veteran voices to guide the locker room and harmonize with the coaches. It’s what Ream did so well with Pochettino, and it shouldn’t be much of an issue for this U.S. team.

Tyler Adams served as the 2022 World Cup captain and has remained one of the program’s most important voices, both on and off the field. Adams’ ability to navigate the challenging diplomatic realities of being a U.S. captain was one of the enduring memories of the Qatar World Cup. But if you ask players around the team over the last four years, Adams was still a player others leaned on — even as he missed time due to injury.

Adams captained the U.S. against Peru on Saturday, the first time he wore the armband under Pochettino. He, along with center back Chris Richards, are obvious picks to be the voices of the program going forward.

Pochettino seemed to indicate he would waste little time anointing his next captain with the 2030 cycle already underway.

“Tyler is doing great. I think after two years, he was showing with (Ream), too, that he’s a great leader,” Pochettino said. “He translates everything — the culture from the country to the new players, to the young players. He translates to the fans what it means to represent that country. He also translates to the coaches. He translates (it) to everyone.

“He’s a perfect ambassador for us because I think he’s great in front to you (the media), translating to the fans and to everyone what it means to defend that badge. And of course, after tomorrow, maybe we are thinking to try to name the new captain. And of course, Tyler is one of the candidates.”

Adams spoke to The Athletic recently about the painful experience of bowing out at the last World Cup — “I looked into the stands and I saw people who were emotional about getting knocked out because they really believed. It hurts. It stings.” — but also of how he sees his role in guiding the next generation of USMNT players into the program.

The answer revealed plenty about why Pochettino is confident in the veterans he has to guide the team forward. Adams, who went through a much more difficult transition into the national team after the USMNT’s failure to qualify for the 2018 World Cup, knows that there cannot be any room for coddling if the U.S. is going to take steps forward.

Adams, like many of these young players, was seen as the next great hope and future of the program. He, along with Christian Pulisic, Weston McKennie and Gio Reyna, were seen as the “golden generation” of the national team. After this summer’s earlier-than-hoped exit, it is abundantly clear that there are no such things as “saviors” to the national team.

The key will be trusting players like Adams to guide younger players into a team that demands and expects more — and understands how much work there still is to do.

“I don’t baby anyone. Trust me,” Adams told The Athletic. “There’s no babying anyone.

“I don’t care if you’re coming from Dortmund. I don’t care if you’re coming from Europe. I don’t care where you are. When you’re in the national team, you’re a U.S. national team player. How do we get the best out of you? If I have to go into a 50-50 (tackle) with you, I’m going as hard as I can. It doesn’t matter if you’re 16, 25, 35 (years old). That’s my mentality. That’s how I was raised.

“These kids, they just need to understand that it’s about doing the work every single day. If you get knocked on your ass, if you make a bad pass, if you do anything, who cares? Next play. That’s how you get better. Now that your foot is in the door, this is your opportunity. We don’t want to set crazy expectations for anyone, but you have an opportunity, so take it.”
